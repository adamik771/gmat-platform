### B. GMAT advice posts (10)

**Advice 1 — The error log**
The single highest-leverage GMAT habit is keeping an error log.

Not a list of questions you got wrong. A log of why you got them wrong.

I tag every miss with one of six reasons: Conceptual, Careless, Time Pressure, Misread, Strategy, or Other. After two weeks of this, a pattern appears, and the pattern is your study plan.

Most people review what. Almost nobody reviews why. The why is where the points are.

If you want the template I used, it is free. Link in the comments.

**Advice 2 — Careless is not careless**
"I just made a careless mistake."

You said that about the last five questions too. At some point, careless is not careless. It is a pattern wearing a disguise.

When I started tagging my misses, my "careless" errors clustered around the same situations: late in a section, after a hard question, on problems I assumed were easy.

Once you can name the pattern, you can train it out. Start by writing down every careless miss for one week and reading them back. You will be uncomfortable. That is the point.

**Advice 3 — Your baseline should be official**
Before you build a study plan, get an honest baseline. Use an official mba.com practice exam, not a random third-party test.

Why official: the scoring and difficulty calibration actually match the real thing. A 600 on a soft third-party test tells you nothing useful.

Take it under real conditions. Real timing, no pausing, no looking things up. The number will sting. Good. Now you have something real to build a plan against.

**Advice 4 — Mocks are practice, not exams**
Most people sit full-length mocks to find out their score. That is the least useful thing a mock can do for you.

A mock is a stress test for your pacing, your stamina, and your decision-making under fatigue. The score is a byproduct.

After every mock I review three things: where I lost time, where I rushed, and which decisions to skip or guess I got wrong. That review is worth more than the mock itself.

Sit fewer mocks. Review them harder.

**Advice 5 — Data Insights is winnable**
Data Insights scares people because it is newer and feels unfamiliar. That fear is your opportunity.

DI rewards a small set of repeatable skills: reading tables fast, translating multi-source prompts, and not over-calculating. It is more learnable than people assume because fewer people drill it properly.

I finished with DI85. Not because I am a natural with graphs, but because I treated DI as a system to be learned, not a mystery to be feared.

If DI is your weak section, that is good news. It moves faster than Verbal.

**Advice 6 — Pacing is a skill, not a personality trait**
"I'm just slow" is not a fixed fact. Pacing is trainable.

The trick is to know your average time per question type and to practice walking away. The hardest skill on the GMAT is abandoning a question you could solve, because the clock is worth more than your ego.

Train it deliberately: set per-question time limits in practice and force yourself to guess and move when you hit them. It feels awful. It also adds points.

**Advice 7 — The 80/20 of Verbal for non-natives**
If English is your second language, do not try to fix everything at once.

Most of the Verbal gains for non-native speakers come from two things: understanding the logical structure of arguments, and spotting the specific traps the test reuses. Vocabulary is rarely the real bottleneck.

I scored V86 as a non-native speaker by drilling argument logic and tracking my recurring traps, not by reading more.

Pick the structure first. The fluency follows.

**Advice 8 — Consistency beats intensity**
Two hours a day, six days a week, beats a ten-hour weekend cram every single time.

The GMAT rewards spaced, repeated exposure. Your brain consolidates between sessions, not during marathon ones.

When I prepped, I protected a daily block like an appointment I could not cancel. Some days it was ninety minutes. The point was that it happened every day.

If your plan depends on motivation, it will fail. Build a schedule that runs without it.

**Advice 9 — Redo the ones you got right but guessed**
Here is a habit almost nobody has: review the questions you got right by luck.

If you guessed and got it, that is a future wrong answer hiding as a current right one. Tag it. Redo it. Understand it properly.

Your real score is the questions you can solve reliably, not the ones you happened to land.

This one habit quietly closed a lot of my gap between 565 and 735.

**Advice 10 — Spaced review or it leaks**
You learn a concept, feel great, and forget it in ten days. That is not failure. That is how memory works.

The fix is spaced retrieval: bringing a topic back right before you would forget it, again and again, until it sticks.

I built this into my prep with a review queue that resurfaces my weak topics on a schedule. Without it, prep is a leaky bucket.

If you take one structural idea from this post, make it this: schedule your reviews, do not just hope they happen.

### C. Common prep mistake posts (10, tighter)

**Mistake 1 — Mistaking volume for progress**
Doing 60 questions a day feels productive. It usually is not.

If you do not review why you missed the ones you missed, you are just practicing your mistakes faster. Twenty questions reviewed deeply beats sixty questions skimmed.

Slow down. Review harder.

**Mistake 2 — No real baseline**
Starting a study plan without an honest baseline is like planning a road trip without knowing your starting city.

Take an official mba.com practice exam under real conditions first. The number will sting. Build from the real one, not the flattering one.

**Mistake 3 — Skipping the error log**
The most common high-scorer habit that low-scorers skip: a real error log.

Not a list of wrong questions. A log of why each one was wrong. The why is the whole game. Start one today.

**Mistake 4 — Treating mocks as scoreboards**
A mock is not there to tell you your score. It is there to test your pacing and stamina.

If you sit a mock and only look at the number, you wasted it. Review where you lost time and where you rushed.

**Mistake 5 — Avoiding your worst section**
We all drift toward the topics we are already good at. It feels nice. It does nothing.

Your score is dragged down by your weakest area, so that is where the points are. Spend your best energy on the section you dread.

**Mistake 6 — Over-calculating Data Insights**
A lot of DI questions do not need full calculation. They need estimation and reading the prompt carefully.

If you are grinding arithmetic on DI, you are losing the time the section is actually testing. Estimate first, compute only when forced.

**Mistake 7 — Cramming on weekends**
A ten-hour Saturday does less than ninety focused minutes a day across the week.

The GMAT rewards spaced repetition. Your brain needs the gaps between sessions to consolidate. Trade the cram for the routine.

**Mistake 8 — Blaming "careless"**
If five of your last ten misses were "careless," it is not carelessness. It is a pattern.

Tag every careless error for a week and read them back. You will find a trigger, and triggers can be trained out.

**Mistake 9 — Ignoring lucky right answers**
You guessed and got it right. Great, and dangerous.

A lucky correct answer is a wrong answer in disguise. Mark it, redo it, and learn it properly before the real test does it for you.

**Mistake 10 — Letting reviews depend on memory**
"I'll come back to that topic later" almost always means never.

Without a scheduled review system, weak topics quietly leak away. Put your reviews on a spaced schedule instead of trusting yourself to remember.

**Hashtags to rotate (use three to five, mix and match):**
#GMAT #GMATprep #MBA #MBAadmissions #GMATFocus #MiM #BusinessSchool #GradSchool

Rotate them rather than reusing the same block every time, and match them to the post topic.

**Posting reminders:**
- Keep links in the first comment, not the post body. LinkedIn tends to suppress reach on posts with outbound links in the main text.
- Vary your hooks. Do not open ten posts in a row with the same line or the same "565 to 735" angle. Rotate angles: the 565 start, the non-native-speaker journey, the error-log method, consistency, mocks, pacing, Data Insights, and the build-in-public thread.
- Personalize every time. These are drafts, not autopilot. Edit for the specific person, audience, and moment before posting.
- One idea per post. Skimmable beats comprehensive.
- Never paste an unfilled testimonial placeholder, an emoji, or any guaranteed-score language into a live post.
