## Part A — 20 LinkedIn posts (post only if you feel like it)

Each is self-contained. Varied angles so you are never repeating yourself. Lower-effort than the `../LAUNCH_ASSETS.md` founder posts.

### A1 — The 565 start
I scored 565 on my first GMAT Focus.

I almost convinced myself that number was just who I was at this test.

It was not. Eight months later I sat at 735. Nothing about my intelligence changed in those eight months. My review process did.

If your first score scared you, that is normal, and it is not a verdict. Happy to talk through what I changed — just ask below.

### A2 — The non-native-speaker edge
English is not my first language, and I scored V86 on the GMAT Focus.

For a long time I treated that as my ceiling. "Natives will always read faster." Then I noticed the Verbal section does not reward vocabulary — it rewards spotting the same argument structures and the same traps, over and over.

That is learnable in any language. If you are a non-native speaker staring at the Verbal section, you are not as far behind as it feels.

### A3 — The error-log method (short version)
The habit that moved my GMAT score the most was not doing more questions.

It was writing down *why* I missed each one. Conceptual gap? Careless? Time pressure? Misread? Wrong strategy?

After a few weeks the pattern was obvious: a handful of error types caused most of my wrong answers. I stopped studying everything and started fixing those. That is when my mocks moved.

### A4 — Consistency over heroics
Two hours a day for sixty days beats a fourteen-hour weekend before the test. Every time.

The GMAT rewards retention, and retention is built by spacing, not cramming. The most boring habit — show up daily, review yesterday's misses — is also the highest-leverage one.

Nothing about my prep was impressive on any single day. The compounding was.

### A5 — Mock-exam strategy
A mock you do not review is just an expensive way to feel anxious.

The score is the least useful part. The useful part is the next morning: every question you missed, tagged by why, turned into this week's study list.

I learned more from reviewing four mocks slowly than I would have from taking twelve and glancing at the totals.

### A6 — Timing and pacing
Most people do not have a content problem on the GMAT. They have a clock problem.

You know the material in practice, untimed. Then the timer turns competence into panic, and panic turns into careless errors on questions you could solve.

Fixing pacing is its own skill. Track time per question, find where you bleed minutes, and practice walking away from the question that is eating you alive.

### A7 — Data Insights is winnable
Data Insights scares people because it is the newer section and feels unfamiliar.

That is exactly why it is the best place to gain points. Fewer people drill it seriously, the question formats are finite, and the trap is almost always over-calculating when the answer only needs an estimate.

Unfamiliar is not the same as hard. It is just under-practiced.

### A8 — Build in public
I am building a GMAT prep platform by myself, and posting about it as I go.

Every chapter, every practice question, the error log, the analytics — solo, around a final year of my BBA. Some nights that means writing Data Insights explanations at midnight.

I am sharing the build partly to keep myself honest and partly because the method behind it is the same one that took me from 565 to 735.

### A9 — Honest beta invite
I built the GMAT platform I wish I had when I was stuck in the 600s, and it is free while it is in private beta.

No credit card, full access: the chapters, the question bank, the error log, the mocks, the study plan. I want real feedback from people actually preparing, not a launch announcement.

If that is you, the link is in the comments. No pressure either way.

### A10 — What I would tell my 565 self
If I could send three lines back to myself the day I scored 565:

Stop doing random questions. Start a real error log. Trust that boring consistency beats heroic cramming.

That is most of the gap between a 565 and a 735, compressed into three sentences I did not believe at the time.

### A11 — Why I built it
This started because people kept asking how I went from 565 to 735.

I gave the same answer so many times that turning it into a structured product felt obvious: the chapters I wish existed, a question bank, and the error-log system at the center of it.

So I built it. It is in private beta now, free, and I am mostly looking for honest feedback.

### A12 — The plateau (600s)
If you are stuck somewhere in the 600s, it is almost never a content problem.

You already know more than enough to score higher. What is leaking points is review: you fix questions one at a time instead of fixing the *category* of mistake that keeps recurring.

Find your top three recurring error types. Fix those. The plateau usually breaks there.

### A13 — Review beats volume
"I did 3,000 practice questions" is not a study plan. It is a way to feel busy.

The students who improve fastest are not the ones who did the most questions. They are the ones who reviewed the deepest — who can tell you the specific reason behind each miss and what they changed.

Quality of review, not quantity of questions.

### A14 — The score is one variable
A top GMAT score does not get you into a program. It removes one reason to say no.

I treated my 735 that way: not as the thing that would get me admitted, but as one variable I could control and take off the table, so the rest of my application could speak.

That framing took a lot of pressure off the number.

### A15 — Quant without being a "math person"
I am not a natural math person, and I scored Q88.

The Quant section is not testing whether you are gifted at math. It is testing whether you recognize a finite set of recurring problem patterns and avoid the traps built into them.

That is pattern recognition, and pattern recognition is just reps plus honest review. Anyone can build it.

### A16 — The taxonomy I used
When I review a missed question, I tag it with one of six labels: conceptual, careless, time pressure, misread, strategy, or other.

Six tags sounds trivial. It is not. After a few weeks the tally tells you the truth — most people are shocked how many of their misses are careless or misread, not conceptual.

You cannot fix a problem you have not named.

### A17 — Studying around a full life
I prepared for the GMAT during my final year of a business degree, as a non-native speaker, with no spare time.

I did not out-hour anyone. I out-structured them: short daily sessions, ruthless review, and a plan built around my actual weak areas instead of a generic syllabus.

If you are fitting prep around a job or a degree, structure is your edge, not raw hours.

### A18 — One question, broken down
Here is a habit that quietly closed a lot of my gap from 565 to 735.

When I missed a hard question, I did not just read the solution. I re-solved it from scratch the next day, cold, no notes. If I could not, it went into a review queue and came back a few days later.

A question is not "learned" the day you understand the explanation. It is learned when you can reproduce it later, alone.

### A19 — Calibration (knowing what you know)
Most test-takers are badly calibrated: confident on questions they get wrong, anxious on ones they get right.

Closing that gap is worth real points. When you review, note not just whether you got it right but how sure you were. The questions you were confident-but-wrong on are your most dangerous blind spots.

Accuracy matters. So does knowing where your confidence is lying to you.

### A20 — The honest hook
There is no shortcut I can sell you, and I would not trust anyone who claims one.

What I can offer is the method I actually used, turned into a platform that is free during its private beta: chapters, a real question bank, the error log, mocks, and a study plan that adapts to the baseline you set with an official practice exam.

If that is useful to you, great. If not, the free study-schedule generator and sample chapters are on the site regardless.
