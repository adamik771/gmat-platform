## @what-the-test-is

The GMAT Focus Edition is the only GMAT that exists now — the legacy test was retired in early 2024 — and it is shorter and sharper than its reputation suggests. **Three sections, 45 minutes each.** That is **64 questions in 2 hours 15 minutes**, plus **one optional 10-minute break** between sections. No essay. Nothing else.

| Section | Questions | Minutes | What lives there |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quant | 21 | 45 | Problem Solving only — algebra, arithmetic, number properties, statistics, word problems. No geometry. |
| Verbal | 23 | 45 | Critical Reasoning and Reading Comprehension only. Sentence Correction is gone. |
| Data Insights | 20 | 45 | Five types: Data Sufficiency, Table Analysis, Graphics Interpretation, Two-Part Analysis, Multi-Source Reasoning. |

Three details matter more than they look.

**You choose the section order** before the timer starts. Most students put their most demanding section first, while fresh — but the right order is the one that produces your best combined score in practice, not the one a guide tells you.

**The on-screen calculator exists only in Data Insights.** Quant is calculator-free: its arithmetic is designed to be done by hand or avoided by reasoning. And the DI calculator is a clue, not a gift — that section is not testing arithmetic.

**Old-test instincts mislead.** If your GMAT stories date from before 2024, recalibrate: the old exam ran about 3 hours 7 minutes, had an essay, tested grammar in Verbal and geometry in Quant. All gone. Data Sufficiency moved out of Quant and into Data Insights.

**Mental model.** This is a short exam with zero filler: roughly two minutes per question, and every question counts toward a section that counts. There is nowhere to hide a weakness.

> **Recall check.** Without looking up: how many questions in each section, and where is the calculator allowed? (Quant 21, Verbal 23, DI 20 — calculator in Data Insights only. Retrieval beats re-reading; that habit runs through this whole course.)

## @how-scoring-works

The Total Score runs from **205 to 805** in 10-point steps, and every valid total ends in 5 — `705` exists, `700` does not. The scale was deliberately offset from the old 200-800 so the two are not comparable; converting an old-test score takes the official concordance table, not arithmetic.

Underneath the total, each section is scored on an identical **60-90** scale, and — this is the single most underrated fact about the exam — **all three sections weigh equally**. A Data Insights point is worth exactly a Quant point. Most students arrive with years of practice behind Quant and Verbal, give DI a fraction of their attention, and then wonder why their total is capped. An underprepared DI section drags your score exactly as hard as an underprepared Quant section would. Treat DI as the full third of the exam it is, from day one.

The exam is **question-adaptive**: it selects later questions based on how you are doing. Two consequences: hitting hard questions mid-section is a sign things are going well — do not let difficulty rattle you; and do not try to estimate your score mid-exam — you will be wrong, and the attempt costs focus.

Finally, the review feature: within a section you can **bookmark questions, return to them, and change up to 3 answers** before time expires. The old test was strictly forward-only, so this is a real strategic tool. Save your flags for genuine 50/50 decisions, not for questions you had no idea on.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why would equal section weighting change how you allocate study time, even if your Quant is weaker than your DI? Say the mechanism out loud: points come from wherever they are cheapest to gain, and an untouched section usually has the cheapest points on the table.

## @what-the-exam-rewards

Here is the point most prep skips: the GMAT is not a knowledge exam. The math tops out around solid high-school level, and Verbal asks you to reason about short texts, not to know trivia. What the exam measures is **decision-making under time pressure** — pick the right approach, commit, execute cleanly, move on, roughly every two minutes, for 45 minutes straight, three times.

That changes what "studying" means. A topic list tells you what to learn; it does not tell you why you miss. Two students can know the same algebra and score far apart, because one of them burns four minutes proving something estimation settles in twenty seconds, second-guesses a correct answer into a wrong one, or misreads "least" as "greatest" under the clock. The exam punishes execution failures exactly as hard as knowledge gaps — and most score plateaus are execution, not knowledge.

This is why **"I knew it but picked wrong" is the score killer.** It feels harmless in review — you shrug, call it careless, and move to the next topic. It is not harmless. It is a repeatable pattern with a cause: a misread qualifier, a skipped final check, a trap answer engineered for people who stop reasoning one step early. Patterns repeat on test day unless you find and fix them, which is why this course will push you to track **error patterns, not topic lists**.

The most expensive lesson I learned personally: I started at `565`, and for months I studied the way most people do — more topics, more questions, more hours. The needle barely moved. What got me to `735` was changing the unit of work from "topics covered" to "mistakes explained": every miss got a named cause, and the causes got fixed one by one. The knowledge was in place long before the score showed it. The gap was decisions.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an exam built for business schools reward fast decisions over deep recall? Articulate it in one sentence — if the design makes sense to you, you will train for the right thing.

## @how-the-platform-works

Everything in this platform serves one loop, and you are standing at the start of it.

**The guided path.** The curriculum is one ordered path of 50+ chapters — this is the first. The order is deliberate: number foundations first, then the strategy toolkit (backsolving, plugging in numbers, estimation — the moves that pay on every section), then Quant, Verbal, and Data Insights built **in rotation** so no section goes stale while you work another, advanced material later, and the timing chapters last. Do not reorder it; the path interleaves on purpose.

**Inside each chapter: read, recall, practice.** Chapters are short readings with recall checks and self-explanation prompts built in — you have already met both above. Answer them from memory before reading on; retrieval is what makes material stick, and passive re-reading teaches almost nothing. After the reading, each chapter has its own **practice tests** that grade the material while it is fresh.

**The error log.** Every miss gets tagged with *why* it happened — concept gap, setup error, calc slip, time sink, overconfidence, and so on — plus a note in your own words. Within weeks the log shows what no topic list can: your three or four personal failure patterns. This is the single highest-leverage habit in the system. An untagged mistake is a mistake you have agreed to repeat.

**The review queue.** Missed questions resurface on a spaced schedule. Clearing the queue is a short daily habit that converts last week's misses into long-term memory.

**The score signal.** Practice numbers inside any platform — including this one — tell you direction, not destination. The only score signal worth trusting is the **six official practice exams from mba.com**, built by the people who write the real test. You enter each result on the **Exams page**, and that trend line is your measuring stick. Everything else is training.

> **Recall check.** What is the loop, in order? (Read the chapter, answer the recall checks from memory, take the chapter's practice tests, tag every miss in the error log, clear the review queue, and measure progress only with official exams.)

## @realistic-prep-arc

Here is what an honest arc looks like. No overnight-score promises — anyone selling those is selling.

**Week one: baseline.** Take **Official Exam 1 on mba.com early, before you feel ready.** Resist the urge to "study a bit first": a prepared baseline measures your cramming, not your starting point, and every plan built on it inherits the distortion. Take it cold, in one sitting, under real conditions, and enter the result on the Exams page. The number may sting; that is what baselines are for.

**The long middle: the loop, on repeat.** Weeks of chapters in path order, practice tests after each, every miss tagged, review queue cleared most days. This phase is unglamorous; it is also where the score is built. Consistency beats intensity: regular sessions most days outperform heroic weekends, because retention — not exposure — is what survives to test day.

**Officials every week or two.** Each official exam is both a stamina rep and a calibration point. Enter every score; watch the trend, not any single result. One bad official means nothing. A flat trend over three of them means something — usually that your error log is being written but not read.

**Timing last.** The path puts timing chapters at the end deliberately. Pacing drills before your accuracy and methods are stable just teach you to do the wrong things faster. Build correct decisions first; compress them at the end.

On hours: I will not invent a universal number — it depends on your gap and your consistency. But meaningful score moves are measured in **weeks and months of regular work**, not days; my own `565` to `735` took months of exactly this loop. Plan your exam date accordingly.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does a cold baseline produce a better plan than a prepared one? One sentence: the plan targets your real weaknesses, and a flattering baseline hides exactly the weaknesses the plan most needs to see.

## @summary

You now know the machine: three 45-minute sections, **64 questions**, your choice of order, calculator in DI only; a **205-805** total built from three equally weighted **60-90** sections; an adaptive engine that rewards calm decisions and punishes "knew it but picked wrong"; and a platform loop — read, recall, practice, tag, review, measure — built to fix exactly that.

Here is your first week, concretely:

1. **Take Official Exam 1 on mba.com.** Cold, one sitting, real conditions, the one optional 10-minute break and nothing more. Do not study for it first.
2. **Enter the score on the Exams page.** That trend line is the only score signal you will trust from now on.
3. **Set your target score and your exam date in the app.** The platform calibrates around the target, and a real date turns "someday" prep into a schedule.
4. **Start the path.** Move to the next chapter and take them in order. Do not skip ahead to your favorite section.
5. **Install the two habits now**, while the stakes are low: tag every single miss in the error log with a cause, and clear the review queue daily. Students who do this from week one are the ones whose trend slopes upward.

One last thing, from someone who sat where you sit at `565`: your target is almost certainly reachable — through the boring loop, not a secret technique. The loop starts on the next page.
