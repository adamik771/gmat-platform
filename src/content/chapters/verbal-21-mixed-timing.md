## @pacing-triage

You get 45 minutes for 23 questions. That is roughly **117 seconds per question** — call it **two minutes flat** as your working average. But you do not spend that budget evenly, and treating every question as a fresh two-minute investment is exactly how strong students arrive at the last passage with four questions unanswered. Your job is to manage a *pool* of time, not a per-question allowance.

**Mental model.** Treat the 45 minutes as a shared bank account, not 23 separate envelopes. An easy question you finish in 70 seconds deposits time back; one brutal Critical Reasoning question can withdraw three minutes and bankrupt you. The discipline is simple: **never let a single question overdraw the account.** The test does not pay extra for the hard ones. A correct answer is worth the same point whether it took you 40 seconds or four minutes — and four minutes means you stole time from two other questions you would have nailed.

You control nothing about order. Questions arrive one at a time, CR and RC interleaved, and you cannot skip ahead to cherry-pick the easy ones. What you *can* do is **bookmark and review**: flag a question, lock in your best current answer, and move on. Use it on every question that stalls.

### Reading Comprehension changes the math

RC does not arrive as single questions — it arrives as **passages that bundle a set**, typically 3–4 questions hanging off one chunk of text. That bundling is your friend if you read correctly. You pay the reading cost **once**, then answer the whole set while the structure is still fresh.

- **Read the passage once, for structure, not for memorization.** Track the author's purpose, the shift words (*however*, *therefore*, *in contrast*), and where each idea lives. Do not try to absorb every detail — you will go back for those.
- Then **answer the set in a burst** before the map fades. Detail questions send you back to a specific line — that is normal and fast. Do not re-read the whole passage per question.
- Budget the *bundle*, not the individual RC question. A 4-question passage might fairly eat **6–8 minutes total** — that is on-pace, even though the reading time front-loads it.

The decoder below is what "on-pace" actually looks like once you stop counting per question and start counting per unit of work:

| Where your time goes | On-pace target |
|---|---|
| Single CR question | ~2:00, hard stop near 2:30 |
| RC passage (first read) | 2:00–3:30 to map it |
| Each RC question after the read | ~1:00 — they are cheap |
| Whole RC bundle (read + 3–4 Qs) | ~6:00–8:00 |

**Worked example.** You are 18 minutes in, and the on-pace marker says you should be near question 9. You are on question 7 — about 4 minutes behind. The current question is a CR *strengthen* about a coffee chain's loyalty app: two answers look defensible and you have already spent 2:20 staring at them. Decision: **stop.** Eliminate the two that clearly don't bear on the causal claim, pick the better of the remaining pair on the logic you can actually articulate, **bookmark it**, and move. You just spent 2:20 — fine — but a third minute would have put you 5 minutes behind with the section's hardest stretch still ahead. The bookmark means that if time remains at the end, you return with fresh eyes; if it doesn't, you still have a scored answer instead of a blank.

**Trap to watch.** The **sunk-cost freeze.** You have invested 90 seconds, narrowed it to two, and you keep re-reading the same two choices hoping the answer reveals itself. It will not. Reading the same lines a fourth time generates no new information — it only spends time you can't get back. The instant you are down to two and stalled, you are *done deliberating*: choose, flag, advance. Freezing is the single most expensive habit on this section.

### The triage habit

Build one reflex and run it every time the clock crosses your budget: **answer-or-bookmark, never freeze.**

- If you have a confident answer, select it and go.
- If you are stuck at the budget, **make your best-supported guess, bookmark, and move.** Always leave a real answer recorded — never an empty one.
- Every few questions, glance at the on-pace marker. **Behind?** Tighten — take fewer questions to the 2:30 ceiling. **Ahead?** You bought yourself permission to spend on the genuinely hard one in front of you.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: how many questions, how many minutes, and what is the per-question average? (*23 questions, 45 minutes, ~117 seconds each.*) Pull these from memory before every practice set — retrieving the numbers cold burns them in far deeper than re-reading the table does, and on test day there is no table to re-read.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does bookmarking-and-moving beat grinding out a hard question to certainty? Say the mechanism in one sentence. (Because every question is worth the same point, so time spent past your budget on one question is time *stolen* from easier questions where that same minute buys a higher probability of a correct answer.)

The goal is not to answer every question perfectly. It is to give every question a fair, bounded shot — and to make sure the clock never decides your last few answers for you.

## @elimination

Your job on a hard CR or RC question is not to find the right answer. It is to delete the four wrong ones. Those are different skills, and the second is faster, more reliable, and far easier to do under time pressure. The "best" answer is whatever survives after you have aggressively cut everything that breaks a rule. Stop hunting; start prosecuting.

**Mental model.** Treat the five choices as five suspects, and you are looking for reasons to release them, not reasons to convict. Most wrong answers are wrong for a *structural* reason that has nothing to do with how true they sound — they go out of scope, overreach with extreme language, reverse the logic, state something true but irrelevant, or quietly distort what the passage said. You do not need to understand why the survivor is brilliant. You need a named, specific charge against each of the other four. No charge, no cut.

Aggression matters because the GMAT writes wrong answers to *feel* relevant. A choice that restates a passage sentence verbatim is comforting and usually useless. A choice with a sweeping word — "all," "never," "eliminates," "proves" — feels strong and is usually too strong to defend. Learn to flinch at these on sight.

Here is the decoder. These five flavors recur across both CR and RC; memorize the tells.

| Wrong-answer flavor | What it looks like | The instant tell |
|---|---|---|
| Out of scope | Introduces a topic, group, or comparison the argument/passage never raised | "Wait, where was *cost* ever mentioned?" |
| Too extreme | Uses absolute language the evidence can't support | all, none, only, always, never, must, proves, eliminates |
| Reverses the logic | True direction, wrong sign — supports when you need to weaken, cause/effect flipped | The fact is real but it helps the *other* side |
| True but irrelevant | Accurate, often quoted from the text, but doesn't answer *this* stem | "Yes, and? It doesn't address the conclusion." |
| Distorts the passage | Close paraphrase that swaps a qualifier, scope, or degree | "could" became "will," "some" became "most" |

**Worked example.** *A regional grocery chain found that customers enrolled in its loyalty program spend 30% more per visit than non-enrolled customers. The marketing director concludes that expanding the program to all stores will raise average spending chain-wide. Which of the following most weakens the argument?*

- (A) The loyalty program costs more to administer than the company initially projected. — **Out of scope.** Cost wasn't the conclusion; spending was. Cut.
- (B) Customers who enroll in loyalty programs are typically those who already shop frequently and spend heavily. — Survives. This attacks the causal leap: the program may not *cause* higher spending; high spenders self-select into it. Hold.
- (C) The grocery chain's competitors have all launched similar loyalty programs. — **True but irrelevant** (and out of scope). Competitor behavior doesn't bear on whether *this* program raises *this* chain's spending. Cut.
- (D) Expanding the program will definitely increase enrollment at every store. — **Reverses the logic.** This strengthens the plan's reach; we were asked to weaken. Cut.
- (E) Loyalty members never make purchases without using their cards. — **Too extreme.** "Never" is indefensible and doesn't address causation anyway. Cut.

Four named charges, one survivor: (B). Notice you never had to decide that (B) was eloquent — you just couldn't fault it.

Now the moment that actually costs people time: **down to two.** When two choices remain, do not re-read both choices again and vibe. Re-read the **question stem.** The stem names the exact job, and the differentiator between your two survivors almost always maps to one word in it.

| Stem split | What it forces you to check |
|---|---|
| "weaken" vs "strengthen" | One survivor probably has the wrong sign — it helps the side you were told to attack |
| "primary purpose" vs "a detail the author mentions" | One survivor is true but too narrow to be the *purpose* |
| "the argument assumes" vs "if true, most supports" | One survivor is a restatement of the conclusion, not an unstated assumption |

Find the single attribute where the two choices differ, then ask which one the stem demands. One differentiator, one decision. If you cannot articulate the difference in a sentence, you are not down to two — you misread the stem.

**Trap to watch.** The seductive distortion. After cutting three obvious losers, students relax and pick the choice that *sounds* most like the passage — the close paraphrase that swapped "may contribute to" for "is the main cause," or "in some markets" for "across markets." It reads familiar precisely because it borrowed the passage's words. Verify the qualifiers and the degree, not just the topic.

> **Recall check.** Without looking up, name the five recurring wrong-answer flavors. (Out of scope, too extreme, reverses the logic, true but irrelevant, distorts the passage.) Pulling these from memory under exam stress is what lets you tag a choice in two seconds; re-deriving them mid-question is what blows your clock.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does re-reading the *stem* — not the two surviving choices — break a down-to-two tie faster? Articulate the mechanism: the stem fixes the single dimension the answer must satisfy, so it converts a fuzzy "which is better" comparison into a binary "which one matches the job" test.

## @temptation-control

The credited answer is rarely the most impressive one in the list. It is the one the passage *forces*. The trap answer, by contrast, is engineered — the test writers reverse-engineer the mistakes real students make and build a choice that rewards each one. Once you see that distractors are manufactured from predictable errors, you stop reading the choices as five neutral candidates and start reading them as four baited hooks and one defensible claim.

**Mental model.** Every wrong answer is a *bet the test writer placed on how you'll go wrong.* They bet you'll import outside knowledge. They bet you'll stretch the scope one notch wider than the text supports. They bet that "extreme" reads as "rigorous" and that an answer dressed in sophisticated vocabulary "sounds smart" enough to be right. The credited answer wins not by being clever but by being **fully supported and exactly the right size.** Your job is to deny each bet — and you can only deny a bet you can name.

Here are the five families of bait you will see again and again. Learn them as a decoder so that the moment a choice "feels right," you can ask *which hook is this*:

| Trap family | What it does | Why it pulls you in | The tell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outside knowledge | True in the real world, but never stated in the passage | You "know" it's correct, so you stop checking the text | You can't point to a supporting line |
| Scope creep | Slightly broader or narrower than the claim ("all," "any," "in general") | One word off, but it reads as a natural paraphrase | A quantifier the passage never used |
| Extreme language | Overstated: "proves," "eliminates," "never," "guarantees" | Strength feels like confidence and authority | A verb stronger than the evidence earns |
| Half-right / half-wrong | First clause matches; second clause distorts | You confirm on the early words and quit reading | The damage lives after the comma |
| Sounds-smart | Abstract, jargon-heavy, plausible | Difficulty masquerades as correctness | Impressive but does no logical work |

The antidote runs before you ever reach choice (A). **Pre-phrase: predict the answer in your own words from the passage or argument, then go shopping for the match.** When you walk in with a prediction, the baited choices have nothing to grab. When you walk in empty, every well-written distractor looks like a live option, because you're letting the choices define plausibility instead of the text.

The second discipline is harder because it fights your competence: **match to the text, not to your opinion.** On a strengthen/weaken question about a business plan you happen to have run yourself, your instinct is to answer from experience. The GMAT does not score your experience. It scores whether the choice does the logical work the *argument* needs. Strong test-takers lose points right here — right about the world, wrong about the passage.

**Worked example.** *Stimulus:* "Brightline Coffee added a loyalty app last quarter; same-store revenue rose 12%. The CFO concludes the app caused the increase and plans to expand it chain-wide." *Question: which most weakens the conclusion?*

Tempting trap: "Loyalty apps in the restaurant industry typically raise customer retention by under 5%." It feels authoritative — it cites an industry benchmark and sounds analytical. But it's **outside-knowledge plus scope creep**: it speaks to the industry "typically," not to Brightline's actual 12%, and it never severs the app-to-revenue link for *this* chain. It rewards you for reasoning about coffee retail in general.

Credited answer: "During the same quarter, Brightline cut prices 10% across all stores." This names an **alternative cause** acting on the same revenue in the same period — the exact logical move "weaken a causal claim" requires. It isn't impressive; it's just lethal to the conclusion. Pre-phrasing would have produced it: before reading choices, you'd predict, "something else that lifted revenue at the same time." Then you shop for that, and the benchmark answer never tempts you.

**Trap to watch.** The half-right answer is the costliest, because you stop reading the instant the first clause confirms your prediction. You see "Brightline cut prices 10%" and select — never noticing the choice continues "...but the cut applied only to stores without the app," which actually *defends* the conclusion. **Read every word of the choice you're about to pick, especially the second clause and the quantifier.** The trap lives where you stopped looking.

> **Recall check.** Name the five trap families from memory before you scroll up. (Outside knowledge, scope creep, extreme language, half-right/half-wrong, sounds-smart.) Retrieving them now installs a checklist you'll actually run under time pressure; re-reading the table just feels like learning without building the recall the test day demands.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does pre-phrasing neutralize a well-built distractor when re-reading the choices does not? (Because a prediction gives you a fixed target to match against, so a choice that's "close" or "smart-sounding" but off-target has nothing to latch onto — whereas reading cold lets each polished distractor define plausibility for you.)

Two rules, every question: **predict first, then match — and match to the passage, never to what you happen to know.** The trap answer feels right because it was built from a feeling. The credited answer is the one you can put a finger on a line and defend.

## @review-edit

The Focus interface hands you a second chance most test-takers waste. You can **bookmark** any question as you go, and at the end of a section — *if time remains* — you reach a review screen where you may revisit any flagged question and **edit up to three answers**. If the clock hits zero, the review screen never appears and your bookmarks die with it. So this is not a safety net you fall into. It is a tool you have to *fund* with banked time and *aim* with disciplined judgment.

**Mental model.** Treat the three edits like three rounds in a magazine, not an "undo" button. You do not get to re-take the section. You get three surgical corrections, and only if you arrive at the review screen with seconds still on the clock. Every bookmark you place and every minute you bank exists to put those three shots on the right targets.

The decoder for *what to bookmark* matters as much as the feature. Over-flaggers reach review with eleven bookmarks and ninety seconds, then panic-edit a question they already had right.

| What you felt on the question | Bookmark it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Down to two answers, picked one, a true 50/50 | **Yes** | This is exactly what review fixes |
| Confident, moved on | No | Re-opening confident answers is how you lose points |
| Total guess, no traction | No | Review can't manufacture knowledge you don't have |
| Right method, worried about a careless slip | **Yes** | A fresh read catches arithmetic or scope errors |
| Stuck and bleeding time | No — answer and **move** | A bookmark is not permission to stall |

The discipline that separates a +20 review from a wash: **change an answer only for a concrete, nameable reason.** "I have a bad feeling about C" is not a reason. "I now see the conclusion was about *profit*, not *revenue*, so the assumption I picked defends the wrong quantity" *is* a reason. If you cannot say in one sentence what was wrong with your original pick, leave it alone. Your first instinct under focused time pressure usually beats your jittery second-guess at minute 44.

**Worked example.** You finish Verbal with 2:10 left and four bookmarks. Question 9 was a Critical Reasoning assumption stem where you wavered between B and D. Question 14 was a Reading Comprehension detail question you flagged out of vague nerves. Question 17 was a guess across three live answers. Question 20 was a Reading Comprehension function question — why the author cites a particular sentence — that you "think" you got. Three edits, two minutes — so you triage. 17 is a pure guess; skip it, review can't help. 14 is vague nerves with no named error; skip it. That leaves 9 and 20, both with *specific* doubts. You re-read 9: the conclusion claimed the loyalty program *caused* repeat purchases, but B only said members spend more — it never closed the causal gap, while D ruled out the obvious alternative cause. **Concrete reason → edit B to D.** On 20 you re-read the cited sentence in context and confirm it functions exactly as your answer said — it illustrates the author's claim, not undercuts it. No nameable error, so you **leave it.** One edit spent, two in reserve, zero flags chased with nothing behind them.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: under what single condition does the review screen disappear entirely? (When the section timer hits zero — no time, no review.) Retrieving this now wires it in deeper than re-reading the sentence, and on test day you'll *feel* the cost of every wasted minute.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does "change only for a concrete reason" actually protect your score, rather than just sounding cautious? Articulate the mechanism: your initial answer was made with full attention on that one question, while a vague review-screen "feeling" is generated under fatigue and clock anxiety — so an edit with no named error is essentially a coin-flip away from your better, more-attentive first read.

To make this repeatable, run the same loop every section:

- **Bank the time.** Hold a target pace so you finish with at least 60–90 seconds in reserve. Review with zero banked time is theater.
- **Flag only true 50/50s and suspected slips.** Aim for three to five bookmarks across the section, not ten.
- **Triage on arrival.** Sort flagged questions into *fixable* (a concrete doubt) and *unfixable* (a blind guess). Spend edits only on the fixable ones.
- **Edit for a reason or not at all.** One-sentence justification, or hands off.
- **Stop early.** If you've burned your three edits or run out of concretely-justified changes, submit. Don't marinate.

**Trap to watch.** The fatal move is reopening a question you answered *confidently*, talking yourself into a "cleverer" reading at minute 44, and overwriting a correct answer with a wrong one. The review feature can *only* cost you points on questions you already had right. Guard those by never bookmarking them in the first place — and by treating every edit as something you must earn with a stated reason.

**Worked example.** You reach the end of the section with three minutes left and two bookmarked questions. On Q14 you narrowed to two choices and picked on a hunch; on Q19 you guessed blindly as time ran low. Spend the review time on Q14 — you have a concrete differentiator to re-check (one choice was out of scope). Leave Q19 unless you can actually re-derive it; swapping one blind guess for another is noise. You have three edits available — spend them only where a *reason* exists.

## @summary

**Mental model.** Verbal scores are won and lost not only on knowing the techniques but on *deploying* them under a ticking clock. This chapter was about the meta-skill: spending your 45 minutes where they pay.

The four habits to carry onto test day:

- **Pace by budget, not by feel.** ~2 minutes per question; an RC passage earns one careful read, then fast answers across its set. Never let one question eat three others.
- **Eliminate, don't hunt.** Cut choices on structural grounds — out of scope, too extreme, reverses the logic, true-but-irrelevant — and let the credited answer be the last one standing.
- **Pre-phrase to resist temptation.** Predict the answer before you read the choices, and match to the text, not to your opinion. The trap answer is engineered to feel right.
- **Bank time for review.** Bookmark genuine 50/50s, finish the section with minutes to spare, and edit up to three answers — but only for a concrete reason, never a vague nerve.

> **Recall check.** How many answers may you edit at the end of a Focus section, and what happens to the review screen if you run out of time? (Up to three; if time expires you lose the review screen entirely.) Retrieve it now so the rule is automatic on test day.

You've finished the Verbal track. Structure first, question second, clock always — that is the whole discipline.
