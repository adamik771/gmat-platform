## @argument-structure

Every Critical Reasoning argument has three parts. Your first job on every question — before you even read the question stem — is to identify them:

**Mental model.** Every CR argument has three parts: evidence, conclusion, and the unstated gap that bridges them. The question is *always* about the gap — strengthening it, weakening it, naming it, evaluating it. Find the gap before you read the answer choices, and the right answer announces itself; read the choices first and you're solving by feel.

**1. Conclusion.** What the author wants you to believe. The main claim. Usually introduced with signal words: "therefore," "thus," "hence," "it follows that," "the chain's management concludes," "researchers hypothesize," "critics argue."

**2. Evidence (or premises).** The facts and reasons the author offers in support of the conclusion. Usually descriptive statements: data, studies, observations, historical facts. No "therefore" attached.

**3. The gap.** The unstated leap from evidence to conclusion. This is the single most important thing to identify on every CR question — because nearly every CR question (strengthen, weaken, assumption, flaw) is about this gap. The gap is whatever *else* would have to be true for the evidence to actually prove the conclusion.

**Worked example.** "A regional grocery chain launched a loyalty program three months ago. Since then, revenue is up 8%. The chain's management concludes the loyalty program is responsible."

- **Conclusion:** The loyalty program caused the revenue increase.
- **Evidence:** Program launched 3 months ago; revenue up 8% in that period.
- **Gap:** "No other explanation accounts for the revenue increase." The argument assumes that nothing else changed in those 3 months (no ad campaign, no competitor closing, no seasonal effect, no new products).

Notice how the gap is phrased as *what the argument takes for granted*. That's the template: look at the evidence, look at the conclusion, and ask "what else would have to be true to get from here to there?"

**The three moves in order:**

1. **Find the conclusion.** Underline or mentally tag the main claim. Signal words help, but sometimes the conclusion is at the beginning, sometimes the end, sometimes the middle.
2. **Find the evidence.** Everything else in the passage that's presented as fact.
3. **Find the gap.** What assumption bridges the two? What alternative explanation could exist?

Once you've done these three moves, *then* read the question stem. Not before. The structure is the same regardless of which of the nine question types you're asked — the gap is always what you're working with.

**Conclusion vs. evidence — the "why" test.** If you can insert "because" or "the reason is" between two statements, the second is evidence for the first. "The loyalty program caused the revenue rise (conclusion) *because* revenue went up 8% after launch (evidence)." Always read evidence as answering the question "*why* does the author believe the conclusion?"

**Common conclusion signal phrases:**

| Phrase | What follows |
|---|---|
| "Therefore…" | The conclusion |
| "Thus…" | The conclusion |
| "So…" (at the start of a sentence) | The conclusion |
| "It follows that…" | The conclusion |
| "We can conclude…" | The conclusion |
| "Researchers argue…" | The conclusion |
| "The company's management believes…" | The conclusion (author may disagree) |

Note the last one: if the author *describes* what management believes, the author's own conclusion may be that management is wrong. Reading for *who* is making the claim matters.

**Common evidence signal phrases:** "Since…", "Because…", "Given that…", "After all…", "In fact…", "Studies show…", "Data indicate…"

> **Recall check.** Close your eyes. State the three parts of every CR argument. Now recall: which part is almost always the subject of the question you'll be asked? (Answer: the gap — the assumption bridging evidence to conclusion.) Forced retrieval of the skeleton makes it available on the next 74 CR questions in this chapter; re-reading the paragraph doesn't.

**Trap to watch.** Don't confuse *premises stated by the author* with *claims the author actually endorses*. The author might describe critics' views only to refute them. Track whose argument you're evaluating — the narrator's, the critic's, or the researcher's. The conclusion you must evaluate is the one the question stem points you at.

## @assumption-and-negation-test

An **assumption** is an unstated premise the argument *requires* to work. Not a plausible add-on — a *necessary* link. If the assumption is false, the argument collapses.

**The negation test — the single most powerful technique for assumption questions.**

1. Take each answer choice.
2. **Negate** it (turn it into its logical opposite).
3. If the negated version *destroys* the argument, that's the assumption.
4. If the negated version leaves the argument intact, that's NOT the assumption.

**Example.** Argument: "Company X switched from annual to quarterly performance reviews. Turnover dropped from 22% to 14%. Therefore, the new review system caused the turnover drop."

Candidate answers:

- A) "The new review system was more expensive than the old one."
- B) "No other major change at Company X during the same period contributed to the turnover drop."

Negate A: "The new review system was NOT more expensive than the old one." Does the argument collapse? No — cost doesn't matter to the causal claim. A is NOT the assumption.

Negate B: "Another major change at Company X during the same period also contributed to the turnover drop." Does this destroy the argument? Yes — if there's another cause, the review system isn't necessarily *the* cause. B is the assumption.

The negation test is mechanical. It converts "does the author need this?" (vague, subjective) into "does the argument break if this is false?" (precise, testable).

**The question stem decoder:**

| Stem wording | Type |
|---|---|
| "…the argument depends on which of the following assumptions…" | Assumption |
| "…which of the following is assumed by the argument…" | Assumption |
| "…the conclusion follows logically from which of the following assumptions…" | Assumption |

**What assumptions typically look like.**

1. **"No other cause exists."** Rules out alternative explanations. Most common type.
2. **"The evidence is representative."** If the argument uses a specific example or study, it often assumes that example generalizes.
3. **"A necessary condition is met."** The argument assumes some prerequisite is true.
4. **"The mechanism works as described."** The argument assumes the causal chain actually functions.

**The "extreme language" filter on assumption questions.** Assumptions tend to be *modest* — "there's no other major cause," "the sample isn't systematically biased." Answer choices that are too strong ("every single alternative has been ruled out," "it is impossible for anything else to have contributed") usually go too far. The argument doesn't *need* that much — it only needs the modest version. When in doubt, pick the weakest phrasing that still makes the argument work.

**Assumption vs. strengthen — a subtle but frequent confusion.** A strengthener makes the argument *more likely* to be true. An assumption makes it *possible* for the argument to be true. Every assumption, if stated, would strengthen — but not every strengthener is an assumption. The assumption is the *minimum* the argument needs; strengtheners are additional support on top.

**Example.** Argument: a green-space/asthma causation claim.

- Strengthener: "Studies show vegetation absorbs particulate matter that triggers asthma."
- Assumption: "Higher-income families don't specifically prefer green-space neighborhoods for reasons that also lower their asthma rates." (A weaker, more defensive claim — but essential.)

The strengthener provides a positive mechanism; the assumption just rules out one of several confounds.

> **Recall check.** Without looking, describe the four steps of the negation test. Now apply it in your head to: Argument "X caused Y because X happened before Y." Which of these is the necessary assumption — (a) "X is a very common cause of Y in general," or (b) "Nothing else besides X plausibly caused Y"? (Answer: b. Negate a → argument survives if X is just a cause occasionally. Negate b → some other cause makes X unnecessary, argument breaks.) This is retrieval practice on a novel stimulus — the most effective form of learning consolidation.

**Trap to watch.** The negated answer choice often looks weird and overly strong when you first read it ("Something other than X definitely caused Y"). That's fine — the negation is meant to sound aggressive. The point is whether it breaks the argument, not whether it sounds natural.

**Worked example.** Apply the negation test to a clean case. Argument: "A hospital adopted a new hand-washing protocol; infection rates fell 40%; the protocol caused the drop." Candidate assumption: "No other infection-control change was introduced in the same period." Negate it: "Another infection-control change *was* introduced in the same period." The argument collapses — the drop could be due to that other change — so the statement is a required assumption. Run that mechanical four-step on every assumption question and you stop guessing.

## @summary

Every Critical Reasoning question lives or dies on one thing: whether you found the **structure** before you read the answers. Untrained test-takers read for topic ("this one's about loyalty programs") and then pick the choice that *sounds* right. Trained test-takers read for **architecture** — what is being claimed, what is offered to support it, and where the support falls short of the claim. Structure first, always. This whole chapter reduces to three moves and one test, and you should be able to run them cold.

**Mental model.** Every argument is a bridge. The **conclusion** is the far bank — the point the author wants you to stand on. The **evidence** is the near bank — the facts you start from. The **gap** is the river between them: everything that must be true to get you across but is never stated. CR rewards the student who stares at the river, not the banks.

The three structural moves, in order:

- **Find the conclusion.** The claim everything else exists to support. It is *not* always last and *not* always signaled. Strip the argument to "the author wants me to believe ___." If a sentence could follow "therefore," it's the conclusion; if it could follow "because," it's evidence.
- **Find the evidence.** The stated facts, studies, premises, and observations offered as support. Treat these as **true** — the GMAT never wants you to dispute the data; it wants you to dispute the *leap* from the data.
- **Find the gap.** Ask: "Even if all the evidence is true, why might the conclusion still fail?" That space is the gap, and nearly every question type attacks, defends, or exploits it.

| Stem wording you see | What it's really asking |
|---|---|
| "depends on which assumption" | name the unstated bridge plank |
| "most weakens / undermines" | widen the gap |
| "most strengthens / supports" | shore up the gap |
| "if true, most justifies the conclusion" | build a missing plank |
| "which, if true, explains" | reconcile two facts (paradox) |

An **assumption** is the unstated premise the argument needs in order to work — a plank the author laid down silently. It is not a restatement of the conclusion and not new outside information; it is the **necessary** connector between the evidence you were given and the conclusion you were sold.

**Worked example.** "TechBrew installed self-order kiosks last quarter and saw average wait times drop 40%. Therefore, the kiosks improved customer satisfaction." Evidence: kiosks in, waits down 40%. Conclusion: satisfaction up. The gap: *shorter waits equal happier customers*, and *nothing else* explains the drop. The assumption is that lower wait time actually maps to higher satisfaction — and that no other factor (fewer customers, a confusing interface) is quietly dragging satisfaction down.

Here is where the chapter's one mechanical tool earns its keep: the **negation test**. To check whether a choice is a required assumption, **negate it** and ask whether the argument collapses.

- Negate the candidate assumption.
- If the negation **destroys** the conclusion, it was required — correct.
- If the negation leaves the conclusion **standing**, it was not required — wrong.

Run it on the example. Candidate: "Shorter wait times increase satisfaction at TechBrew." Negated: "Shorter wait times do *not* increase satisfaction." Now the 40% drop tells you nothing about satisfaction, and the conclusion falls apart — the plank was load-bearing. Contrast a tempting wrong choice: "Kiosks reduced labor costs." Negated: "Kiosks did not reduce labor costs." The satisfaction conclusion survives untouched. Irrelevant, eliminated.

**Trap to watch.** Students negate too softly. The negation of "all managers approved" is **not** "no managers approved" — it's "at least one manager did not approve." Negate to the *logical minimum*, not the dramatic opposite. Over-negating makes weak choices look load-bearing and quietly wrecks your accuracy.

> **Recall check.** What are the three structural moves, in order? (Find the conclusion, find the evidence, find the gap.) Pull this from memory instead of re-skimming the page — forced retrieval is what makes the sequence automatic under timed pressure, whereas re-reading only feels productive and builds no recall.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does negating a *necessary* assumption break the argument, while negating a merely *helpful* fact does not? Put it in your own words: what is the difference between a plank the bridge needs to stand and a decoration bolted to the side?

You now own the engine: structure (conclusion / evidence / gap), the definition of an assumption, and the negation test that turns assumption questions into a mechanical check instead of a judgment call. In the next chapter, **CR Task Families**, you'll point exactly these tools at the nine question types — Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Evaluate, Flaw, Paradox, Inference, Boldface, and Conclusion — and see that each is just a different operation performed on the **same gap** you already know how to find.
