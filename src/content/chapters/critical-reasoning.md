## @argument-structure

Every Critical Reasoning argument has three parts. Your first job on every question — before you even read the question stem — is to identify them:

**Mental model.** Every CR argument has three parts: evidence, conclusion, and the unstated gap that bridges them. The question is *always* about the gap — strengthening it, weakening it, naming it, evaluating it. Find the gap before you read the answer choices, and the right answer announces itself; read the choices first and you're solving by feel.

**1. Conclusion.** What the author wants you to believe. The main claim. Usually introduced with signal words: "therefore," "thus," "hence," "it follows that," "the chain's management concludes," "researchers hypothesize," "critics argue."

**2. Evidence (or premises).** The facts and reasons the author offers in support of the conclusion. Usually descriptive statements: data, studies, observations, historical facts. No "therefore" attached.

**3. The gap.** The unstated leap from evidence to conclusion. This is the single most important thing to identify on every CR question — because nearly every CR question (strengthen, weaken, assumption, flaw) is about this gap. The gap is whatever *else* would have to be true for the evidence to actually prove the conclusion.

**Worked example.** "A regional grocery chain launched a loyalty program three months ago. Since then, revenue is up 8%. The chain's management concludes the loyalty program is responsible."

- **Conclusion:** The loyalty program caused the revenue increase.
- **Evidence:** Program launched 3 months ago; revenue up 8% in that period.
- **Gap:** "No other explanation accounts for the revenue increase." The argument assumes that nothing else changed in those 3 months (no ad campaign, no competitor closing, no seasonal effect, no new products).

Notice how the gap is phrased as *what the argument takes for granted*. That's the template: look at the evidence, look at the conclusion, and ask "what else would have to be true to get from here to there?"

**The three moves in order:**

1. **Find the conclusion.** Underline or mentally tag the main claim. Signal words help, but sometimes the conclusion is at the beginning, sometimes the end, sometimes the middle.
2. **Find the evidence.** Everything else in the passage that's presented as fact.
3. **Find the gap.** What assumption bridges the two? What alternative explanation could exist?

Once you've done these three moves, *then* read the question stem. Not before. The structure is the same regardless of which question type you're asked — the gap is always what you're working with.

**Conclusion vs. evidence — the "why" test.** If you can insert "because" or "the reason is" between two statements, the second is evidence for the first. "The loyalty program caused the revenue rise (conclusion) *because* revenue went up 8% after launch (evidence)." Always read evidence as answering the question "*why* does the author believe the conclusion?"

**Common conclusion signal phrases:**

| Phrase | What follows |
|---|---|
| "Therefore…" | The conclusion |
| "Thus…" | The conclusion |
| "So…" (at the start of a sentence) | The conclusion |
| "It follows that…" | The conclusion |
| "We can conclude…" | The conclusion |
| "Researchers argue…" | The conclusion |
| "The company's management believes…" | The conclusion (author may disagree) |

Note the last one: if the author *describes* what management believes, the author's own conclusion may be that management is wrong. Reading for *who* is making the claim matters.

**Common evidence signal phrases:** "Since…", "Because…", "Given that…", "After all…", "In fact…", "Studies show…", "Data indicate…"

**Micro-drill.** For each mini-argument, name the conclusion and state the gap in one phrase. 60 seconds total:

1. "Sales of our flagship app rose 30% the month after we cut its price. The price cut clearly drove the increase." → conclusion: ___ ; gap: ___
2. "Employees who attended the optional wellness program had fewer sick days than those who didn't. The program must improve health." → conclusion: ___ ; gap: ___

Answers: (1) Conclusion — *the price cut caused the 30% sales rise*. Gap — *nothing else (a marketing push, a seasonal spike, a competitor's outage) caused the rise*. (2) Conclusion — *the wellness program improves health*. Gap — *the two groups were comparable to begin with* — but attendance was optional, so healthier, more motivated employees likely self-selected in (reverse/selection effect). Naming the gap is the move; once you have it, every question type tells you what to do with it.

> **Recall check.** Close your eyes. State the three parts of every CR argument. Now recall: which part is almost always the subject of the question you'll be asked? (Answer: the gap — the assumption bridging evidence to conclusion.) Forced retrieval of the skeleton makes it available on every CR question you'll face; re-reading the paragraph doesn't.

**Trap to watch.** Don't confuse *premises stated by the author* with *claims the author actually endorses*. The author might describe critics' views only to refute them. Track whose argument you're evaluating — the narrator's, the critic's, or the researcher's. The conclusion you must evaluate is the one the question stem points you at.

## @boldface-and-complete

Two GMAT question types that look different on the page but work on the same structural foundation you just learned — identifying the parts of an argument.

### Boldface questions

Two portions of an argument are marked in **bold**. The question asks you to identify the role each bold portion plays in the argument.

**The template.** For each bold portion, ask: is it a premise (evidence), the main conclusion, a subsidiary conclusion, an objection the author is addressing, or a counter-position the author is arguing against?

**The five main roles:**

1. **Main conclusion** — the primary claim the author is advancing.
2. **Premise supporting the main conclusion** — evidence for the main claim.
3. **Subsidiary conclusion / intermediate conclusion** — a claim the author draws from evidence that's then used as evidence for the main conclusion.
4. **Objection or counter-claim** — a view the author mentions, often to refute.
5. **Concession** — something the author grants to the other side before arguing for their own conclusion.

**Template for solving.**

1. Find the main conclusion of the argument first (ignore boldface initially).
2. For each bold portion, ask whether it supports the main conclusion, *is* the main conclusion, is a counter-claim, or is a subsidiary step.
3. Match to answer choices.

**Common answer pairs.** "Bold 1 is a premise that supports the main conclusion; Bold 2 is the main conclusion." Or: "Bold 1 is a counter-position the author disputes; Bold 2 is the evidence the author uses to dispute it." GMAT boldface answers almost always use the same small vocabulary — "supports," "is the conclusion," "is a claim the author disputes," "is evidence for the author's position."

### Complete-the-argument questions

The passage ends with a blank or a "therefore…" that cuts off. You're asked which answer choice best completes the argument.

**The template.** The answer is the conclusion that follows most logically from the premises given — and typically, the answer directly addresses the gap between evidence and what the evidence actually shows.

**Example stem.** "The company's profits have increased every quarter since it launched the new product line. However, customer satisfaction has been steadily declining. Therefore, ______."

The completion should bridge the evidence (profits up, satisfaction down) to a conclusion that accounts for both. A strong completion: "…the company's profit gains may not be sustainable, since declining satisfaction typically precedes customer churn." It connects both pieces of evidence to a forward-looking claim.

**What to avoid.** Completions that are too strong ("profits will definitely drop"), too narrow (addresses only one piece of evidence), or introduce outside assumptions ("the company should fire its CEO" — unsupported).

**The question stem decoder:**

| Stem wording | Type |
|---|---|
| "…the role played by the boldface portions…" | Boldface |
| "…in the argument, the two portions in boldface play which of the following roles…" | Boldface |
| "…which of the following best completes the argument…" | Complete |
| "…which of the following most logically completes the passage…" | Complete |

**The "logical link" filter on complete-the-argument.** The right completion follows from the stated premises — it doesn't require any unstated assumption. If you find yourself adding "well, we'd also need to assume…" to make an answer work, it's wrong. The right answer is supported by the passage alone.

> **Recall check.** List the five main roles a boldface portion can play. Now list three things to verify about any completion answer (logical follow, addresses all premises, no outside assumption). Retrieval practice on these structural categories is what makes boldface and complete questions feel mechanical rather than fresh puzzles.

## @cr-answer-choice-traps

The type-specific chapters give you the strategy for *what to look for* on each question type. This section gives you the six patterns that show up in *wrong answers* across every type. Memorize these and your elimination speed roughly doubles — most wrong answers fall into one of these buckets, and recognizing the pattern lets you reject the choice in two seconds rather than re-evaluating the whole argument.

**Trap 1: Out-of-scope.**

The answer introduces something the argument never addresses — a different population, a different time period, a different metric, a different topic entirely. The answer might be a true statement about the world; it just doesn't bear on the specific argument.

*Example.* Argument: "Company X switched to quarterly reviews; turnover dropped." Out-of-scope answer: "Companies with quarterly reviews are also more likely to offer parental leave." The answer talks about a different policy — it has nothing to do with the causal claim about reviews and turnover.

The cure: **before reading any answer, name the gap in one sentence**. ("Does the policy switch explain the turnover drop, or could something else?") Then ask of each answer: "does this touch *that* gap?" If no — eliminate, regardless of how interesting the statement sounds.

**Trap 2: Half-right, half-wrong.**

The first clause of the answer fits the gap perfectly; the second clause introduces a fact that's irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope. Students under time pressure read the first clause, recognize a match, and bubble — without finishing the sentence.

*Example.* Strengthen-the-argument question. Answer reads: "Studies show the new review system improves retention, *and the system is widely used in Fortune 500 companies*." The first clause strengthens; the second clause is irrelevant ("widely used" doesn't bear on whether it works). The right answer would be a pure strengthener with no irrelevant tail.

The cure: **read every answer to its period before deciding**. If any portion of the answer is irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope, the whole answer is wrong. CR doesn't grade on a partial-credit scale.

**Trap 3: Number/percent confusion.**

The argument cites a percentage; the right-answer candidates flip to absolute counts (or vice versa). Often the "weakener" or "strengthener" depends on which metric the argument was making a claim about.

*Example.* Argument: "Region A has a 5% youth unemployment rate; Region B has 8%. Therefore Region A's economy is healthier for young workers." A "weakener" that says "Region B's labor force is twice as large, so Region B employs more young people overall" attacks the wrong claim — the argument was about *rates*, not absolute counts. A correct weakener would attack the rate-to-health link directly.

The cure: **before evaluating answers, identify which metric the conclusion rests on**. Argument about a percentage → answers about absolute counts are usually irrelevant. Argument about absolute counts → answers about percentages are usually irrelevant. The two flip every test session.

**Trap 4: Time-frame shift.**

The argument is about a specific period (past results, future predictions, current state). The wrong answer references a different time frame — "in the long run," "historically," "next decade" — and either strengthens or weakens a claim about that different period, not the one the argument made.

*Example.* Argument: "Last quarter's sales rose 12% after the rebrand. Therefore the rebrand drove sales." A wrong "weakener": "Brand-loyal customers will likely fatigue from the new image within 18 months." That weakens a *future* claim the argument didn't make. The argument was only about last quarter.

The cure: **note the time frame of the conclusion explicitly**. If the argument says "last quarter," reject answers that talk about future quarters, past years, or steady-state trends.

**Trap 5: The cited view trap.**

Some arguments cite an opposing view to refute it. Question: "Identify the author's main conclusion." Student picks the view the author *attacked*, not the view the author *endorsed*.

*Example.* Passage: "Critics claim the new tax will reduce small-business hiring. But payroll data from three similar reforms in the EU show no such effect. The tax should therefore not be expected to harm hiring." Wrong "main conclusion" pick: "The new tax will reduce small-business hiring." That's the *cited* view. The author's actual conclusion is the third sentence — that the tax will *not* harm hiring.

The cure: **after reading the passage, ask 'whose claim is this?' for each sentence**. The author's view is what the passage builds toward; the cited view is what the passage argues against. Words like "however," "but," "critics claim," "some argue" usually signal a cited view about to be refuted.

**Trap 6: Extreme language.**

Answers containing "always," "never," "all," "none," "only," "must," or "impossible" are usually wrong — because the right CR answer typically establishes a *plausibility shift*, not a universal truth. The argument needs something modest enough to be defensible; extreme statements are easy to refute with a single counterexample.

This is especially true on **assumption** questions: the right assumption is the *minimum* the argument needs. Extreme assumptions are almost always overshoot.

*Example.* Assumption question. Wrong: "No factor whatsoever, in any company anywhere in the world, has ever contributed to a turnover drop other than performance-review structure." Right: "No other major change at Company X during the same period contributed to the turnover drop." The wrong one is true-only-in-utopia; the right one is the modest claim the argument actually requires.

The cure: **when two answers compete, prefer the milder one**. Extreme language is a discrimination signal. The GMAT tests modest, defensible reasoning, not absolute truths.

**The unified meta-discipline.**

All six traps share a structure: the wrong answer is *plausibly related* to the argument but doesn't satisfy the *specific demand* of the question. The discipline that catches all of them is the same:

1. **Name the gap or the demand in one sentence** before reading answers.
2. **Read each answer to its period** — check both halves.
3. **Test the answer against the specific demand**, not against general truth or plausibility.

That three-step routine adds ~15 seconds per question and saves you from the traps that the test writers spend the most effort planting.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why are these six traps universal across CR question types rather than tied to one type? If you can say "because they're patterns about how *answer choices* are constructed — not about how *arguments* work — and the same construction tricks apply whether the question asks for a strengthener, an assumption, or an inference," you've understood that CR mastery is two layers: argument-reading (which differs per type) and answer-elimination (which doesn't). The elimination layer is where most students hemorrhage points on hard questions.

## @summary

Critical Reasoning rests on three structural moves and one elimination discipline. This chapter gave you both. Once they're automatic, each question type is just a specific demand placed on the same argument skeleton.

**The three structural moves — always, before reading the question stem:**

1. **Find the conclusion.** Who's arguing what?
2. **Find the evidence.** What supports the conclusion?
3. **Find the gap.** What's the unstated leap between them?

**The six universal answer-choice traps:** out-of-scope, half-right/half-wrong, number/percent confusion, time-frame shift, the cited-view trap, and extreme language. Name the demand, read to the period, test against the demand.

**Your next step — the five question-type chapters.** Each one takes the skeleton above and gives you a dedicated technique for that question's specific demand. Study them in any order; if Verbal is your weak area, start at the top and work down.

| Chapter | The question's demand | The technique you'll learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengthen & Weaken** | Narrow or widen the gap | The five alternative-cause patterns and their mirror strengtheners |
| **Assumption** | State the necessary premise | The negation test — if it's false, the argument collapses |
| **Inference** | State what *must* be true | "Consistent with" ≠ "must be true"; quantifier and contrapositive logic |
| **Evaluate** | Find the decisive question | The two-way test — yes strengthens, no weakens |
| **Flaw & Paradox** | Name the error / reconcile the facts | The five flaw types; the both-facts test for paradox |

**Time-management targets (every CR type).**

- Easy CR: 45-60 seconds.
- Medium CR: 75-90 seconds.
- Hard CR: up to 2 minutes — but rarely more.

If you're past 2 minutes on any CR question, flag and move on. Your first read should always be the passage (to do the three moves), and only then the stem.

**What to do next.** Drill the mixed problem sets below to lock in the skeleton and the trap patterns on general CR questions. Then move into the type chapters — each has its own pretest, focused readings, and graded problem sets. Any CR question you miss in practice or on a mock: log the *type* and the *trap* in the error log, so you can see which specific question type is costing you points and study that chapter again.
