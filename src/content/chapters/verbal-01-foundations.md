## @how-verbal-works

GMAT Focus Verbal is two question types, full stop: **Critical Reasoning** and **Reading Comprehension**. Sentence Correction is gone — the grammar drills you may have heard about from older GMAT prep do not exist on this test anymore. That single fact changes your whole preparation: there is no rulebook of idioms or subject-verb edge cases to memorize. What's left is **reasoning about text**, and reasoning is a skill you build by training your eye, not your memory.

Here is the shape of the section. You get **23 questions in 45 minutes** — call it roughly **two minutes per question**, though RC clusters several questions on one shared passage and CR gives you self-contained problems. Verbal is scored **60–90** and weighted **equally** with Quant and Data Insights in your Total Score. The section is computer-adaptive, so every answer shapes what comes next. Two minutes is generous if you read well and brutal if you read everything twice.

**Mental model.** Read every Verbal stimulus on two connected levels: **what it means** and **how its parts work together**. Understand what each sentence says, whose view it represents, and any limits on the claim; then identify its role in the surrounding text. A sentence can state a claim, supply evidence, give background, qualify an idea, or address an objection. You cannot reliably identify that function without understanding the content. Read for **meaning and function together.**

This is the throughline of the entire Verbal track: build a **map that preserves meaning**. Track the main ideas, their support, the different viewpoints, and the moves between them. You do not need to memorize every number or name, but you do need to understand what the evidence shows and notice qualifications such as "some," "only," and "may." Record where details belong so you can return to them. A structural map helps you locate relevant evidence; it does not replace reading that evidence carefully.

> **Recall check.** Which question type was removed from the Focus Edition, and how many questions and minutes does the section give you? (Sentence Correction was removed; 23 questions in 45 minutes.) Answer from memory before reading on — forcing retrieval now wires the fact in far better than glancing back up at the paragraph.

The two disciplines train the same muscle at different scales.

| Discipline | What you're given | What you do with it | The core skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical Reasoning (CR) | A short stimulus, often an argument, ~3–5 sentences | **Analyze** its claims, evidence, and relationships for the task | Evaluating reasoning and drawing supported conclusions |
| Reading Comprehension (RC) | A passage, ~200–350 words | **Understand and map** its ideas, purpose, and paragraph relationships | Interpreting meaning, function, and relevant evidence |

In an argument-based CR question, you isolate the conclusion, understand the evidence, and examine the unstated connection between them. RC asks you to understand a longer passage while tracking why each paragraph exists, how the ideas connect, and whose stance is being expressed. Not every passage is an argument for one conclusion, and not every CR task asks you to find a gap. Different scales, shared habit: **understand the text, map its relationships, then answer the specific question.**

That last step matters as much as the first two. Read the question stem precisely: it names the exact task. Then check the relevant evidence, whether it is one sentence, a relationship across paragraphs, or the passage as a whole. For a detail question, verify the stated fact and its limits. For a function question, connect what the cited text says to its role. For a strengthen or weaken question, assess how the new information affects the particular conclusion, not merely whether it concerns the same topic.

**Worked example.** A stimulus argues: "City buses switched to electric engines last year. Since then, downtown air-quality complaints have fallen 30%. The new engines are therefore responsible for the cleaner air."

The **conclusion** attributes cleaner air to the new engines. The **evidence** is a decline in complaints after the switch, not a direct measurement of air quality. There are two gaps to examine: do fewer complaints indicate cleaner air, and did the engines cause that improvement? A change in how complaints were collected could explain the reported decline; a factory closing could offer a competing explanation for cleaner air. An unrelated change downtown would do neither.

- For a **weaken** question, look for information that undermines the evidence or causal link.
- For a **strengthen** question, look for information that supports that link or reduces a relevant alternative explanation.
- For a **necessary-assumption** question, test what the reasoning needs, not the much stronger claim that nothing else changed.

The same map helps with all three tasks, but it does not give them the same answer.

**Trap to watch.** Understanding isolated sentences without connecting them. You may follow every statement yet miss what the second paragraph *accomplishes* in relation to the first. The reverse mistake is labeling a paragraph "evidence" without understanding what it supports or how strongly. Check both: can you paraphrase the paragraph's main idea and explain its role? If either is unclear, reread the relevant lines before relying on your map.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why do "what does this sentence say?" and "what is it doing here?" need to work together? Explain how knowing both would help you answer a function question, and why a detail question would still require checking the relevant wording.

Carry one mindset into everything that follows: **meaning, function, then question-specific evidence.** Every CR and RC technique in this track builds on understanding the text and using its organization to check the exact task on the screen.

## @active-reading

Active reading combines **comprehension and attention to function**. As you read a Reading Comprehension passage or Critical Reasoning stimulus, paraphrase its important ideas and connect them to the surrounding text. Keep the map brief enough to use, but specific enough to distinguish what the author claims from what someone else believes. The aim is not perfect recall of every detail; it is a sound understanding that lets you find and evaluate the evidence a question requires.

**Mental model.** Build a map of ideas and relationships, not a two-bucket classification. **Claims** and **evidence** are central to arguments, but passages also explain, compare, describe, and qualify. Useful roles to notice include:

- **Claims:** assertions or positions, including the author's conclusion and views attributed to others. Ask whose claim it is and what it says.
- **Evidence:** reasons, findings, or examples offered in support of a claim. Ask what they support and what they actually establish.
- **Background:** context or definitions that make the discussion understandable without necessarily supporting a conclusion.
- **Qualifications:** limits on a claim's scope, certainty, or conditions. Preserve them in your paraphrase.
- **Objections:** challenges to a claim or its support. Track both the challenge and any response.
- **Concessions:** points a writer accepts while maintaining or refining a broader position. Acceptance need not be followed by a rebuttal.
- **Transitions:** connections between ideas, such as contrast, continuation, or a shift of topic or viewpoint.

These are reading aids, not an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive sentence types. One sentence can concede a point and qualify a claim; a claim can also support a later conclusion. Tag the relationships that clarify the passage rather than forcing every sentence into one label.

So read with two linked questions: *What does this mean? How does it relate to what came before?* Notice whether a statement adds evidence, changes viewpoint, narrows a claim, or introduces an objection. **Structure words** can help you recognize these relationships, but their meaning depends on context. Use them as clues and confirm the connection by reading the statements on both sides.

| Signal phrase | Relationship to check |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, hence, so | A conclusion may follow; identify its support and whether it is the main or an intermediate conclusion |
| because, since, given that | A reason may follow; identify the claim it supports ("since" can also mark time) |
| for example, for instance, consider | An illustration or case may follow; understand what it shows and where to find its details |
| however, but, yet, nevertheless | A contrast or qualification; identify which ideas differ and whose views they are |
| although, while, granted, admittedly | A concession or contrast may follow; check what is accepted and what is limited ("while" can also mark time) |
| moreover, furthermore, in addition | An addition; check whether it adds evidence, another point, or another objection |

When you hit **"however,"** check the contrast; the earlier statement may remain true and important. When you hit **"therefore,"** ask which preceding statements support what follows and whether that conclusion supports another claim later. Signal words help you find relationships, but they do not settle the author's position or the main conclusion by themselves.

**Worked example.** Take this stimulus: *"Many retailers assume that loyalty programs increase profits. A national chain found that members spent 30 percent more per visit than non-members. However, the chain also discovered that members would have shopped there anyway, and the discounts offered through the program erased most of the added revenue. The program therefore did little to improve the chain's bottom line."* Map meaning and function, sentence by sentence: (1) a **common assumption** attributed to retailers, which the author will challenge; (2) **apparent support** for it, since members spent more per visit, though spending is not profit; (3) a **contrast** introducing existing shopping intentions and the cost of discounts, which limit what the spending comparison establishes; (4) the author's **conclusion** that this program did little to improve profits. Your map: *common view, spending comparison, limits on that evidence, limited profit benefit.* The 30-percent figure is not disposable: a detail question could ask about the comparison, while a function question could ask why it was introduced. Return to its wording to check that it compares spending **per visit**, not total spending or profit.

**Trap to watch.** Indiscriminate highlighting. Marking every number, name, and clause can leave you with a page that shows no priorities. Mark **meaningful relationships and decisive wording**: a conclusion and its support, a shift of viewpoint, a qualification, or a detail you need to locate again. Highlighting and rereading can be useful when they serve a specific purpose. Do not skip an example merely because it is an example; understand what it illustrates, then return for the precise details when the question requires them.

This is why active reading underlies both halves of Verbal. In Critical Reasoning, strengthening, weakening, or finding an assumption requires understanding the conclusion, its support, and the connection being tested. In Reading Comprehension, you need both the passage's organization and its substantive claims. Use your map to locate the relevant text, then check the answer against its meaning, scope, and qualifications. Some questions need a local detail; others need connections across the passage.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: name two words that can signal a conclusion and one that can signal a contrast. (Conclusion: *therefore, thus*. Contrast: *however, but, yet*.) Then explain why you still need to read the statements they connect. Try recalling first; revisit the table to check and correct your answer.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** In the loyalty-program example, explain both what the spending comparison says and how it functions before the contrast. How would you use that understanding differently for a detail question and a question about the author's reasoning?

Understand the ideas, map their relationships, and verify the evidence required by the question.

## @argument-anatomy

An argument offers **premises** as support for a **conclusion**. Some arguments leave an unstated connection or a weakness in that support; others support their conclusions without a missing assumption. Start by understanding the claims and their relationship, then use the question stem to decide what to examine. This anatomy is useful for argument-based CR questions and RC questions about reasoning, but not every passage or CR stimulus is an argument.

**Mental model.** Think of the reasoning as a bridge from premises to conclusion. The **premises** are the starting claims offered as support; the **conclusion** is the claim the author wants you to accept on that basis. A **necessary assumption** is an unstated claim the reasoning depends on. A fact can strengthen an argument without being necessary to it, so do not treat every helpful fact or possible concern as an assumption.

Start by separating evidence from conclusion. Students confuse these constantly because both are just declarative sentences sitting next to each other. Two tools fix this.

First, the **why test**. The conclusion is the claim the rest of the argument exists to support. So ask of each sentence: *why should I believe this?* The conclusion is the sentence the others answer; the premises are the ones doing the answering. Equivalently, drop the word **because** between two statements — whichever direction reads naturally tells you which is evidence.

**Worked example.** "TrailBrew should open its third café downtown. Foot traffic in the downtown core has risen 40 percent since the new transit hub opened." Try it both ways. "TrailBrew should open downtown *because* foot traffic rose 40 percent" reads fine. "Foot traffic rose 40 percent *because* TrailBrew should open downtown" is nonsense. So the foot-traffic sentence is the **premise**, and "should open downtown" is the **conclusion**. The why test resolved it in one pass.

Second, **conclusion signal words**. Authors often flag the conclusion explicitly, and learning the markers turns identification into recognition instead of reasoning.

| Signal phrase | What it marks |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently | conclusion follows |
| clearly, evidently, it follows that | conclusion follows |
| because, since, for, given that, as | premise follows |
| after all, in light of the fact that | premise follows |

One caution: not every conclusion is signaled, and "since" can mark *time* rather than *reason*. Treat signal words as a fast first guess, then confirm with the why test.

Now examine the **gap**, if there is one. In TrailBrew's case, more foot traffic does not by itself establish that opening a café downtown is a good decision. The visitors' likelihood of buying coffee, likely revenue relative to costs, and existing competition could all affect that recommendation. Those are questions to investigate, not automatically necessary assumptions. For example, a traffic increase near an existing café need not make the new downtown café a bad idea. Judge each answer by how it bears on this recommendation and by what the question asks.

**Trap to watch.** Students treat the conclusion as another fact to be analyzed, instead of as the thing being defended. The moment you start arguing about whether the conclusion is *true*, you've left the task. The GMAT never asks you to agree with the author. It asks you to find what the author needs, assumed, or overlooked. Stay on the bridge, not on the far bank.

> **Recall check.** What distinguishes a premise, a conclusion, and a necessary assumption? (A premise supplies support; the conclusion is the claim supported; a necessary assumption is an unstated claim the reasoning depends on.) Does every potentially helpful fact have to be a necessary assumption? Explain why not.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** In "TrailBrew should open downtown because foot traffic rose," which part is the conclusion and which is the premise? (The recommendation comes first; the reason follows "because.") The order can also be reversed: "Because foot traffic rose, TrailBrew should open downtown." Explain why the support relationship, not sentence order alone, identifies their roles.

Use this anatomy where the text presents an argument. In Reading Comprehension, a study might support a claim, illustrate a method, supply background, or introduce an objection. Its role depends on the surrounding text. In Critical Reasoning, the question may ask you to assess support, draw an inference, or resolve an apparent discrepancy; finding a missing assumption is not always the task.

The CR chapters develop **nine question types**: Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Evaluate, Flaw, Inference, Boldface, Paradox, and Method of Reasoning. Argument anatomy helps with many of them, but their standards differ. An inference must follow from the information; a paradox answer must explain how apparently conflicting facts can both hold; a strengthen answer need only improve the support for the conclusion. Let the stem determine which job you do.

**Worked example.** Read this with the anatomy lens: "Sales of our premium plan rose after we added live chat support; therefore, live chat is driving the upgrades." The **conclusion** attributes the increase to live chat; the **evidence** is that sales rose after chat launched. Timing alone does not show that chat contributed to the increase. A simultaneous promotion or seasonal change could offer an alternative explanation, but an unrelated website change would not. Evidence separating chat's effect from relevant competing causes would strengthen the argument. You need not assume that nothing else changed: several factors can contribute to the same outcome.

## @summary

You've now built the foundation. Before you touch a single Critical Reasoning argument or Reading Comprehension passage, lock in the operating system this whole chapter installed: GMAT Focus Verbal is a closed, learnable system, and you win it by running the same disciplined moves every time — not by reading harder or reacting faster.

**Mental model.** GMAT Focus Verbal has two question families: **Critical Reasoning (CR)** and **Reading Comprehension (RC)**. Sentence Correction is gone. Both require **understanding meaning, recognizing relationships, and checking evidence for the exact question**. A structural map keeps the ideas organized; careful reading preserves what those ideas actually say. Recognizing a question type helps you choose an approach, but the answer must still fit the particular text and task.

Here are the foundational moves, all on one screen:

- **Scope the test.** CR + RC only. Roughly **45 minutes for ~23 questions**, so about **2 minutes per question** — generous enough to think, tight enough that re-reading whole passages will sink you.
- **Read for meaning and function.** Ask both "what does this say?" and "what is it doing here?" Map claims, evidence, background, qualifications, objections, concessions, and transitions without losing the content or confusing viewpoints.
- **Find the argument anatomy when there is an argument.** Separate the **premises** from the **conclusion** and examine any unstated connection. Do not force descriptive passages or tasks such as explaining a discrepancy into a premise-conclusion-gap template.
- **Check the evidence for the exact task.** Paraphrase, connect, and return to the relevant wording. Preserve quantities, comparisons, and qualifications when they matter to an answer; use the whole passage when the question requires it.

Use this decoder to convert what you read into what to do:

| What you're reading | Your job in that moment |
|---|---|
| A CR stimulus | Understand the statements and task; where an argument is present, identify its **conclusion**, **premises**, and any **gap** |
| An RC paragraph | Paraphrase its main idea and tag its **function**; retain key qualifications and locate supporting details |
| A signal word: *therefore, thus, hence* | Check whether it introduces a **conclusion**, whose conclusion it is, and what supports it |
| A signal word: *because, since, given that* | Check for a **reason** and the claim it supports; confirm the word's meaning in context |
| A signal word: *but, however, yet, although* | Identify the **contrast or concession** without automatically rejecting what came before |
| A question stem | Identify the exact **task**, then check the relevant text and evidence against each candidate answer |

**Worked example.** "A regional grocery chain found that customers enrolled in its loyalty program spend 30% more per visit than non-members. The chain concludes that expanding the program will raise overall revenue." Run the moves. The **conclusion** is "expanding the program will raise revenue." The **premise** is the 30% spending gap. The **gap**? The argument treats the comparison as evidence that expanding membership will increase revenue, but already-high spenders might be more likely to enroll. Understanding what the comparison measures and how it supports the prediction reveals that possible alternative explanation. To answer a question, you still need to identify the exact task and check how each choice bears on the stated evidence or conclusion.

**Trap to watch.** A vague "I get it" feeling is not enough, whether it comes from recognizing the topic or spotting a familiar argument pattern. In the grocery-chain example, you need to understand what the spending comparison measures and how it is being used to predict revenue. A structural label alone cannot establish causation, and recalling the percentage alone cannot explain the reasoning. Connect the content to its role, then assess the evidence needed for the question you are actually asked.

> **Recall check.** Without looking up, name the two question types on GMAT Focus Verbal and the three parts of argument anatomy. (CR and RC; premise, conclusion, gap/assumption.) Pulling this from memory now — instead of re-skimming the chapter — is what wires it in so it's available under test pressure.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** How does understanding the conclusion help you evaluate the premises, and why must you still read the question stem carefully? Explain how the same argument would require different work for a weaken question and a question asking for the role of a statement.

You now have the mindset: understand meaning and function together, map the relationships, and check relevant evidence for the exact question. Next, you'll develop the CR half in more depth.
