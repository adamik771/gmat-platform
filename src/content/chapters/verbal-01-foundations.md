## @how-verbal-works

GMAT Focus Verbal is two question types, full stop: **Critical Reasoning** and **Reading Comprehension**. Sentence Correction is gone — the grammar drills you may have heard about from older GMAT prep do not exist on this test anymore. That single fact changes your whole preparation: there is no rulebook of idioms or subject-verb edge cases to memorize. What's left is **reasoning about text**, and reasoning is a skill you build by training your eye, not your memory.

Here is the shape of the section. You get **23 questions in 45 minutes** — call it roughly **two minutes per question**, though RC clusters several questions on one shared passage and CR gives you self-contained problems. Verbal is scored **60–90** and weighted **equally** with Quant and Data Insights in your Total Score. The section is computer-adaptive, so every answer shapes what comes next. Two minutes is generous if you read well and brutal if you read everything twice.

**Mental model.** Treat every Verbal stimulus as a machine with moving parts, not a paragraph of facts. Your job is to see what each sentence is *doing* — is it a claim, the evidence for that claim, a concession, a counterexample, a definition? — before you worry about what it *says*. The test almost never rewards you for recalling a detail. It rewards you for knowing the role a piece of text plays in the structure around it. Read for **function first, content second.**

This is the throughline of the entire Verbal track, so internalize it now. Weak test-takers read to absorb information, the way you'd cram a textbook before an exam. The GMAT punishes that: it buries one load-bearing sentence inside three forgettable ones, then asks a question only the load-bearing sentence answers. Strong test-takers read to **map** — they tag the conclusion, the support, and the moves between them, and let the rest wash over them on the first pass. When the question comes, they already know where to look.

> **Recall check.** Which question type was removed from the Focus Edition, and how many questions and minutes does the section give you? (Sentence Correction was removed; 23 questions in 45 minutes.) Answer from memory before reading on — forcing retrieval now wires the fact in far better than glancing back up at the paragraph.

The two disciplines train the same muscle at different scales.

| Discipline | What you're given | What you do with it | The core skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical Reasoning (CR) | A short argument, ~3–5 sentences | **Dissect** it into conclusion + evidence + assumption | Spotting the logical gap |
| Reading Comprehension (RC) | A passage, ~200–350 words | **Map** it into purpose + structure per paragraph | Tracking the author's intent |

CR is structure under a microscope: one tight argument where you isolate the conclusion, find the evidence, and name the unstated assumption bridging them. RC is structure at scale: a longer passage where you don't memorize content but build a skeleton — why each paragraph exists and how the author's stance shifts. Different sizes, identical instinct: **find the structure, then answer the specific question.**

That second half matters as much as the first. Once you've mapped the structure, you do not free-associate toward an answer. You read the question stem precisely — it names the exact task — and you return to the one part of the structure that task touches.

**Worked example.** A stimulus argues: "City buses switched to electric engines last year. Since then, downtown air-quality complaints have fallen 30%. The new engines are therefore responsible for the cleaner air." Don't memorize "30%." Tag the parts: the **conclusion** is "the engines caused the cleaner air"; the **evidence** is the timing of two events; the **assumption** is that nothing else downtown changed. Now whatever the stem asks — weaken, strengthen, find the assumption — you already know the soft spot: any other change last year (fewer cars, a factory closing) attacks it. You answer from the structure, not the trivia.

**Trap to watch.** The detail trap. You'll finish reading and feel you "understood" the passage because you followed every sentence. Then a question asks what the second paragraph *accomplishes*, and you have nothing — you tracked words, not roles. Comprehension is not the goal; **structural awareness** is. If you can't say in one phrase what a paragraph or sentence is doing, you haven't yet read it the way the GMAT demands.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why would a test deliberately reward structure over content — why is "what is this sentence doing" a better predictor of the right answer than "what does this sentence say"? Articulate the mechanism in your own words; if you can explain *why* the strategy works, you'll hold to it under time pressure instead of reverting to slow, detail-hungry reading.

Carry one mindset into everything that follows: **structure beats content.** Every CR and RC technique in this track is a specific way of finding structure fast, then aiming it at the exact question on the screen.

## @active-reading

Reading on the GMAT is not about comprehension. It is about **function**. A Reading Comprehension passage and a Critical Reasoning stimulus are machines with moving parts, and your job is to identify what each part *does*, not to memorize what it *says*. The student who reads for the words drowns in detail and forgets the paragraph the moment it ends. The student who reads for the skeleton walks away with a five-word map and answers the question in seconds. That map is the whole game.

**Mental model.** Every argument and every passage is built from exactly two kinds of sentences: the **claim** (what the author wants you to believe) and the **support** (the reasons, evidence, and examples propping it up). Active reading is the act of sorting every sentence into one of those two buckets *as you go*. You are not a sponge soaking up text. You are a sorter, asking one question at each sentence: **is this the point, or is this in service of the point?**

So read with a running interrogation. At each sentence, ask: *What is this doing here?* Stating the main claim? Backing it up? Raising an objection? Conceding a point so it can knock it down later? You are tagging function, and the text tells you the function out loud through **structure words**. These are the load-bearing beams. Learn what each one signals and you read the argument's blueprint instead of its bricks.

| Signal phrase | What it's doing |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, hence, so | conclusion — the claim lands here; tag it and stop hunting |
| because, since, given that | support — this reason is feeding a claim |
| for example, for instance, consider | illustration — skim it, don't memorize it |
| however, but, yet, nevertheless | pivot — the author changes direction; the real point usually follows |
| although, while, granted, admittedly | concession — author gives ground before countering it |
| moreover, furthermore, in addition | more of the same — stacking support, no new direction |

When you hit **"however,"** your pen should twitch: a pivot means the sentence before it is about to be outranked. When you hit **"therefore,"** you have just found the conclusion. These words are free directions. Passive readers read past them; active readers steer by them.

**Worked example.** Take this stimulus: *"Many retailers assume that loyalty programs increase profits. A national chain found that members spent 30 percent more per visit than non-members. However, the chain also discovered that members would have shopped there anyway, and the discounts offered through the program erased most of the added revenue. The program therefore did little to improve the chain's bottom line."* Map function, sentence by sentence: (1) a **common assumption** the author will challenge; (2) **support** for it — the 30-percent stat; (3) **"however"** — the pivot, where the real argument begins, plus two pieces of counter-evidence; (4) **"therefore"** — the author's actual conclusion. Your skeleton: *assumption stated, evidence for it, pivot, conclusion = program didn't help profits.* You now know the argument hinges on whether members were already loyal. You did not memorize "30 percent" — you tagged it as disposable support and moved on.

**Trap to watch.** The detail-absorption reflex. Students highlight every number, every name, every clause, and end with a yellow page that maps nothing, because if everything is important, nothing is. Highlighting and re-reading *feel* like work, but they are passive — you are recording the text, not processing it. The fix is to mark **function, not facts**: circle the "however," bracket the conclusion, leave the examples bare. You can always go back for a detail. The question will tell you which one you need.

This is why active reading underlies both halves of Verbal. In Critical Reasoning, you cannot strengthen, weaken, or find an assumption until you have separated the conclusion from its support — that separation *is* the active read. In Reading Comprehension, the questions reward you for knowing **where** things live and **why** a paragraph exists, not for reciting its contents. Map the structure and the passage becomes an index you can search.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: which two structure words flag a conclusion, and which one flags a pivot? (Conclusion: *therefore, thus*. Pivot: *however, but, yet*.) Pulling this from memory now is what burns it in — re-reading the table would feel productive and teach you nothing, because recognition is not retrieval.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does sorting each sentence into "claim" or "support" *as you read* make the question easier than reading for full comprehension first? Answer in one sentence, in your own words — articulating the mechanism is what converts a tip into a habit you actually deploy under time pressure.

Read for the skeleton. The words are scaffolding; the structure is the building.

## @argument-anatomy

Every GMAT argument is built from the same three parts. Learn to see them and most of Verbal stops feeling like reading and starts feeling like disassembly. Your job on Critical Reasoning — and on the author-argument questions buried in Reading Comprehension — is to take any chunk of prose apart into **evidence**, **conclusion**, and the **gap** between them, fast, every time, before you even glance at the answer choices.

**Mental model.** An argument is a bridge. The **premises** are the ground on each side — facts the author hands you. The **conclusion** is the far bank the author wants you to reach. The **assumption** is the bridge itself: the unstated thing that must hold for the evidence to carry you across. The GMAT loves to attack the bridge, because it's the only part the author didn't write down. Find the bridge and you've found where every question lives.

Start by separating evidence from conclusion. Students confuse these constantly because both are just declarative sentences sitting next to each other. Two tools fix this.

First, the **why test**. The conclusion is the claim the rest of the argument exists to support. So ask of each sentence: *why should I believe this?* The conclusion is the sentence the others answer; the premises are the ones doing the answering. Equivalently, drop the word **because** between two statements — whichever direction reads naturally tells you which is evidence.

**Worked example.** "TrailBrew should open its third café downtown. Foot traffic in the downtown core has risen 40 percent since the new transit hub opened." Try it both ways. "TrailBrew should open downtown *because* foot traffic rose 40 percent" reads fine. "Foot traffic rose 40 percent *because* TrailBrew should open downtown" is nonsense. So the foot-traffic sentence is the **premise**, and "should open downtown" is the **conclusion**. The why test resolved it in one pass.

Second, **conclusion signal words**. Authors often flag the conclusion explicitly, and learning the markers turns identification into recognition instead of reasoning.

| Signal phrase | What it marks |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently | conclusion follows |
| clearly, evidently, it follows that | conclusion follows |
| because, since, for, given that, as | premise follows |
| after all, in light of the fact that | premise follows |

One caution: not every conclusion is signaled, and "since" can mark *time* rather than *reason*. Treat signal words as a fast first guess, then confirm with the why test.

Now the part the test actually cares about: the **gap**. Once you have evidence and conclusion, the assumption is whatever unstated claim has to be true to get from one to the other. In TrailBrew's case, the leap from "more foot traffic" to "should open here" quietly assumes that this traffic is the kind that buys coffee, that no equivalent traffic gain is happening near the existing cafés, and that a competitor hasn't already saturated the corner. None of that was stated. Each is a place the GMAT can hand you a fact that breaks the bridge.

**Trap to watch.** Students treat the conclusion as another fact to be analyzed, instead of as the thing being defended. The moment you start arguing about whether the conclusion is *true*, you've left the task. The GMAT never asks you to agree with the author. It asks you to find what the author needs, assumed, or overlooked. Stay on the bridge, not on the far bank.

> **Recall check.** Without looking back, name the three parts of every argument and the one that's always unstated. (Premise, conclusion, assumption — the assumption is unstated.) Pulling this cold now wires it in far better than re-reading the table; under time pressure you need to retrieve it, not recognize it on the page.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does dropping "because" reliably separate premise from conclusion? Say the mechanism in one sentence. (Because "because" demands that a reason precede a claim, so only the evidence-then-conclusion ordering produces a sentence that makes logical sense.)

This anatomy is the shared engine under most of Verbal. In Reading Comprehension, when a question asks why the author mentions a study or what a paragraph's main point is, you're locating premise versus conclusion inside a passage. In Critical Reasoning, the gap *is* the question.

The CR chapters take this primer and drill it into **nine question-type templates** — Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Evaluate, Flaw, Inference, Boldface, Paradox, and Method of Reasoning. Every one is just a different operation performed on the same bridge: name the assumption, reinforce it, break it, test it, or describe it. Master the anatomy here, and those nine stop being separate skills and become nine moves on one familiar structure.

**Worked example.** Read this with the anatomy lens: "Sales of our premium plan rose after we added live chat support; therefore, live chat is driving the upgrades." The conclusion is *live chat causes upgrades*; the evidence is *upgrades rose after chat launched*; the gap is *nothing else changed at the same time*. You memorized no detail — you tagged three roles, and the soft spot (a coinciding price promotion, a new feature, a seasonal bump) is already visible. That is the whole Verbal instinct in miniature: roles first, then the question.

## @summary

You've now built the foundation. Before you touch a single Critical Reasoning argument or Reading Comprehension passage, lock in the operating system this whole chapter installed: GMAT Focus Verbal is a closed, learnable system, and you win it by running the same disciplined moves every time — not by reading harder or reacting faster.

**Mental model.** GMAT Focus Verbal is two question types and nothing else: **Critical Reasoning (CR)** and **Reading Comprehension (RC)**. Sentence Correction is gone. So every Verbal point you earn comes from one skill — **reading an argument or passage for its structure and function, then answering a specific, pre-categorized question type about it.** You are not being tested on whether you understood every word. You are being tested on whether you can locate the **logical skeleton** and reason about it under time pressure. Treat each question as a pattern to be matched, not a fresh puzzle to be solved from scratch.

Here are the foundational moves, all on one screen:

- **Scope the test.** CR + RC only. Roughly **45 minutes for ~23 questions**, so about **2 minutes per question** — generous enough to think, tight enough that re-reading whole passages will sink you.
- **Read for structure and function, not detail.** As you read, your running question is "what is this sentence *doing*?" — stating a claim, supporting it, objecting, giving background — not "what does this sentence *say*?" Details are lookup-able; structure is what the questions hinge on.
- **Find the argument anatomy.** Every CR stimulus, and most RC paragraphs, reduce to **premise(s) → conclusion**, joined by an **assumption** that bridges the **gap** between them. Name the conclusion and the gap, and you can predict most answers before reading the choices.
- **Read actively, not passively.** Anticipate, paraphrase, react. Active reading is the master habit that makes the other three fast instead of laborious.

Use this decoder to convert what you read into what to do:

| What you're reading | Your job in that moment |
|---|---|
| A CR stimulus | Find the **conclusion** first, then the **premises**, then the **gap** between them |
| An RC paragraph | Tag its **function** (claim? evidence? counterpoint? example?), not its details |
| A signal word: *therefore, thus, hence* | What follows is the **conclusion** — mark it |
| A signal word: *because, since, given that* | What follows is a **premise** — support, not the point |
| A signal word: *but, however, yet, although* | A **pivot** — the author's real position usually lives after it |
| A question stem | Classify the **type** before reading choices, so you know what a right answer must look like |

**Worked example.** "A regional grocery chain found that customers enrolled in its loyalty program spend 30% more per visit than non-members. The chain concludes that expanding the program will raise overall revenue." Run the moves. The **conclusion** is "expanding the program will raise revenue." The **premise** is the 30% spending gap. The **gap**? The argument assumes membership *causes* higher spending — when it's at least as likely that already-high spenders are the ones who bother to enroll. You found the vulnerability in fifteen seconds, before seeing one answer choice, because you read for structure. That is the entire game.

**Trap to watch.** The seductive failure mode is reading for **content** — getting absorbed in the grocery chain, the percentages, the industry — and arriving at the question with a vague "I get it" feeling but no map of the logic. When the choices then attack the *assumption* you never isolated, every option looks plausible. Slow reading for detail feels productive and is the single biggest time sink on Verbal. Structure first, always.

> **Recall check.** Without looking up, name the two question types on GMAT Focus Verbal and the three parts of argument anatomy. (CR and RC; premise, conclusion, gap/assumption.) Pulling this from memory now — instead of re-skimming the chapter — is what wires it in so it's available under test pressure.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does identifying the conclusion *first*, before the premises, make a CR question faster to answer? (Because the conclusion defines what the premises are supposed to prove, so once you have it you can immediately test whether the support actually gets there — and spot the gap an answer will exploit.)

You now have the mindset: closed system, two types, structure over detail, anatomy under everything, active reading driving it all. Next, you'll make the CR half mechanical. Turn to the **CR Core Logic** chapter, where you'll drill premise-conclusion-gap until naming the anatomy of any argument becomes reflex.
