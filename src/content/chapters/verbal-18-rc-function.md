## @rc-function

A Function question does not ask what a sentence *says*. It asks what the author is **doing** by putting it there. You answer with a **role** — support, counterexample, concession, illustration, transition — read off the passage map you built while reading. Get the role right and the literal content barely matters. The skill being tested is not comprehension of a single line but a grasp of how the parts of an argument fit together.

**Mental model.** Treat the passage as a machine and every detail as a part with a job. The question hands you one part and asks, "What does this do for the whole?" Your answer names the *job*, not the part. A detail can illustrate a claim, rebut a rival view, concede a limit, set a baseline, or pivot the argument — but it is always working *for the author's larger point*. Two questions force the right altitude: *what came right before this?* and *what is the author trying to convince me of overall?* The function bridges those two. (Function is the mirror of Attitude: Function asks what a piece *does*; Attitude asks how the author *feels*.)

Why does this type reward structure-readers so heavily? Because the answer was already decided while you read. If you tracked *purpose* — "paragraph 1 sets up a puzzle, paragraph 2 gives the old explanation, paragraph 3 knocks it down" — then when the stem points at a sentence in paragraph 3 you already know its job is part of the knockdown. Students who read for *content* ("paragraph 3 is about enzymes") must reconstruct the logic under time pressure, and that is where decoys win.

> **Recall check.** What two questions do you ask to fix a detail's function? (What came *immediately before* it, and what is the author's *overall point* — the function is the bridge between them. Retrieving these now wires them as a reflex; re-reading the rule only makes it feel familiar.)

**The method, in five steps.** Memorize this sequence and run it the same way every time:

1. **Locate** the cited element and re-read it *plus the sentence before and after*. Function is contextual — the role only appears in relation to its neighbors. Never judge a detail from the line alone.
2. **Scan for a signal word** at the seam: *however, in contrast, for example, admittedly, therefore, to be sure*. The author usually labels the transition for you; the signal word is half the answer.
3. **Name the role in your own words** before looking at choices: "this gives an example of the prior claim," "this raises an objection the author will knock down." Commit to a **verb**.
4. **Map your verb to the passage's logic.** Is the surrounding text agreeing, contrasting, conceding, or building? Contrast words mean the detail sets up or fills a contrast; concession words mean the author is granting a point before pushing back.
5. **Match the choice that describes DOING, not SAYING.** Eliminate any choice that merely paraphrases the content, and for your finalist, point to the exact sentence whose role it names. Run all five even on questions that feel obvious — those are exactly where a confident wrong instinct does the most damage.

**The "role, not content" test.** A correct function answer describes the author's *action*; a wrong one re-describes the text. "Describes a business practice" restates content. "Illustrates a contrast with more effective approaches" names an action. The gut check: "Could I have written this choice from the single cited sentence, with the rest of the passage blacked out?" If yes, it is content, not function — and paraphrase is never the function answer.

Here is the menu of roles to recognize. Learn the left column as triggers; the right column is the verb you reach for:

| Stem signal / context | Likely function |
|---|---|
| Detail follows a general claim | Provide an **example** / illustrate the claim |
| Detail sits beside *however, in contrast, while* | Illustrate or set up a **contrast** |
| Detail names a view the author then criticizes | Introduce a **counterargument** to rebut |
| Detail adds data, an analogy, a study | **Strengthen** the main claim |
| Detail says *to be sure*, *admittedly*, *although* | **Concede / qualify** a limit or exception |
| Detail bridges two claims | **Transition** between ideas |
| Detail gives a standard or starting figure | Establish a **baseline** for comparison |
| Detail opens with a question or surprising fact | **Set up the problem** the passage will address |

**Worked example (straightforward).** A passage on subscription businesses argues that *genuine* switching costs retain customers better than tricks. In paragraph 3 the author notes that some firms use "dark patterns" to block cancellation. Stem: "The author mentions dark patterns in order to..."

- A) "Describe a common business practice." Pure content, ignores the contrastive use. **Wrong.**
- B) "Illustrate a response to the retention problem that contrasts with more effective approaches." **Correct** — it names the *action* (a contrast against "genuine switching costs").
- C) "Define a technical term." The author isn't teaching vocabulary. **Wrong.**
- D) "Argue that dark patterns are illegal." No legal claim in the passage. **Wrong** (out-of-passage).

Content alone could not separate A from B — only the *role* could.

**Worked example (example/illustration).** A passage claims that early-stage startups overvalue features and undervalue distribution. The next sentence reads: "Consider a messaging app that shipped twelve new features in a quarter yet never cracked 1,000 users." Stem: "The reference to the messaging app primarily serves to..." The signal is the imperative "Consider" plus a specific case following a general claim — the classic **example** trigger. Correct answer: "illustrate the claim that startups misallocate effort toward features over distribution." Tempting wrongs: "introduce a new argument about user growth" (it introduces nothing new — it instantiates the existing claim) and "describe the typical feature roadmap of a messaging app" (pure content). The verb is *illustrate*, and the prior sentence is the claim it illustrates — cite both.

**Worked example (concession).** A passage claims a new battery chemistry is promising, *but* paragraph 2 opens, "Admittedly, the cells degrade after 800 cycles, far short of commercial lithium-ion." Stem: "The reference to the 800-cycle figure primarily serves to..." The signal word *admittedly* flags a **concession**. The right answer reads like "acknowledge a limitation of the technology the author otherwise endorses" — not "prove the chemistry is a failure" (overstates the author's stance) and not "explain how battery degradation works" (pure content). The tell: the concession is bounded — it is granted *so that* the main claim survives, not so it collapses.

> **Recall check.** A detail appears right after "to be sure." What function is it most likely performing? (A **concession** — granting a limit or opposing point before the author returns to the main claim.) Producing "concession" from the cue *to be sure* builds the trigger; skimming the table again does not.

**Worked example (transition / structure).** Paragraph 1 catalogues the failures of a 1970s urban-renewal model; paragraph 2 begins: "A different approach, pioneered in smaller cities, reversed several of these assumptions." Stem: "The first sentence of paragraph 2 primarily functions to..." The right answer is something like "**signal a shift** from the criticized model to the alternative the passage will endorse." Wrong flavors: "summarize the failures described earlier" (that's paragraph 1's job — the **wrong-paragraph** decoy), and "introduce the passage's main thesis" (the thesis is the alternative *developed across* paragraph 2, not announced in one bridging sentence). The verb is *transition / pivot*, and the cue "A different approach" hands it to you.

**Worked example (hard — nested role).** A passage argues a famine was caused by policy, not drought. Paragraph 3 reads: "Defenders of the regime point to rainfall data showing the driest year in a century." Stem: "The author refers to the rainfall data in order to..." This is hard because the detail *supports the opposing side*, yet sits in a passage arguing the other way. The function is not "support the drought explanation" — the author doesn't endorse it. The author is **presenting the opposing argument's strongest evidence**, almost always to then rebut or limit it (watch for the *however* in the next sentence). Correct answer: "introduce the evidence cited by those who attribute the famine to drought, which the author will challenge." The trap "demonstrate that drought was the primary cause" puts the *opponent's* claim in the *author's* mouth — a viewpoint-attribution error. Always ask: *whose* claim is this detail serving?

> **Recall check.** When a detail belongs to a view the author opposes, what is the author usually about to do with it — and what word often signals that? (Rebut or limit it, frequently flagged by *however*, *yet*, or *but* in the next sentence. Producing that move from memory is what stops you picking the "author endorses this" decoy under time pressure.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does naming the role in your own words *before* reading the choices make you faster and more accurate? (Because you walk in with a committed verb to match against, so paraphrase, wrong-paragraph, and viewpoint-attribution traps can't quietly reframe the question — you're matching to your prediction, not shopping the options for the most familiar wording.)

**Trap to watch.** The **wrong-paragraph** trap. When the stem asks for the role of *paragraph 3*, a choice describing paragraph 1's job — "introduces the central thesis" — sits right there looking plausible, because it's a true statement about the passage, just about the wrong piece. The thesis was introduced earlier; paragraph 3 might *support*, *qualify*, or *apply* it. Always re-read the exact element named before judging choices: a ten-second targeted re-read kills a minute of confusion.

**Trap to watch.** Picking the choice with the most matching vocabulary. Function answers are deliberately worded in abstract role-language; the choice that echoes the passage's nouns is often the paraphrase decoy planted to catch the keyword-matcher. Let the *verb* decide, not the noun overlap.

**Trap to watch.** **Viewpoint misattribution.** When a detail belongs to a view the author *opposes* or merely *reports*, decoys phrase the function as if the author endorses it. A detail can do a job for the author — introducing an opponent's evidence so it can be rebutted — without the author agreeing with its content. Track who owns each claim while you read.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Content instead of action** — choosing the option that accurately restates what the detail *says* rather than what it *does*. The accurate paraphrase is the most common wrong answer on this type.
- **Judging the line in isolation** — skipping the before/after read, so the contrast or concession that defines the role never gets seen.
- **Mis-sizing the author's commitment** — turning a hedged concession into a refutation, or a reported opposing view into the author's own thesis.
- **Letting noun overlap pick the answer** — choosing the option that reuses the passage's vocabulary instead of the one with the correct verb.

**The discipline that ties it together: cite the line.** For any function choice you like, point to the sentence whose *role* it describes and the neighbor that fixes that role. If you cannot put your finger on where the author performs that action, the choice names a job the text never gave the detail — and it is wrong. This habit settles most close two-answer splits, because the paraphrase decoy has no neighbor to point at.

**Recap.**

- Function asks what a detail *does* for the author's larger point, not what it *says* — answer with a **verb/role**, never a paraphrase.
- Run the fixed sequence: **locate (with neighbors) → scan the signal word → predict the role → map it to the passage logic → match DOING, not SAYING.**
- Signal words do most of the work: *however/in contrast* = contrast, *for example/consider* = illustration, *admittedly/to be sure* = concession, *therefore* = conclusion, "a different approach" = transition.
- Beware the three decoys: accurate-paraphrase, wrong-paragraph, and viewpoint-misattribution.
- Confirm every finalist by citing the exact sentence whose role it names; if you can't point to it, drop it.

## @function-is-your-purpose-tag

Step back and notice what a Function question really is: it hands you back, as a multiple-choice problem, the exact thing the reading process already had you do. While reading you tag each paragraph with its **function** and keep a one-line-per-paragraph **skeleton** — "P1 sets up the puzzle, P2 gives the old view, P3 knocks it down." A Function stem just points at one piece of that skeleton and asks, "What's the tag?" If you built the map, the answer is already written; you're not solving, you're *reading off* your own notes.

**Mental model.** Function = your purpose tag, phrased as a question. The reading method asks "why is this paragraph here?" The Function question asks "why is this sentence here?" — the same move at a finer grain. So answer the way you tagged: with the *role* it plays for the author's larger point (supports, objects, qualifies, illustrates, concludes), never with the topic it happens to be about. The reflex from the reading chapter applies unchanged: if your answer doesn't contain a verb, it's a topic label, not a function — and topic labels are decoys here.

**Worked example.** Three-line skeleton on a passage about open-plan offices. P1: "Designers championed open floors as engines of spontaneous collaboration." Tag: *introduces the standard rationale.* P2 opens "However, a 2021 badge-tracking study found face-to-face contact actually *fell* by a third after walls came down." Tag: *author's pivot — evidence against the rationale.* P3: "To be sure, the same firms reported modest gains in scheduled cross-team meetings." Tag: *concession, bounded.* Now the stem: "The author mentions the badge-tracking study in order to —." You don't re-read; you glance at your P2 tag, which already says *evidence against the rationale*. The credited answer is "present evidence undercutting the case for open floors," and you can cite the *however* that owns it. The tag was the answer the whole time.

**Trap to watch.** The choice that describes what the line **says** instead of what it **does**. Here that's "report findings from a 2021 study of workplace contact" — accurate, on-topic, and dead wrong, because it restates content rather than naming the job. It's seductive precisely because its nouns echo the passage. Don't track point of view loosely either: if the cited line belongs to a reported voice ("designers championed"), don't hand its content to the author. And don't obsess over the study's details — you needed its *role*, not its numbers. This is efficiency over speed: the map you built on the first read is what spares you a frantic re-read now.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why is a Function question "already answered" the moment your skeleton is built? (Because the skeleton's per-line tag *is* the function — the stem only re-asks "what's the tag for this piece?", so a complete map turns the question from a search into a lookup.)

Then close it the way you close every RC question: by **elimination**. Predict the role from your tag, then hunt the four wrongs — the content-paraphrase, the wrong-paragraph tag, the misattributed voice — and take the survivor. Don't fall in love with the choice that sounds most like the passage; that's usually the bait. Find four that name a job the text never gave the line, and the verb that's left is your answer.
