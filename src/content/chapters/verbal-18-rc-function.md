## @rc-function

A Function question does not ask what a sentence *says*. It asks what the author is **doing** by putting it there. You answer with a **role** — support, counterexample, concession, illustration, transition — read off the passage map you built while reading. Get the role right and the literal content of the detail barely matters.

**Mental model.** Treat the passage as a machine and every detail as a part with a job. The question hands you one part and asks, "What does this do for the whole?" Your answer names the *job*, not the part. A detail can illustrate a claim, rebut a rival view, concede a limit, set a baseline, or pivot the argument — but it is always working *for the author's larger point*. (Function is the mirror of Attitude: Function asks what a piece *does*; Attitude asks how the author *feels*. This chapter is purely about the doing.)

**The method, in four steps.**

- **Locate** the cited element and re-read it *plus the sentence before and after*. Function is contextual — the role only appears in relation to its neighbors.
- **Name the role in your own words** before looking at choices: "this gives an example of the prior claim," "this raises an objection the author will knock down." Commit to a verb.
- **Map your verb to the passage's logic.** Is the surrounding text agreeing, contrasting, conceding, or building? Contrast words (*however, in contrast, while, yet*) almost always mean the detail sets up or fills a contrast.
- **Match the choice that describes DOING, not SAYING.** Eliminate any choice that merely paraphrases the content.

**The "role, not content" test.** A correct function answer describes the author's *action*; a wrong one re-describes the text. "Describes a business practice" restates content. "Illustrates a contrast with more effective approaches" names an action. If a choice could be true even with no surrounding argument, it is probably just paraphrase.

Here is the menu of roles to recognize:

| Stem signal / context | Likely function |
|---|---|
| Detail follows a general claim | Provide an **example** / illustrate the claim |
| Detail sits beside *however, in contrast, while* | Illustrate or set up a **contrast** |
| Detail names a view the author then criticizes | Introduce a **counterargument** to rebut |
| Detail adds data, an analogy, a study | **Strengthen** the main claim |
| Detail says *to be sure*, *admittedly*, *although* | **Concede / qualify** a limit or exception |
| Detail bridges two claims | **Transition** between ideas |
| Detail gives a standard or starting figure | Establish a **baseline** for comparison |

**Worked example.** A passage on subscription businesses argues that *genuine* switching costs retain customers better than tricks. In paragraph 3 the author notes that some firms use "dark patterns" to block cancellation. Stem: "The author mentions dark patterns in order to..."

- A) "Describe a common business practice." Too neutral — pure content, ignores the author's contrastive use. **Wrong.**
- B) "Illustrate a response to the retention problem that contrasts with more effective approaches." **Correct** — it names the *action* (illustrating a contrast against "genuine switching costs").
- C) "Define a technical term." The author isn't teaching vocabulary. **Wrong.**
- D) "Argue that dark patterns are illegal." The passage makes no legal claim. **Wrong** (out-of-passage).

Notice how the contrast logic in the surrounding text drove you straight to choice B.

**Worked example.** A passage claims a new battery chemistry is promising, *but* paragraph 2 opens, "Admittedly, the cells degrade after 800 cycles, far short of commercial lithium-ion." Stem: "The reference to the 800-cycle figure primarily serves to..." The signal word *admittedly* flags a **concession**. The right answer reads like "acknowledge a limitation of the technology the author otherwise endorses" — not "prove the chemistry is a failure" (overstates) and not "explain how battery degradation works" (pure content). The author concedes a weakness to look balanced before pressing the larger optimistic case.

**Trap to watch.** The **wrong-paragraph** trap. When the stem asks for the role of *paragraph 3*, a choice describing paragraph 1's job — "introduces the central thesis" — will sit right there looking plausible. The thesis was introduced earlier; paragraph 3 might *support* or *qualify* it. Always re-read the exact element named before judging choices. A ten-second targeted re-read kills a minute of confusion.

**Trap to watch.** Picking the choice with the most matching vocabulary. Function answers are deliberately worded in abstract role-language; the choice that echoes the passage's nouns is often the paraphrase decoy. Let the *verb* decide, not the noun overlap.

> **Recall check.** A detail appears right after the phrase "to be sure." What function is it most likely performing? (A concession — conceding a limit or opposing point before the author returns to the main claim.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does naming the role in your own words *before* reading the choices make you faster and more accurate? (Because you walk in with a committed verb to match against, so paraphrase decoys and wrong-paragraph traps can't quietly reframe the question for you.)

**The discipline that ties it together: cite the line.** For any function choice you like, point to the sentence whose *role* it describes and the neighbor that fixes that role. If you cannot put your finger on where in the passage the author performs that action, the choice is describing a job the text never gave the detail — and it is wrong.
