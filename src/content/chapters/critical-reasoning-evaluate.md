## @two-way-test

Evaluate questions ask: "Which of the following would be most useful to know when evaluating this argument?" You're asked to identify the piece of information that would most decisively help you judge whether the argument works.

**Mental model.** A good evaluate answer is a *light switch* for the argument: flip it one way and the argument looks strong, flip it the other and it looks broken. A bad answer is a dimmer that barely changes the room. You're hunting for the switch.

**The template.** The answer is a question (or a piece of information framed as a question) whose answer would either strongly support or strongly undermine the argument depending on which way it goes.

**The "two-way test" — the technique that cracks every evaluate question.**

1. For each answer, imagine getting the information phrased as a "yes" answer and as a "no" answer.
2. Ask: "Does the argument strengthen a lot in one case and weaken a lot in the other?"
3. If yes — that's the one. You're evaluating something that actually *matters*.
4. If the argument doesn't change much either way — irrelevant, eliminate.

**Example.** Argument: "Company Y switched to quarterly reviews; turnover dropped from 22% to 14%. Therefore the new review system caused the drop."

Candidate answers:

- "What was the average salary at Company Y during the same period?"
- "Did other companies in the industry, without switching review systems, experience similar turnover changes?"
- "How many employees worked at Company Y?"

Apply the two-way test:

- Salary: if salary went up a lot, that could explain the turnover drop (weakens original). If unchanged, original argument survives. Relevant — but weaker effect.
- Other companies: if others saw similar drops without the switch, the switch isn't necessarily the cause (weakens). If others saw no change or rising turnover, the switch's effect looks real (strengthens). **BIG swing in both directions** — this is the best evaluator.
- Headcount: small or large, doesn't bear on whether the review system caused the change. Irrelevant.

**The question stem decoder:**

| Stem wording | Type |
|---|---|
| "…most useful to know in evaluating the argument…" | Evaluate |
| "…most helpful to establish whether the conclusion is warranted…" | Evaluate |
| "…the most useful information would be whether…" | Evaluate |

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the two-way test work? If you can explain "because evaluating an argument means knowing whether it's likely true or false, and the best question is one whose answer tells us decisively — which requires the answer to produce a big swing in either direction," you've internalized the structure.

## @evaluate-connections

Evaluate questions reuse skills you already have. Seeing the connection lets you predict the answer instead of testing all five.

**Evaluate = assumption + impact.** Evaluate questions are essentially asking "which assumption would it be most useful to verify?" The correct answer often tracks the argument's central assumption — the one whose truth or falsity most affects the conclusion.

**The link back to strengthen/weaken.** An evaluate answer is a question whose "yes" answer *strengthens* the argument and whose "no" answer *weakens* it (or vice versa). If you remember the alternative-cause and control-case patterns, you already know what to look for: the decisive question almost always probes an alternative cause, a relevant comparison group, or a reverse-causation check.

**The "too specific" trap.** Evaluate answers sometimes ask about very specific details (exact dates, exact dollar amounts) that don't bear on the central claim. A good evaluate question asks about something *structural* — an alternative cause, a relevant comparison group, a reverse-causation check — not a precise number.

**Micro-drill.** Argument: "After the city added streetlights to Elm Park, nighttime crime there fell 20%. The lights made the park safer." For each candidate, apply the two-way test and mark decisive or irrelevant. 60 seconds:

1. "Did nighttime crime fall by a similar amount in comparable parks that got no new lights?" → ___
2. "Exactly how many streetlights were installed?" → ___
3. "Were the lights installed by the city's own crews or by a contractor?" → ___

Answers: (1) **Decisive** — "yes, other parks fell too" guts the claim (citywide trend); "no, only Elm fell" strengthens it. Big two-way swing, and it's the alternative-cause probe. (2) **Irrelevant** — the count doesn't change whether lights caused the drop. (3) **Irrelevant** — who installed them has no bearing on the effect. Notice the decisive answer is the *structural* comparison-group question; the other two are "too specific" distractors.

## @summary

Evaluate asks for the single question whose answer would most change your verdict. Find it with the two-way test, and recognize that the decisive question almost always probes the same gap a weakener would attack.

**The process.**

1. Do the foundations move — conclusion, evidence, gap.
2. For each answer, imagine the "yes" and the "no."
3. Keep the one that swings the argument hard in both directions; drop anything that barely moves it or asks for a precise detail.
4. Sanity check: the winner usually probes an alternative cause or a comparison group.

**What to do next.** Drill the problem sets below. When you miss one, write out the "yes" and "no" outcomes for both the answer you picked and the correct one — seeing the swing side by side is what trains the instinct.
