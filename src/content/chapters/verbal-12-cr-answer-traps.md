## @cr-answer-traps

The question-type sections above give you the strategy for *what to look for*. This section gives you the six patterns that show up in *wrong answers* across every question type. Memorize these and your elimination speed roughly doubles — most wrong answers fall into one of these buckets, and recognizing the pattern lets you reject the choice in two seconds rather than re-evaluating the whole argument.

**Trap 1: Out-of-scope.**

The answer introduces something the argument never addresses — a different population, a different time period, a different metric, a different topic entirely. The answer might be a true statement about the world; it just doesn't bear on the specific argument.

*Example.* Argument: "Company X switched to quarterly reviews; turnover dropped." Out-of-scope answer: "Companies with quarterly reviews are also more likely to offer parental leave." The answer talks about a different policy — it has nothing to do with the causal claim about reviews and turnover.

The cure: **before reading any answer, name the gap in one sentence**. ("Does the policy switch explain the turnover drop, or could something else?") Then ask of each answer: "does this touch *that* gap?" If no — eliminate, regardless of how interesting the statement sounds.

**Trap 2: Half-right, half-wrong.**

The first clause of the answer fits the gap perfectly; the second clause introduces a fact that's irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope. Students under time pressure read the first clause, recognize a match, and bubble — without finishing the sentence.

*Example.* Strengthen-the-argument question. Answer reads: "Studies show the new review system improves retention, *and the system is widely used in Fortune 500 companies*." The first clause strengthens; the second clause is irrelevant ("widely used" doesn't bear on whether it works). The right answer would be a pure strengthener with no irrelevant tail.

The cure: **read every answer to its period before deciding**. If any portion of the answer is irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope, the whole answer is wrong. CR doesn't grade on a partial-credit scale.

**Trap 3: Number/percent confusion.**

The argument cites a percentage; the right-answer candidates flip to absolute counts (or vice versa). Often the "weakener" or "strengthener" depends on which metric the argument was making a claim about.

*Example.* Argument: "Region A has a 5% youth unemployment rate; Region B has 8%. Therefore Region A's economy is healthier for young workers." A "weakener" that says "Region B's labor force is twice as large, so Region B employs more young people overall" attacks the wrong claim — the argument was about *rates*, not absolute counts. A correct weakener would attack the rate-to-health link directly.

The cure: **before evaluating answers, identify which metric the conclusion rests on**. Argument about a percentage → answers about absolute counts are usually irrelevant. Argument about absolute counts → answers about percentages are usually irrelevant. The two flip every test session.

**Trap 4: Time-frame shift.**

The argument is about a specific period (past results, future predictions, current state). The wrong answer references a different time frame — "in the long run," "historically," "next decade" — and either strengthens or weakens a claim about that different period, not the one the argument made.

*Example.* Argument: "Last quarter's sales rose 12% after the rebrand. Therefore the rebrand drove sales." A wrong "weakener": "Brand-loyal customers will likely fatigue from the new image within 18 months." That weakens a *future* claim the argument didn't make. The argument was only about last quarter.

The cure: **note the time frame of the conclusion explicitly**. If the argument says "last quarter," reject answers that talk about future quarters, past years, or steady-state trends.

**Trap 5: The cited view trap.**

Some arguments cite an opposing view to refute it. Question: "Identify the author's main conclusion." Student picks the view the author *attacked*, not the view the author *endorsed*.

*Example.* Passage: "Critics claim the new tax will reduce small-business hiring. But payroll data from three similar reforms in the EU show no such effect. The tax should therefore not be expected to harm hiring." Wrong "main conclusion" pick: "The new tax will reduce small-business hiring." That's the *cited* view. The author's actual conclusion is the third sentence — that the tax will *not* harm hiring.

The cure: **after reading the passage, ask 'whose claim is this?' for each sentence**. The author's view is what the passage builds toward; the cited view is what the passage argues against. Words like "however," "but," "critics claim," "some argue" usually signal a cited view about to be refuted.

**Trap 6: Extreme language.**

Answers containing "always," "never," "all," "none," "only," "must," or "impossible" are usually wrong — because the right CR answer typically establishes a *plausibility shift*, not a universal truth. The argument needs something modest enough to be defensible; extreme statements are easy to refute with a single counterexample.

This is especially true on **assumption** questions (the existing section flags this): the right assumption is the *minimum* the argument needs. Extreme assumptions are almost always overshoot.

*Example.* Assumption question. Wrong: "No factor whatsoever, in any company anywhere in the world, has ever contributed to a turnover drop other than performance-review structure." Right: "No other major change at Company X during the same period contributed to the turnover drop." The wrong one is true-only-in-utopia; the right one is the modest claim the argument actually requires.

The cure: **when two answers compete, prefer the milder one**. Extreme language is a discrimination signal. The GMAT tests modest, defensible reasoning, not absolute truths.

**The unified meta-discipline.**

All six traps share a structure: the wrong answer is *plausibly related* to the argument but doesn't satisfy the *specific demand* of the question. The discipline that catches all of them is the same:

1. **Name the gap or the demand in one sentence** before reading answers.
2. **Read each answer to its period** — check both halves.
3. **Test the answer against the specific demand**, not against general truth or plausibility.

That three-step routine adds ~15 seconds per question and saves you from the traps that the test writers spend the most effort planting.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why are these six traps universal across CR question types rather than tied to one type? If you can say "because they're patterns about how *answer choices* are constructed — not about how *arguments* work — and the same construction tricks apply whether the question asks for a strengthener, an assumption, or an inference," you've understood that CR mastery is two layers: argument-reading (which differs per type) and answer-elimination (which doesn't). The elimination layer is where most students hemorrhage points on hard questions.
