## @cr-answer-traps

The question-type sections above give you the strategy for *what to look for*. This section gives you the six patterns that show up in *wrong answers* across every question type. Memorize these and your elimination speed roughly doubles — most wrong answers fall into one of these buckets, and recognizing the pattern lets you reject the choice in two seconds rather than re-evaluating the whole argument.

Here is why this matters more than any single question-type trick: on the hard questions, the test writers no longer hide the correct answer. They surround it with four *plausibly related* distractors, each engineered to feel right for a different reason. You don't beat those questions by re-reading the stimulus a third time — you beat them by knowing the **shape of the bait**. Think of it as a two-layer skill. The first layer, *argument-reading*, changes with the question type: a strengthen prompt wants a different mental move than an inference prompt. The second layer, *answer-elimination*, is identical across every type — the same six construction tricks recur whether you're hunting an assumption, an inference, or a flaw. Most students drill the first layer endlessly and neglect the second, which is exactly where the points hemorrhage on the 700-level set. Below is the full catalogue, with a memorizable procedure at the end.

Here is a fast decoder you can hold in your head while you scan the five choices:

| What the answer does | Likely trap | Default move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brings in a new population/metric/topic | Out-of-scope (Trap 1) | Eliminate unless it touches *your named gap* |
| Great first clause, weird second clause | Half-right, half-wrong (Trap 2) | Read to the period; one bad half kills it |
| Switches between % and raw counts | Number/percent (Trap 3) | Match the answer's metric to the conclusion's |
| Talks about a different time window | Time-frame shift (Trap 4) | Reject if it's not the conclusion's window |
| Restates a view the author *attacked* | Cited-view (Trap 5) | Ask "whose claim is this?" |
| Uses always/never/only/must | Extreme language (Trap 6) | Prefer the milder competing answer |

**Trap 1: Out-of-scope.**

The answer introduces something the argument never addresses — a different population, a different time period, a different metric, a different topic entirely. The answer might be a true statement about the world; it just doesn't bear on the specific argument.

*Example.* Argument: "Company X switched to quarterly reviews; turnover dropped." Out-of-scope answer: "Companies with quarterly reviews are also more likely to offer parental leave." The answer talks about a different policy — it has nothing to do with the causal claim about reviews and turnover.

The cure: **before reading any answer, name the gap in one sentence**. ("Does the policy switch explain the turnover drop, or could something else?") Then ask of each answer: "does this touch *that* gap?" If no — eliminate, regardless of how interesting the statement sounds.

**Worked example.** *(straightforward)* Argument: "After installing solar panels, the factory's electricity bills fell 30%. The panels are paying for themselves." Strengthen question. Choice: "Solar panel installations nationwide rose 40% last year." Walk it: the named gap is *do the panels, specifically, explain this factory's savings?* The choice talks about national adoption trends — a different population entirely. It's true, it's about solar, and it's completely irrelevant to whether *this* factory's bill drop came from the panels. Out-of-scope; eliminate in two seconds.

> **Recall check.** What single sentence should you write *before* reading any answer choice? (Answer: the gap or demand — e.g. "could something other than the panels explain the savings?") Forcing yourself to recall and write the gap from memory primes you to feel the mismatch when a distractor drifts off it — re-reading the stimulus does not build that primed filter.

**Trap 2: Half-right, half-wrong.**

The first clause of the answer fits the gap perfectly; the second clause introduces a fact that's irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope. Students under time pressure read the first clause, recognize a match, and bubble — without finishing the sentence.

*Example.* Strengthen-the-argument question. Answer reads: "Studies show the new review system improves retention, *and the system is widely used in Fortune 500 companies*." The first clause strengthens; the second clause is irrelevant ("widely used" doesn't bear on whether it works). The right answer would be a pure strengthener with no irrelevant tail.

The cure: **read every answer to its period before deciding**. If any portion of the answer is irrelevant, wrong-direction, or out-of-scope, the whole answer is wrong. CR doesn't grade on a partial-credit scale.

**Worked example.** *(medium)* Weaken question. Argument: "The new bus lane cut average commute times, so the city should add more bus lanes." Choice: "The bus lane reduced car congestion on that corridor, *but the corridor also gained two new traffic signals during the same period that slowed cars elsewhere*." Read it to the period. First clause: actually *supports* the lane working. Second clause: "slowed cars elsewhere" — wait, does that weaken? It introduces a confound, but it's about a *different* part of the network, not the corridor the commute-time claim rests on. The two halves point in opposite directions and neither cleanly weakens the specific conclusion. The half that feels like a weakener doesn't attach to the right claim. Eliminate — and notice how a hurried reader who stopped at "but" would have bubbled it.

**Trap to watch.** The most expensive version of Trap 2 is when the *bad* half comes first and the *good* half comes second, so the answer reads as wrong for a sentence and then "redeems" itself. Students who eliminated on the first clause feel clever and move on — but the GMAT also writes answers where the first clause is a throwaway and the operative, correct logic is in the tail. The lesson is symmetric: **read the whole sentence every time.** You cannot judge a CR answer from either half alone.

> **Recall check.** A choice's first clause is a flawless match for your gap. Are you done evaluating it? (Answer: no — read to the period; one irrelevant or wrong-direction clause kills the whole choice, and the operative logic sometimes lives in the *second* clause.) Pulling this rule up from memory mid-question is what overrides the bubble-on-recognition reflex that re-reading never touches.

**Trap 3: Number/percent confusion.**

The argument cites a percentage; the right-answer candidates flip to absolute counts (or vice versa). Often the "weakener" or "strengthener" depends on which metric the argument was making a claim about.

*Example.* Argument: "Region A has a 5% youth unemployment rate; Region B has 8%. Therefore Region A's economy is healthier for young workers." A "weakener" that says "Region B's labor force is twice as large, so Region B employs more young people overall" attacks the wrong claim — the argument was about *rates*, not absolute counts. A correct weakener would attack the rate-to-health link directly.

The cure: **before evaluating answers, identify which metric the conclusion rests on**. Argument about a percentage → answers about absolute counts are usually irrelevant. Argument about absolute counts → answers about percentages are usually irrelevant. The two flip every test session.

**Worked example.** *(hard)* Argument: "Last year the clinic's *rate* of post-surgical infections fell from 4% to 2%. The new sterilization protocol is working." Weaken question. Choice: "Last year the clinic performed nearly twice as many surgeries as the year before." Pause — this is the subtle one. The conclusion is about the *rate*, but a doubling in volume can be a genuine confound *if* the new high-volume surgeries were systematically lower-risk (e.g. a flood of simple outpatient procedures dilutes the rate). The trap framing wants you to think "volume is a count, conclusion is a rate, therefore irrelevant — eliminate." But here the *count* changes the *composition* underlying the rate, which is exactly the rate-to-cause link the argument needs. So this one can legitimately weaken. The discipline isn't "counts never matter to rate claims" — it's "**match the metric, and check whether the count secretly changes what the rate measures.**" That distinction is what separates a 90th-percentile elimination from a reflexive one.

> **Recall check.** When an argument's conclusion rests on a *percentage*, what is your default suspicion about an answer choice phrased in *raw counts*? (Answer: probably irrelevant — but verify it doesn't change the composition behind the rate.) Retrieving the rule *plus its exception* under self-quiz is what stops you from over-applying it on the one hard question where the count actually bites.

**Trap 4: Time-frame shift.**

The argument is about a specific period (past results, future predictions, current state). The wrong answer references a different time frame — "in the long run," "historically," "next decade" — and either strengthens or weakens a claim about that different period, not the one the argument made.

*Example.* Argument: "Last quarter's sales rose 12% after the rebrand. Therefore the rebrand drove sales." A wrong "weakener": "Brand-loyal customers will likely fatigue from the new image within 18 months." That weakens a *future* claim the argument didn't make. The argument was only about last quarter.

The cure: **note the time frame of the conclusion explicitly**. If the argument says "last quarter," reject answers that talk about future quarters, past years, or steady-state trends.

**Worked example.** *(medium)* Argument: "This spring, the museum's free-admission Fridays drew record crowds, proving free entry boosts attendance." Choice offered as a weakener: "In the museum's first decade, paid-admission years often outdrew free-admission years." Note the conclusion's window: *this spring*. The choice reaches back to "the first decade" — a different era with different exhibits, demographics, and competition. It speaks to a claim the argument never made. Time-frame shift; eliminate. Compare with a real weakener that *stays in the window*: "This spring also featured a blockbuster exhibit on free Fridays only." That one stays in-period and supplies an alternate cause — which is what a genuine weakener does.

**Trap 5: The cited view trap.**

Some arguments cite an opposing view to refute it. Question: "Identify the author's main conclusion." Student picks the view the author *attacked*, not the view the author *endorsed*.

*Example.* Passage: "Critics claim the new tax will reduce small-business hiring. But payroll data from three similar reforms in the EU show no such effect. The tax should therefore not be expected to harm hiring." Wrong "main conclusion" pick: "The new tax will reduce small-business hiring." That's the *cited* view. The author's actual conclusion is the third sentence — that the tax will *not* harm hiring.

The cure: **after reading the passage, ask 'whose claim is this?' for each sentence**. The author's view is what the passage builds toward; the cited view is what the passage argues against. Words like "however," "but," "critics claim," "some argue" usually signal a cited view about to be refuted.

**Worked example.** *(hard)* Passage: "Many economists insist that minimum-wage hikes always cost low-wage jobs. Granted, the textbook model predicts exactly that. Yet a dozen recent natural experiments found no measurable employment loss — and in several cases, modest job *gains*. The standard model, then, is missing something." Main-conclusion question. The trap-rich feature here is the concession: "Granted, the textbook model predicts exactly that" sounds like agreement, and a careless reader tags the author as pro-textbook. But "Granted… Yet…" is the classic concede-then-pivot. The cited views are sentences one and two (what economists insist, what the model predicts); the author's actual conclusion is the last sentence — *the standard model is incomplete*. Sort every sentence by owner: economists/textbook = cited, author = "the model is missing something." Choosing "minimum-wage hikes cost jobs" means you handed the author's microphone to the people he's refuting.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why are these six traps universal across CR question types rather than tied to one type? If you can say "because they're patterns about how *answer choices* are constructed — not about how *arguments* work — and the same construction tricks apply whether the question asks for a strengthener, an assumption, or an inference," you've understood that CR mastery is two layers: argument-reading (which differs per type) and answer-elimination (which doesn't). The elimination layer is where most students hemorrhage points on hard questions.

**Trap 6: Extreme language.**

Answers containing "always," "never," "all," "none," "only," "must," or "impossible" are usually wrong — because the right CR answer typically establishes a *plausibility shift*, not a universal truth. The argument needs something modest enough to be defensible; extreme statements are easy to refute with a single counterexample.

This is especially true on **assumption** questions: the right assumption is the *minimum* the argument needs. Extreme assumptions are almost always overshoot.

*Example.* Assumption question. Wrong: "No factor whatsoever, in any company anywhere in the world, has ever contributed to a turnover drop other than performance-review structure." Right: "No other major change at Company X during the same period contributed to the turnover drop." The wrong one is true-only-in-utopia; the right one is the modest claim the argument actually requires.

The cure: **when two answers compete, prefer the milder one**. Extreme language is a discrimination signal. The GMAT tests modest, defensible reasoning, not absolute truths.

**Worked example.** *(medium, with the exception)* Strengthen question down to two choices. (A): "The drug reduced symptoms in *most* trial patients." (B): "The drug reduced symptoms in *every* patient who has ever taken it, in all conditions." Reflex says prefer the milder (A) — and usually that's right. But notice *why*: (B) overshoots what a strengthener needs and is fragile to one counterexample. The genuine exception to "avoid extremes" is the **inference / must-be-true** question, where the *stimulus itself* states something absolute ("the budget was cut to zero"). There, an extreme answer can be exactly correct because it's *derived*, not assumed. The rule is therefore: **extreme language is a red flag on strengthen/weaken/assumption, but not automatically wrong on inference questions, where the correct answer is whatever the premises force.** Knowing the boundary is what keeps the heuristic from costing you points.

Here is a compact mapping from the stem you're reading to the trap most worth pre-loading before you scan the choices:

| Question stem | What the right answer must do | Trap to pre-load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Which most strengthens / weakens…" | Shift plausibility of the *named link* | Out-of-scope, half-right, extreme |
| "The argument assumes that…" | State the *minimum* required, no more | Extreme language |
| "Which must be true / can be inferred…" | Be *forced* by the premises | Extreme is OK here; out-of-scope is the killer |
| "The author's main conclusion is…" | Be the sentence the passage builds toward | Cited-view |
| Conclusion cites a rate or a count | Attack/support the *same* metric | Number/percent |

**The step-by-step procedure (memorize this).**

1. **Read the stimulus and find the conclusion.** Underline it mentally. Everything else is premise or noise.
2. **Name the gap or the demand in one sentence.** Strengthen/weaken → what's the unstated link? Assumption → what must be true? Inference → what's forced? Main conclusion → which sentence does the passage build toward?
3. **Tag the conclusion's metric and time frame.** Rate or count? This quarter, last decade, or steady state? (Pre-empts Traps 3 and 4.)
4. **Tag whose voice owns each claim.** Author vs. cited/attacked view. (Pre-empts Trap 5.)
5. **Read each answer to its period.** Both clauses. (Pre-empts Trap 2.)
6. **Test the answer against your named demand, not against general truth.** Does it touch *that* gap? (Pre-empts Trap 1.)
7. **When two survive, prefer the milder one** — unless it's an inference question, where you prefer the one the premises force. (Handles Trap 6.)

That routine adds ~15 seconds per question and saves you from the traps that the test writers spend the most effort planting. The first three steps happen *before* you look at a single answer choice; that ordering is deliberate — it builds the filter in your head while the stimulus is fresh, so the distractors hit a primed mind instead of a blank one.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Bubbling on the first matching clause.** The match feels like recognition; the rest of the sentence still has to clear. Always read to the period.
- **Treating a true statement as a relevant one.** Half of CR distractors are factually fine and argumentatively useless. "Is it true?" is the wrong question; "does it touch my gap?" is the right one.
- **Over-applying the heuristics.** "Counts are irrelevant to rate claims" and "extremes are wrong" are *defaults*, not laws. The hard questions are built precisely on the exception — check composition (Trap 3) and check question type (Trap 6).
- **Letting concessions flip the author.** "Granted… / It is true that…" sets up a pivot. Don't credit the author with the view he's about to overturn.

**Recap.**

- Six trap patterns cover most wrong CR answers; they live in the *answer-elimination* layer, which is identical across all question types.
- The traps: out-of-scope, half-right/half-wrong, number/percent, time-frame shift, cited-view, and extreme language.
- The unifying flaw is the same every time: the wrong answer is *plausibly related* to the argument but doesn't satisfy the *specific demand* of the question.
- One seven-step routine — conclusion → gap → metric/time → voice → read-to-period → test-against-demand → prefer-milder — catches all six.
- The two genuine exceptions worth memorizing: counts *can* matter when they reshape a rate, and extreme answers *can* be correct on inference questions.
