## @cr-paradox

A Paradox question hands you two facts that both come from the passage, both stated as true, and that seem like they cannot both hold at once. Your job is not to pick a side. Your job is to find the missing piece of information that makes the apparent conflict dissolve — the third fact that lets **both** stated facts stand. This is one of the most pattern-driven question types on the GMAT Focus Critical Reasoning section: once you internalize the structure, you stop reading paradoxes as confusing and start reading them as a tidy little machine with one part missing.

**Mental model.** A paradox is never a real contradiction. It is a contradiction *with a gap*. The passage gives you "A is true" and "B is true," and your instinct screams "but if A, then surely not B." That instinct rests on a hidden assumption you didn't notice you were making. The correct answer names the unstated condition that breaks that assumption, so A and B coexist peacefully. You are hunting for an **and**, never an **either/or**. (Paradox is the structural cousin of Flaw questions — both ask you to spot something "off" — but where Flaw hands you a broken argument to diagnose, Paradox hands you two clean facts to reconcile; there is no argument to attack here, only a surprise to explain.)

Because there is no argument, there is also no conclusion to weaken or strengthen — a trap worth naming up front. Students fresh off Strengthen/Weaken chapters reach for "what makes the author's point stronger," but a paradox has no author's point. It has a tension. The correct answer is not "stronger" or "weaker"; it is **explanatory**. Keep that frame and half the wrong answers fall away before you've finished reading them.

**The "explain both" test.** This is the entire game. Take your candidate answer and ask two questions in order:
- Does this explain fact 1?
- Does this explain fact 2?

If either answer is "no," eliminate it immediately. Trap answers almost always pass one test and fail the other — they are on topic, they sound like they belong, but they account for only half the puzzle and leave the other half just as mysterious as before. The discipline is to ask *both* questions every single time, even when the first answer is an enthusiastic yes. A "yes" on fact 1 is exactly what the trap is engineered to give you; it buys your confidence so you skip the second question. Don't.

**The step-by-step procedure.** Memorize this and run it on every paradox question, easy or hard:

1. **Read the stem first** and confirm the type — look for *resolve, explain, reconcile, account for, discrepancy, surprising* (see the decoder below). Note immediately whether it is a normal or an **EXCEPT** variant.
2. **State the two facts in your own words** — literally label them Fact 1 and Fact 2. If you can't name two facts, you haven't found the paradox yet; reread.
3. **Name the hidden assumption** — finish the sentence "These feel incompatible *because I'm assuming...*." This is the move most students skip, and it is the one that makes the right answer obvious.
4. **Predict the gap** — what kind of third fact would break that assumption? You don't need the exact wording, just the *shape* (e.g. "something that distinguishes the two cities," "something about measurement rather than reality").
5. **Run the explain-both test on each choice** — Fact 1? Fact 2? Eliminate on the first "no."
6. **Confirm the survivor carries both facts** before committing — say it out loud: "With this true, Fact 1 holds *and* Fact 2 holds."

Steps 2 through 4 are the part untrained students skip, and they are the part that converts a 90-second guess into a 30-second elimination. If you predict the *shape* of the answer before reading the choices, the credited answer often jumps off the page and the traps reveal themselves as one-sided. Do the prediction work before you touch the answers.

**Worked example (straightforward).** "After banning plastic bags, City A saw paper-bag use rise by only 10% — far below the 200% jump in other cities that imposed the same ban. Yet City A reported **no** increase in reusable-bag purchases." The surprise: how did City A dodge the paper surge *without* people buying reusable bags? Your hidden assumption is "the only two options are paper or newly bought reusables." A good resolution kills that assumption: "Many City A shoppers began bringing bags they **already owned** from home." Run the test — does it explain the low paper use? Yes, they used owned bags instead. Does it explain the flat reusable *purchases*? Yes, they didn't need to buy any; they already had them. Both facts now stand. Other valid resolutions work the same way — a retailer gave away free canvas bags, or shoppers consolidated into fewer, larger trips. Each introduces a **third path** the passage never ruled out.

**Worked example (medium).** "A factory installed expensive new safety equipment last year. Reported on-the-job injuries at the factory rose 30% in the following months." The surprise: safer equipment, *more* reported injuries? The assumption is "reported injuries track actual injuries." Resolution: "The new equipment came with a policy requiring workers to **report** every minor incident, including ones previously ignored." Test it — does it explain the equipment (it changed reporting rules, not just hardware)? Yes. Does it explain the rise in *reported* injuries (more reporting, not more injury)? Yes. A tempting wrong answer — "the new equipment was difficult to operate at first" — explains a rise in *actual* injuries but ignores that the equipment was bought to be *safer*; it makes the factory look reckless rather than reconciling the surprise. It fails fact 1's spirit. Notice the lever here: the paradox dissolved by attacking the word *reported*, not the word *injuries*. When a fact is phrased as a measurement ("reported," "recorded," "diagnosed," "surveyed"), suspect that the **measurement changed**, not the underlying reality.

> **Recall check.** What two questions make up the explain-both test, and what do you do if a candidate fails just one of them? (Does it explain fact 1? Does it explain fact 2? If either is "no," eliminate.) Force yourself to *produce* the two questions from memory rather than glancing back up — generating them is what wires them in for test day, where there's nothing to glance at.

**Worked example (medium).** "A coastal town installed brighter streetlights along its main road to deter crime. In the year after, the number of *reported* night-time thefts on that road climbed sharply, even though residents say they feel safer than ever." Fact 1: brighter lights, meant to deter crime. Fact 2: more reported thefts, yet a stronger sense of safety. Hidden assumption: "more reported thefts means more thefts are happening." Break it: "Better lighting let residents and cameras *see* and report minor thefts that previously went unnoticed in the dark." Test — Fact 1 holds (the lights did their job; visibility went up). Fact 2 holds (reports rose because detection rose, not because crime rose — hence people still feel safer). Both stand. The reflex to build: when "feel safer" sits next to "more reported X," the resolution almost always lives in the gap between *what happens* and *what gets counted*.

**Worked example (medium-hard).** "A pharmaceutical company's newest painkiller outperformed the older version in every clinical trial. Yet in the year after release, the new drug's sales were far lower than the old drug's had been at the same point in its launch." Fact 1: the new drug is clinically superior. Fact 2: it sold worse. Hidden assumption: "a better drug sells better — clinical performance drives sales." Break it with anything that separates *quality* from *sales*: "The new drug was priced at four times the older drug and was not yet covered by most insurance plans." Test — Fact 1 untouched (it's still the better drug). Fact 2 explained (doctors and patients couldn't or wouldn't pay). Both stand. A classic trap: "The older drug had occasionally caused mild side effects in some patients." True-sounding, on topic — but it only deepens the mystery, since it gives one *more* reason the superior new drug should have outsold the old one. An answer that makes the surprise *worse* is a guaranteed wrong answer.

> **Recall check.** In step 3 of the procedure, what sentence do you complete to surface the hidden assumption? ("These feel incompatible *because I'm assuming...*.") Retrieving the exact prompt now means you'll reach for it under time pressure instead of staring at five answers hoping one lights up — re-reading the step never builds that reflex.

**Trap to watch.** The classic wrong answer supports only **one** side of the discrepancy. It will be true, on topic, and confidently worded — and it leaves the paradox fully intact, because the paradox was about the two facts *together*, not either one alone. A second trap: an answer that *deepens* the mystery (makes the conflict worse), as in the painkiller example, or that simply restates one of the given facts in fresh words without adding anything. A third, subtler trap: an answer that would resolve the paradox only if you add *another* unstated assumption of your own — the correct answer resolves it cleanly, with no extra scaffolding required. Always force the two-part test; never accept "this sounds relevant."

**Worked example (hard, EXCEPT variant).** "Despite a sharp regional drought that cut the wheat harvest by a third, the country's bread prices fell over the same period. Each of the following, if true, helps explain this EXCEPT:" Here four choices each resolve, and your answer is the one that **fails** the explain-both test. Resolving choices might be: "the government released a large strategic grain reserve onto the market," "bread demand fell as consumers switched to cheaper rice," "imported wheat became far cheaper after a neighbouring country's bumper crop," or "a new milling technology sharply cut production costs." Each lets the drought (Fact 1) and the falling price (Fact 2) coexist. The odd one out — the credited answer — does *not*: e.g. "the drought also damaged the region's barley crop." That is on topic and true-sounding, but it touches neither the wheat supply story nor bread prices in a way that explains the drop; it leaves the paradox standing. On EXCEPT questions, don't switch modes — run the *same* explain-both test on all five, then pick the single failure. A practical tip: physically mark each choice "R" (resolves) or "X" (doesn't) as you go; the lone "X" is your answer, and the labels keep you from second-guessing the four that worked.

**The stem decoder.** Paradox stems advertise themselves with the language of surprise and reconciliation:

| Stem wording | What it signals |
|---|---|
| "...best **resolves** the apparent contradiction..." | Paradox |
| "...**explains how both** statements can be true..." | Paradox |
| "...**accounts for the discrepancy**..." | Paradox |
| "...explains the **surprising result / paradox**..." | Paradox |
| "...most helps to **reconcile** the two findings..." | Paradox |
| "...**EXCEPT** [which does not resolve]..." | Paradox, inverted — four answers each resolve; pick the one that doesn't |

That last row is the common variant. On a "resolve EXCEPT" question, four choices each pass the explain-both test, and the odd one out — the answer — is the one that fails it. Don't flip your brain into a different mode; run the same test, then pick the failure.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an answer that strongly supports only one of the two facts *never* resolve a paradox — even when it's a true, on-topic statement? (Because the surprise lives in the *combination*; explaining one fact better leaves the other just as inconsistent with it, so the tension is untouched.)

> **Recall check.** Name the six steps of the procedure in order, from reading the stem to confirming the survivor. ((1) Read the stem and confirm the type; (2) state the two facts in your own words; (3) name the hidden assumption; (4) predict the gap's shape; (5) run the explain-both test on each choice; (6) confirm the survivor carries both facts.) Listing them from memory now is what lets you run the routine on autopilot at minute 40 of the section — skimming the list again would only feel like learning.

**Common mistakes.**
- **Treating it like Strengthen/Weaken.** There's no conclusion to support or attack — hunting for "the author's point" wastes the read. The right answer is *explanatory*, not stronger or weaker.
- **Stopping at one "yes."** You confirm the choice explains Fact 1, feel relief, and click. The trap is built to earn that first yes; the missing second question is where it dies.
- **Picking the deepest-sounding fact.** The most detailed, technical-sounding choice is often a one-sided trap or one that *worsens* the mystery. Sophistication is not resolution.
- **Skipping the assumption step.** If you don't name *why* the two facts feel incompatible, you're matching answers by vibe. Naming the assumption tells you the exact shape of the answer to look for.

**Recap.**
- A paradox is a contradiction *with a gap*; the answer supplies a **third fact** so both stated facts stand — you want an **and**, never an **either/or**.
- Always run the **explain-both test**: Fact 1? Fact 2? One "no" eliminates.
- Surface and break the **hidden assumption** ("these feel incompatible because I'm assuming...") to predict the answer's shape *before* reading the choices.
- The credited answer **reconciles**; wrong answers support one side, restate a fact, or *deepen* the mystery.
- On **EXCEPT** variants, run the identical test on all five and pick the single choice that *fails* to resolve.
