## @cr-paradox

A Paradox question hands you two facts that both come from the passage, both stated as true, and that seem like they cannot both hold at once. Your job is not to pick a side. Your job is to find the missing piece of information that makes the apparent conflict dissolve — the third fact that lets **both** stated facts stand.

**Mental model.** A paradox is never a real contradiction. It is a contradiction *with a gap*. The passage gives you "A is true" and "B is true," and your instinct screams "but if A, then surely not B." That instinct rests on a hidden assumption you didn't notice you were making. The correct answer names the unstated condition that breaks that assumption, so A and B coexist peacefully. You are hunting for an **and**, never an **either/or**. (Paradox is the structural cousin of Flaw questions — both ask you to spot something "off" — but where Flaw hands you a broken argument to diagnose, Paradox hands you two clean facts to reconcile; there is no argument to attack here, only a surprise to explain.)

**The "explain both" test.** This is the entire game. Take your candidate answer and ask two questions in order:
- Does this explain fact 1?
- Does this explain fact 2?

If either answer is "no," eliminate it immediately. Trap answers almost always pass one test and fail the other — they are on topic, they sound like they belong, but they account for only half the puzzle and leave the other half just as mysterious as before.

**Worked example.** "After banning plastic bags, City A saw paper-bag use rise by only 10% — far below the 200% jump in other cities that imposed the same ban. Yet City A reported **no** increase in reusable-bag purchases." The surprise: how did City A dodge the paper surge *without* people buying reusable bags? Your hidden assumption is "the only two options are paper or newly bought reusables." A good resolution kills that assumption: "Many City A shoppers began bringing bags they **already owned** from home." Run the test — does it explain the low paper use? Yes, they used owned bags instead. Does it explain the flat reusable *purchases*? Yes, they didn't need to buy any; they already had them. Both facts now stand. Other valid resolutions work the same way — a retailer gave away free canvas bags, or shoppers consolidated into fewer, larger trips. Each introduces a **third path** the passage never ruled out.

**Worked example.** "A factory installed expensive new safety equipment last year. Reported on-the-job injuries at the factory rose 30% in the following months." The surprise: safer equipment, *more* reported injuries? The assumption is "reported injuries track actual injuries." Resolution: "The new equipment came with a policy requiring workers to **report** every minor incident, including ones previously ignored." Test it — does it explain the equipment (it changed reporting rules, not just hardware)? Yes. Does it explain the rise in *reported* injuries (more reporting, not more injury)? Yes. A tempting wrong answer — "the new equipment was difficult to operate at first" — explains a rise in *actual* injuries but ignores that the equipment was bought to be *safer*; it makes the factory look reckless rather than reconciling the surprise. It fails fact 1's spirit.

**Trap to watch.** The classic wrong answer supports only **one** side of the discrepancy. It will be true, on topic, and confidently worded — and it leaves the paradox fully intact, because the paradox was about the two facts *together*, not either one alone. A second trap: an answer that *deepens* the mystery (makes the conflict worse) or that simply restates one of the given facts. Always force the two-part test; never accept "this sounds relevant."

**The stem decoder.** Paradox stems advertise themselves with the language of surprise and reconciliation:

| Stem wording | What it signals |
|---|---|
| "...best **resolves** the apparent contradiction..." | Paradox |
| "...**explains how both** statements can be true..." | Paradox |
| "...**accounts for the discrepancy**..." | Paradox |
| "...explains the **surprising result / paradox**..." | Paradox |
| "...most helps to **reconcile** the two findings..." | Paradox |
| "...**EXCEPT** [which does not resolve]..." | Paradox, inverted — four answers each resolve; pick the one that doesn't |

That last row is the common variant. On a "resolve EXCEPT" question, four choices each pass the explain-both test, and the odd one out — the answer — is the one that fails it. Don't flip your brain into a different mode; run the same test, then pick the failure.

> **Recall check.** What two questions make up the explain-both test, and what do you do if a candidate fails just one of them? (Does it explain fact 1? Does it explain fact 2? If either is "no," eliminate.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an answer that strongly supports only one of the two facts *never* resolve a paradox — even when it's a true, on-topic statement? (Because the surprise lives in the *combination*; explaining one fact better leaves the other just as inconsistent with it, so the tension is untouched.)

Your move on every paradox: name the hidden assumption that makes the two facts feel incompatible, then hunt for the choice that introduces a third condition breaking that assumption — and confirm it carries **both** facts before you commit.
