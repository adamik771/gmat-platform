## @how-di-works

Data Insights gives you **20 questions in 45 minutes**. Divide that out and you get `2:15` per question — the most generous per-question budget on the whole exam. Quant gives you about `2:08`; Verbal gives you just under two minutes. That generous-looking number is where DI fools people: the average hides a lumpy reality. A Multi-Source Reasoning prompt makes you read a tabbed dossier before its first question pays you anything, and a single Table Analysis question is really three separate verdicts wearing one question number. `2:15` is a soft cap, not an entitlement — pace by the question in front of you, not by the average.

The section mixes **five question types**, and they arrive interleaved — apart from Multi-Source Reasoning, whose questions come as a consecutive set sharing one dossier, DI never groups by type, so you can go from a chart straight into a logic puzzle:

| Type | Came from | One question looks like | What must be right |
|------|-----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Data Sufficiency | The old Quant section | A question plus two statements; you judge whether the information is *enough* | One decision |
| Table Analysis | Old Integrated Reasoning | A sortable table plus three yes/no claims | All three verdicts |
| Graphics Interpretation | Old Integrated Reasoning | A chart with drop-down blanks to fill | Every blank |
| Two-Part Analysis | Old Integrated Reasoning | A grid where you select one answer per column | Both columns |
| Multi-Source Reasoning | Old Integrated Reasoning | Two or three tabs of sources feeding a set of questions | Each question in the set, fully |

That second column matters. Four of the five types came from the old test's Integrated Reasoning section; Data Sufficiency was lifted out of Quant. DI is not a new invention — it is a re-weighting, and how much it now counts is the next section's subject.

One more format fact, the one everyone remembers: DI is the **only section with an on-screen calculator**. Quant gives you nothing. Treat that asymmetry as information — the test makers added arithmetic help here because DI difficulty does not live in the arithmetic. The calculator is a tool when the numbers are genuinely ugly *and* the answer demands precision. The rest of the time it is a trap: reflexive reaching, typing before you know what you are solving, grinding what an estimate would have settled. The full discipline is trained in DI Foundations. For now, fix the orientation: calculator here and only here, and its presence is a clue about what is *not* being tested.

> **Recall check.** What is the per-question average in DI, and why is it misleading? (45 minutes over 20 questions is `2:15` — the biggest budget on the test — but multi-tab prompts and multi-part questions make the real distribution lumpy.)

## @why-di-decides

Here is the stake that changes everything: **DI is scored on its own 60-90 scale and counts toward your 205-805 Total Score with exactly the same weight as Quant and Verbal.** Not a side section, not an asterisk on the report — a full third of your score.

Now hold that against the history. The four Integrated Reasoning formats that became most of DI used to sit in a section that **did not count toward the old Total Score** at all. Prep culture grew around that fact — IR was the section you glanced at the weekend before the exam — and the culture did not die when the scoring changed. Most plans still run Quant-first, Verbal-second, DI-whenever. I did this myself on my first attempt, and DI was the cheapest score I left on the table. The inefficiency is in your favor — the section that moves a third of your score is the one your competition prepares last and least.

The second stake is scoring mechanics: **DI gives no partial credit.** A Table Analysis question with three yes/no claims pays zero if you get two of three. Two-Part Analysis pays zero if one column is wrong. Each Multi-Source Reasoning question is all-or-nothing too, even though the set shares one dossier. This rewrites your triage math. On Quant, partial understanding still converts to a decent guess. On DI, "almost solved" has a precise value: nothing.

So the triage rule is binary: **commit or bail, early.** The worst DI outcome is the slow bleed — half-working a multi-part question, pushing on because you have "already invested," and finishing with a question that pays nothing. Decide on the first read whether this question is yours. If it is, finish *all* of it — the last verdict is where the payment lives. If it is not, pick something and leave. The review screen softens this: you can flag questions as you go and **change up to three answers at the end of the section**, so a fast forced answer is a placeholder, not a verdict.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does no-partial-credit make abandoning early *cheaper* than abandoning late, in a way that is not true on Quant? (Because on a multi-part question every part you complete short of the last one converts to zero — the investment has no salvage value, so the only cheap exit is the immediate one.)

## @what-di-tests

Strip away the five formats and DI tests three skills. None of them is arithmetic — which is why this is the one section where arithmetic help is provided.

**Skill one: reading data displays fast.** Sortable tables, dual-axis charts, tabbed documents that mix memos with spreadsheets. Every DI question hands you a structure, and the first competence measured is whether you can orient inside it in seconds: what is one row, what does each axis own, which tab carries which kind of fact. Students who map the structure before touching the numbers consistently beat students who dive straight in. The same way Verbal trains you to read for an argument's skeleton, DI trains you to read for a display's skeleton — a trainable skill, not a talent.

**Skill two: sufficiency logic.** Data Sufficiency asks it openly — is this information *enough* to answer, without you ever producing the answer? But the logic leaks into everything else: knowing which tab holds the deciding fact in Multi-Source Reasoning, which single column settles a Table Analysis claim, when a comparison needs an ordering and not a value. It feels strange at first precisely because every other exam in your life rewarded finishing the computation.

**Skill three: knowing when *not* to compute.** Most DI questions are settled by an estimate, a bound, a sort, or a ratio comparison; a minority genuinely need exact arithmetic. Telling those apart quickly is the skill the on-screen calculator silently examines. If the answer choices are far apart, rough numbers decide it. The strong DI scorer is not the one who calculates fastest but the one who calculates *least* while still deciding correctly.

Notice what is missing from that list: formulas, advanced math, memorized content. DI has the lightest content load of the three sections and the heaviest judgment load — which means shaky Quant does not block you here. Display literacy, sufficiency judgment, and computational restraint are built by deliberate practice on the formats themselves, and the chapters ahead do exactly that, one skill and one format at a time.

## @di-in-the-path

This platform teaches one guided path, and DI is deliberately *spread through it* rather than parked at the end. The path rotates Quant, Verbal, and DI so all three sections grow in parallel — roughly one DI chapter lands in each rotation block. The spacing is by design: each return to DI forces retrieval of the last block's skills instead of letting them decay untouched.

Here is the DI line through the path, in order:

1. **DI Foundations** — immediately after this chapter. The core mindset, calculator discipline, table and chart literacy.
2. **Data Sufficiency** — the type that defines the section's logic.
3. **Table Analysis** — sortable tables and three-verdict claims.
4. **Graphics Interpretation** — axes, trends, and drop-down precision.
5. **Two-Part Analysis** — two linked answers under one constraint.
6. **Multi-Source Reasoning** — tabbed dossiers and cross-source synthesis.
7. **DI Timing** — at the very end of the whole path, alongside the Quant and Verbal timing chapters, once you have all five types to pace against each other.

How to study them: take each DI chapter when the path serves it, not in a clump. Do the readings actively — answer the recall checks from memory before reading on — and let the gap before the next DI chapter do its work. Between chapters, the error log matters more in DI than anywhere else, because DI mistakes split sharply into *misread the display*, *misjudged sufficiency*, and *computed when I should have estimated* — and the fix is different for each.

And measure on the real instrument. Your measuring stick for DI progress is not a feeling of familiarity; it is the **six official mba.com practice exams**, logged on the mock page as you take them. The DI section score sits on the same 60-90 scale as the others, so the trend across officials tells you plainly whether the work is converting.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does spacing the DI chapters through the path beat finishing all of them in one week? (Each spaced return forces retrieval under mild forgetting, which is what makes skills durable — massed practice feels smoother and stores worse.)

## @summary

Lock in the orientation before the training starts.

**The format.** `20` questions, `45` minutes, five types arriving shuffled: Data Sufficiency, Table Analysis, Graphics Interpretation, Two-Part Analysis, Multi-Source Reasoning. The `2:15` average is the most generous on the exam and the most deceptive — multi-tab prompts and multi-part questions make the real cost lumpy, so pace by the question, not the average. The on-screen calculator exists in DI and nowhere else, and its presence tells you arithmetic is not what is being graded.

**The stakes.** DI is scored 60-90 and weighs exactly as much as Quant and Verbal in your 205-805 total — one third of your score, inherited from formats that used to count for nothing, prepared last and least by your competition. No partial credit anywhere: three verdicts, two right, zero paid. Triage is binary — commit fully or bail immediately, and use the flags plus your three end-of-section answer changes instead of slow-bleeding on a question you have already lost.

**The skills.** Three, and none is arithmetic: read data displays fast, judge sufficiency instead of solving, and know when not to compute.

**The plan.** The DI chapters are spaced through your guided path, roughly one per rotation block: Foundations, then one chapter per question type, then DI Timing at the end of the path. Take them as the path serves them, log every miss with an honest cause, and judge progress by your DI section scores across the six official mba.com practice exams — not by familiarity.
