## @summary

- Identify the conclusion and its weakest link before reading the choices.
- Prefer evidence that closes the exact gap, rules out a competing explanation, or confirms the proposed mechanism.
- A strengthener need not prove the conclusion; it must make it more likely than it was.

## @cr-strengthen

A Strengthen question hands you an argument that already limps — evidence on one side, a conclusion on the other, and an unstated leap in between. Your job is **not to prove** the conclusion airtight. It is to find the one answer choice that, if true, makes that leap shorter: the conclusion becomes **more likely** given the evidence. (Weaken questions are the mirror operation — they widen the same gap — but you never need that mirror to solve a Strengthen. You only need to find the gap and pour support into it.)

**Mental model.** Every argument is a bridge from evidence to conclusion with a missing plank. A strengthener is a plank. The right answer doesn't have to make the bridge perfect; it just has to make it *more walkable* than it was before you read the choice. Ask of every option: "does the conclusion get more believable if this is true?" If the believability needle doesn't move, the choice is wrong — no matter how true or interesting it sounds. Notice the wording the test uses in the stem: **"if true."** You are never asked whether a choice is *real*; you assume each choice is a fact and ask only what it does to the conclusion. That single reframe kills the urge to argue with the answers. A choice can be wildly improbable in the real world and still be the credited answer — improbability is irrelevant; *effect on the conclusion* is everything.

**Find the gap first.** Before you read a single choice, separate the argument into its parts. The conclusion is the claim the author wants you to accept — often signaled by *so, thus, therefore, clearly, this shows.* The evidence is everything offered in support. The gap is the unstated assumption that has to be true for the evidence to deliver the conclusion. On the GMAT, the most common gap by far is **causal**: the author observes that two things move together and concludes one caused the other. Name that gap in a sentence and the credited answer almost announces itself. The discipline here is everything — students who skip straight to the choices end up grading five options on a vague feeling of "support," while students who name the gap first have a precise filter that throws out four wrong answers fast.

> **Recall check.** What is the *minimum* a strengthener has to do to the conclusion? (Make it **more likely** — not certain, not airtight; just move the believability needle even slightly in the right direction.) Retrieving this now, instead of skimming back up, is what installs the standard you'll apply to every choice under time pressure — the small effort of pulling it from memory is exactly what makes it automatic on test day.

**The four strengthener patterns.** Most GMAT Strengthen answers are one of these. Recognize the pattern and you'll spot the credited answer on sight.

- **Rule out an alternative cause.** If the argument says X caused Y, show that nothing *else* changed that could have caused Y. This shuts a competing explanation.
- **The control case.** If X caused Y, show that *without* X, Y didn't happen elsewhere. You've effectively run an experiment: same conditions, no X, no Y. This is the single most powerful strengthener on the test.
- **Confirm the data is representative.** If the conclusion rests on a sample or study, show the sample was large enough and unbiased, so the result generalizes.
- **Establish the mechanism.** Show *how* the cause produces the effect. A plausible physical or behavioral chain turns a bare correlation into a causal story.

A fifth, quieter pattern is worth keeping in your pocket: **rule out reverse causation.** When the author claims X caused Y, the sneaky rival is that Y actually caused X. A choice showing the proposed cause came *first* in time — that X was present before Y appeared — strengthens by closing that door.

**Worked example (straightforward).** Argument: "A restaurant chain launched a loyalty program; revenue then rose 8%. So the loyalty program drove the revenue lift." The gap: maybe something else lifted revenue. Strengthener (control case): "A competing chain in the same region, with no loyalty program, saw revenue *decline* 2% over the same period." Same market, same economy, no program, opposite outcome. That comparison isolates the program as the likely driver — the conclusion just got far more believable. Note what the choice did *not* do: it never claimed the program was popular or well-designed. It simply removed the "the whole regional market was booming" objection, and that alone is enough.

**Worked example (medium).** Argument: "Neighborhoods with more green space have lower childhood asthma rates, so green space lowers asthma." The leap: correlation isn't cause. Strengthener (mechanism): "Vegetation absorbs airborne particulate matter, a known asthma trigger." Now you have a *reason* the relationship would run from green space to lower asthma — the mechanism is supplied, and a mere correlation becomes a plausible causal claim. Watch how this choice doesn't add a single new statistic; it adds a *story* that connects the two facts you already have. That is often what a strengthener does.

**Worked example (medium, reverse causation).** Argument: "Companies that sponsor employee wellness programs have lower healthcare costs, so the programs cut costs." The hidden rival: maybe *healthy, low-cost* workforces are the ones whose companies can afford to add wellness programs — the effect could be driving the "cause." Strengthener (timing/reverse-causation): "In the year *before* launching wellness programs, the sponsoring companies had healthcare costs no lower than comparable firms." That establishes the cost gap opened up *after* the program began, not before — slamming the reverse-causation door and leaving the program as the explanation.

> **Recall check.** Name the four core strengthener patterns. (Rule out an alternative cause; the control case; confirm the data is representative; establish the mechanism.) Forcing yourself to list them from memory — rather than glancing back up the page — is what wires them in; the small effort of retrieval is exactly what builds durable recall.

**The stem decoder.** Strengthen wording is consistent. If the stem points at *supporting* the conclusion, you're strengthening.

| Stem wording | What it's asking |
|---|---|
| "…most strengthens the argument…" | Strengthen |
| "…most supports the conclusion…" | Strengthen |
| "…provides the best reason to believe…" | Strengthen |
| "…if true, most justifies the conclusion…" | Strengthen |
| "…would be most useful in establishing that the conclusion is correct…" | Strengthen |

**A procedure you can memorize.** Run the same five steps on every Strengthen question until they're automatic:

1. **Read the stem first.** Confirm the task is Strengthen (use the decoder) and note whose conclusion you're supporting.
2. **Isolate the conclusion.** Underline it mentally; everything else is either evidence or background.
3. **Name the gap in one sentence.** Usually: "does X, rather than something else, explain Y?" Write it as a question.
4. **Predict the pattern.** Decide which of the four (or five) strengtheners would fill *this* gap before you read the choices.
5. **Test each choice against the gap, to its period.** Keep only choices that make the conclusion more likely; if two survive, pick the one that hits the *central* leap, not a side premise.

**Worked example (medium-hard, alternative-cause).** Argument: "After our city installed brighter streetlights downtown, nighttime crime fell 15%. The lights deterred crime." Gap: did the *lights* cause the drop, or something else? A tempting-but-weak choice says "residents reported feeling safer." Feelings aren't crime. The credited strengthener rules out a rival cause: "Police staffing, patrol routes, and downtown foot traffic were unchanged over the period." By freezing every other plausible cause, the choice leaves the lights as the standing explanation — and the conclusion firms up.

**Worked example (hard, sample/representativeness).** Argument: "In a trial, patients given the new drug recovered two days faster than those given a placebo, so the drug speeds recovery." Gap: was the trial result trustworthy and generalizable? A trap choice notes "the drug is already approved in three other countries" — appeal to authority, not evidence about *this* claim. The strengthener confirms the data is sound: "Patients were assigned to the drug and placebo groups at random, and the two groups were similar in age, illness severity, and baseline health." Random, balanced groups mean the recovery difference is unlikely to be an artifact of who landed in which group — the inference from study to conclusion holds.

> **Recall check.** What does the word "if true" in a Strengthen stem instruct you to do? (Treat every choice as an assumed fact and judge only its *effect* on the conclusion — never whether the choice is realistic or arguable.) Pull this from memory now; recalling the rule under your own steam beats re-reading the sentence above, because the act of retrieval is what makes it stick on test day.

**Elimination: the irrelevant-answer trap.** Most wrong Strengthen answers are simply *irrelevant* — true statements that never touch the gap. Before reading the choices, name the gap in one sentence ("does the loyalty program, rather than something else, explain the revenue rise?"). Then test each choice against *that* gap: "does this tell me anything about how the evidence connects to the conclusion?" If no, eliminate, however plausible it sounds. An answer that the program "is popular with managers" or "is used by other chains" is real-world trivia, not a strengthener.

**Trap to watch.** Half-right answers. A choice whose first clause is a clean strengthener but whose second clause adds an irrelevant or wrong-direction fact is *wrong in full* — CR gives no partial credit. Read every choice to its period. Beware too the **partial strengthener**: an answer that shores up a minor premise while leaving the *central* gap untouched. The credited answer always addresses the main leap, so read all five before committing. And under time pressure, watch the **opposite-direction slip** — tired test-takers grab a *weakener* on a Strengthen question. Before you bubble, re-read the stem: am I supporting or undermining this? A two-second check prevents a thirty-second error.

**Trap to watch.** The "restates the premise" decoy. A choice that simply repeats evidence you were already given adds zero support — the conclusion was already standing on that fact. A real strengthener brings in *new* information that connects evidence to conclusion. If a choice feels comfortable because it echoes the passage, that comfort is the trap: confirm it adds a new link, not a familiar one.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the control case strengthen so powerfully? (Because it removes the leading rival explanation: by showing the effect fails to appear when the proposed cause is absent, it makes the cause the most likely remaining account of the effect — exactly the inference the conclusion needs.)

**Common mistakes.**

- **Strengthening the wrong claim.** Pour support into a premise instead of the conclusion, and the needle on the conclusion never moves. Always anchor to the *main* leap.
- **Rewarding interest over relevance.** A vivid, true, real-world fact that doesn't touch the gap is a distractor by design. Relevance to the gap is the only currency.
- **Stopping at the first clause.** A strong opening half lures you into bubbling before the disqualifying second half. Read to the period, every time.
- **Demanding proof.** You only need *more likely*, not *certain.* A choice that helps a lot but leaves the conclusion short of airtight can still be the credited answer.

**Recap.**

- A strengthener makes the conclusion **more likely**; it never has to make it certain.
- Find and name the **gap** in one sentence *before* reading choices — on the GMAT it's usually a causal leap.
- Know the patterns: **rule out an alternative cause, control case, representative data, mechanism** (plus reverse-causation and timing).
- Treat every choice as true and judge only its *effect*; eliminate the irrelevant, the premise-restaters, and the half-right.
- Read each choice to its period and confirm Strengthen, not Weaken, before you commit.
