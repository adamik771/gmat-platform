## @how-quant-works

GMAT Focus Quant is **21 questions in 45 minutes** — about **2:08 per question** — and every one of them is the same type: **Problem Solving**. A question, five answer choices, one correct. That is the whole format. Data Sufficiency, which older prep materials treat as half of Quant, is no longer in this section: on the Focus Edition it lives in **Data Insights**. Old prep that teaches statement-elimination grids as Quant strategy is describing a section that no longer exists.

The second defining fact: **no calculator.** Data Insights gets an on-screen calculator; Quant gets nothing but your head. The test-writers know that, so every question is built to be solvable with clean mental moves — fractions that cancel, percentages that snap to benchmarks, products that collapse before you multiply them out. This is why **technique beats grinding** here: cross-multiplication to compare fractions, cancelling before you multiply, benchmark percentages instead of long division. The chapters ahead teach each of these deliberately. The diagnostic to carry: if you are three steps into ugly four-digit arithmetic, you missed the shortcut the question was built around. The ugliness is the signal, not the task.

**Mental model.** Every Quant question is a locked door with two keys taped near it — one obvious and slow, one less obvious and fast. The slow key always works, eventually; the section is timed so that "eventually" loses. Your training is learning to reach for the fast key first.

Quant is scored on its own **60–90 scale** and counts **equally** with Verbal and Data Insights toward your **205–805** total — one-third of your score, no more.

One more format feature: you can **bookmark questions, revisit them within the section, and change up to three answers at the end** — a question you cannot crack in two minutes is not a now-or-never decision. The timing chapter trains this properly; until then, just know the escape hatch exists.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: how many questions, how many minutes, which question type, and is there a calculator? (21 questions, 45 minutes, all Problem Solving, no calculator. Pulling this from memory now is the cheap rep that makes it automatic later.)

## @what-quant-tests

Focus Quant draws from a short, closed list of topics. This matters more than it sounds: the section is not "math" in general, it is a finite syllabus you can actually finish.

| Topic family | What it looks like on test day |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic | Order of operations, signed numbers, fractions and decimals |
| Number properties | Even/odd, divisibility, primes, remainders, exponents and roots |
| Algebra | Linear equations and systems, quadratics, inequalities, functions |
| Word problems | Translating sentences into equations — the wrapper around everything else |
| Rates, ratios, percents | Speed/work problems, proportions, percent change, mixtures |
| Statistics | Mean, median, range, standard deviation as a concept |
| Counting and probability | Arrangements, combinations, probability of events |

And here is what is **not** on the list: **geometry**. Focus Quant no longer tests geometry or coordinate geometry — the triangles, circles, and slope formulas that filled chapters of old-GMAT prep are gone. If you studied for the old test, reallocate those hours. If you are starting fresh, you dodged it entirely.

Look at the table again: almost everything reduces to **arithmetic executed cleanly plus a translation skill**. Algebra is arithmetic with a letter in it. Rate problems are division with a story attached. Word problems are a language exercise before they are a math exercise. This is why the path starts with arithmetic foundations rather than the "interesting" topics — every later chapter spends the fluency you build in the first three.

**Trap to watch.** Students skim the topic list, recognize every word — "I know fractions, I know percents" — and skip ahead to hard problems. Recognizing a topic and executing it in under two minutes without a calculator are different skills. That gap is where my own first score came from; the foundations chapters close it.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why would removing geometry and the calculator make the section *more* learnable rather than less? (Fewer topics and mentally-sized numbers mean the test must reward a small set of repeatable techniques — and a small set of techniques is exactly what you can train to mastery.)

## @method-selection

Here is the highest-leverage idea in the entire Quant track: **the same question can take four minutes or forty seconds depending on which method you choose in the first ten.** Not on how fast you compute — on what you decide to do before computing anything.

**Worked example.** A theater sold `250` tickets. Adult tickets cost `$12`, child tickets cost `$7`, and total revenue was `$2,400`. How many adult tickets were sold? Choices: (A) 110 (B) 120 (C) 130 (D) 140 (E) 150.

Route one — the reflex: define `a`, write `12a + 7(250 - a) = 2400`, distribute, collect, solve `5a = 650`, get `a = 130`. Correct — and a minefield under pressure: one dropped sign in the distribution and you confidently mark a wrong answer.

Route two — test the middle choice: if adults are `130`, children are `120`; revenue is `130 × 12 = 1,560` plus `120 × 7 = 840`, total `2,400`. Done. Two multiplications and an addition — arithmetic on known numbers, nothing to translate, no sign to drop. Had C come out too big or too small, it would have killed half the remaining choices.

Both routes are legitimate mathematics. One of them is the right *exam* move. The point of this example is not "always test answers" — it is that **the choice existed**, and most untrained test-takers never see it.

**Mental model.** Before solving anything, you choose from a small menu: do the algebra, test the answer choices, substitute convenient numbers, or estimate and eliminate. Method selection is one question asked before touching the problem — "what is the cheapest route to the answer?" Ten seconds of choosing routinely saves two minutes of executing.

This is exactly why the path puts four **strategy-toolkit chapters** immediately after the arithmetic foundations, before any topic drilling: **Backsolving**, **Plugging In Numbers**, **Estimation**, and **Answer-Choice Tactics**. Each one is a full chapter on one route — when it triggers, the mechanical procedure, and where it breaks. Learn them early and every topic chapter afterward gets cheaper — you attack each new topic with four routes instead of one.

**Trap to watch.** The reflex to start solving is strongest in students who were good at school math — derivation was always the rewarded behavior. The GMAT awards no method points and nobody sees your work: an elegant algebraic solution and a brute test of choice C earn identical credit, and the clock only respects one of them.

> **Recall check.** What are the four strategy routes the toolkit chapters cover, and what is the one question method selection asks before solving? (Backsolving, plugging in numbers, estimation, answer-choice tactics; the question is "what is the cheapest route to the answer?")

## @working-the-path

The platform's curriculum is **one guided path of 50+ chapters**, and the Quant stretch inside it has a deliberate shape. Knowing the shape stops you from second-guessing the order.

1. **Arithmetic foundations first.** Order of operations and signed numbers, fractions and decimals, GCF/LCM and units digits. Unglamorous, the prerequisite for everything else, and where the no-calculator fluency gets built.
2. **The strategy toolkit.** The four method chapters you just read about — placed this early because every topic chapter afterward assumes you have them.
3. **Topic chapters, easy to hard, in rotation.** Number properties, then algebra, then word problems, ratios, percents, rates, statistics, counting, probability. The path interleaves Verbal and Data Insights chapters between them, so all three sections grow in parallel instead of Quant peaking in week two while Verbal rots.
4. **Timing last.** The Quant Timing & Pacing chapter comes at the end of the stretch, because pacing strategy is meaningless until the techniques it paces are in place.

Within any single chapter, the working method is fixed, and it is the same one I used:

- **Read the sections** — actively, not as scrolling. The worked examples are meant to be worked, not admired.
- **Do the recall checks honestly.** Answer from memory *before* reading the answer in the parentheses. The temptation is to glance and nod; recognition is not retrieval, and the exam only pays for retrieval.
- **Then the graded problem sets**, easy through hard, against the target accuracy for your score goal.
- **Log every miss.** Every single one goes in the error log with an honest tag — conceptual gap, careless slip, misread, time pressure. Your misses are not noise; they are the literal map of your next ten points, and the review queue resurfaces them until they stop being misses.

The measuring stick for your progress is not a feeling — it is the **six official practice exams from mba.com**, taken under full exam conditions and logged on the Mock page. Chapters and problem sets are training; the officials are the scoreboard. Train here, measure there.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does logging a careless error matter as much as logging a conceptual one, when "I knew how to do it"? (Because an error you make repeatedly is part of your real score regardless of what you knew — and you can only fix a pattern you have written down and named.)

## @summary

The whole chapter on one screen — lock these in before you start the foundations.

**The hard constraints.** Quant is **21 Problem Solving questions in 45 minutes** — about **2:08 each** — with **no calculator**. Data Sufficiency is gone to Data Insights; geometry is gone entirely. The section is scored **60–90** and is exactly one-third of your **205–805** total. You can bookmark, revisit within the section, and change up to three answers at the end.

**The content.** A closed list: arithmetic, number properties, algebra, word problems, rates/ratios/percents, statistics, counting and probability — finite, learnable, finishable, and nearly all of it reducing to clean arithmetic plus translation. That is why foundations come first.

**The lever.** Method selection. Before solving, ask: *what is the cheapest route?* Algebra, backsolving, plugging in numbers, estimation — the same question can cost four minutes or forty seconds depending on that choice. The four strategy-toolkit chapters make the choice automatic.

**The working loop.** Read the chapter, answer every recall check from memory before peeking, run the graded problem sets, and log every miss with an honest tag. Train on the chapters; measure on the six official mba.com exams. Repeat.

**Trap to watch.** The urge to skip foundations because the topics look familiar. Familiar is not fast, and fast-without-a-calculator is the actual test. I thought I was above the foundations material when I started; I was not, and finding that out cheaply in a chapter beats finding it out expensively in an exam.

> **Recall check.** Close your eyes and recite: the question count and time, the two things the Focus Edition removed from Quant, and the one question method selection asks. (21 in 45; Data Sufficiency moved out and geometry removed; "what is the cheapest route to the answer?")

That is the section. Now go build the engine it runs on — turn to **Arithmetic: Order of Operations & Signed Numbers** and start with the grammar of every calculation you will do on test day.
