## @cr-flaw

A **Flaw question** hands you an argument that is broken and asks you to *name the break* in abstract terms. The stem reads like "Which of the following describes a flaw in the argument's reasoning?" or "The argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it…" You are not asked to weaken the argument, supply its missing assumption, or judge whether its conclusion is true in the real world. You are asked to **diagnose the reasoning move that fails** — and to recognize that diagnosis dressed in generic, content-free language.

**Mental model.** A flaw answer is a *medical diagnosis*, not a *symptom*. The argument is the patient; the answer choice is the chart entry. A good chart entry says "bacterial infection," not "the patient coughed on Tuesday." So the correct choice names the *category* of error ("confuses correlation with causation," "generalizes from an unrepresentative sample") rather than re-narrating the argument's specific content. The instant you can name the error before looking at the choices, the answer hunt becomes matching, not reasoning.

Why does naming-first work so well here? Because Flaw choices are written to punish readers who shop the menu before forming a view. If you walk in undecided, every choice that describes a *plausible-sounding* fallacy feels tempting, and the test-writer has stocked the menu with three or four such temptations. If you walk in having already said the error in your own words, four of the five choices simply do not match what you said, and they fall away without a fight. Diagnosis is your anchor; the choices are the current trying to drag you off it. There is a hidden time payoff here too: a student who diagnoses first spends ten seconds matching, while a student who browses re-reads the passage against each choice and burns ninety. On a section where pace decides the score, the habit of naming the error before reading is not just an accuracy edge — it is a clock edge.

Most GMAT flaws fall into a short, repeatable list. Learn it cold and you will recognize the error on first read.

- **Correlation vs. causation.** The author sees X and Y together and concludes X *caused* Y — ignoring reverse causation, a common third cause, or coincidence.
- **Unrepresentative sample.** A conclusion about a whole population is drawn from a sample too small, biased, or atypical to support it.
- **Percent vs. number.** The author treats a change in *percentage* as if it were a change in *absolute count* (or vice versa) — a rising percentage can sit on a shrinking base.
- **Necessary vs. sufficient.** "If A then B" is treated as "if B then A," or a condition that is merely required is treated as enough on its own.
- **Equivocation.** A key term shifts meaning between premise and conclusion ("a good *lawyer*" meaning ethical, then meaning successful).
- **Ad hominem / appeal to authority.** The argument attacks the *source* instead of the claim, or leans on an authority irrelevant to the point.
- **Circular reasoning.** The conclusion is smuggled into the evidence — it assumes what it set out to prove.
- **Part vs. whole (composition/division).** The author assumes what is true of the parts must be true of the whole, or vice versa ("each ingredient is tasty, so the dish is tasty").

These eight are not a vocabulary list to skim; they are a recognition library to over-learn. The difference matters. You want the error to *announce itself* the way a familiar face does in a crowd — instantly, before you can articulate why. That only comes from repetition. The practical test of mastery is this: given a one-sentence argument, can you slap the right label on it in under three seconds, the way a trained eye reads an X-ray? If you still have to reason your way to "oh, that's correlation/causation," the library is not yet over-learned, and timed practice is the only fix.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up, list as many of the eight standard GMAT flaws as you can and say what each one confuses. (Correlation vs. causation; unrepresentative sample; percent vs. number; necessary vs. sufficient; equivocation; ad hominem or appeal to authority; circular reasoning; part vs. whole.) Forcing the list out of memory now — and noticing which ones you blank on — builds far more durable recall than reading the list a second time, because retrieval is the act that strengthens the trace.

**Worked example.** (Straightforward.) "Residents of neighborhoods with more coffee shops report better moods. Therefore, opening coffee shops improves residents' moods." The author saw a correlation and asserted causation. Wealthier, more walkable neighborhoods may attract both coffee shops *and* happier residents — a third cause. The correct flaw choice reads: **"it concludes that one factor caused another merely because the two are associated."** Notice the choice never mentions coffee — that abstraction is the signature of a right answer.

**Worked example.** (Straightforward.) "Last year 8% of accidents involved drivers under 25; this year 11% did. Clearly young drivers have become more reckless." The author leaned on a *percentage* shift. If total accidents fell sharply, the absolute number of young-driver accidents could have dropped even as their *share* rose — older drivers simply crashed less. The diagnosis: **"it treats a change in proportion as evidence of a change in underlying behavior, ignoring shifts in the total."** Again, content-free.

**Worked example.** (Medium.) "A survey of subscribers to a personal-finance magazine found that 70% own stocks. The magazine concludes that most adults in the country own stocks." The conclusion jumps from a *self-selected, finance-interested* slice of people to the *entire adult population*. People who subscribe to an investing magazine are precisely the people most likely to own stocks — the sample is biased toward the conclusion. Diagnosis in five words: *generalizes from an unrepresentative group.* The matching choice reads **"it draws a conclusion about a population from a sample that is unlikely to be representative of that population."** A trap choice here might say "it assumes that owning stocks is the same as being wealthy" — true-sounding, but it is not the move this argument made.

**Worked example.** (Medium.) "Every employee who took the optional ethics seminar reported high job satisfaction. So the seminar must be what makes employees satisfied." Watch the self-selection hiding inside a causal claim — this example blends two library entries, which is exactly how harder Flaw questions are built. The seminar was *optional*, so the people who chose it may already have been the engaged, satisfied employees; their satisfaction could be the *cause* of attending, not the effect. The argument both ignores reverse causation and treats a self-selected group as if it told us about the seminar's power. Either label points the same direction, but the cleaner abstract diagnosis is **"it takes a correlation found among a self-selected group as proof that one factor produced the other."** When two flaws overlap, name the one the *conclusion* actually leans on — here, the causal leap — and find the choice that captures it.

> **Recall check.** A choice in front of you reads, "the argument assumes that what is true of each individual member of a group is therefore true of the group as a whole." Which library entry is that, and what is its mirror-image twin? (Part vs. whole — specifically the *composition* direction; its twin is *division*, assuming what is true of the whole must be true of each part.) Naming both directions from memory now beats re-reading the bullet, because the test rarely labels the flaw for you — you have to supply the name under pressure.

**Worked example.** (Medium-hard.) "Every employee who received the new training passed the certification exam. So to guarantee that next year's hires pass, the company need only enroll them in the training." The premise establishes that training was *sufficient* for the people who took it last year — everyone who took it passed. But the conclusion treats training as the *only* path and as a *guarantee* for a different group. Even granting last year's perfect record, the argument confuses "this worked for that cohort" with "this is necessary and sufficient for any cohort." More precisely, it treats a condition that proved sufficient for one self-selected group as guaranteed-sufficient for a new group with possibly different characteristics. The clean diagnosis: **"it assumes that a factor sufficient under one set of conditions will remain sufficient under different conditions."** Note how the right answer abstracts away from "training" and "certification" entirely.

Use the stem decoder to confirm you are in Flaw territory before you start hunting.

| Stem wording | Type |
|---|---|
| "…describes a flaw in the argument's reasoning…" | Flaw |
| "…the reasoning above is flawed because…" | Flaw |
| "…the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that…" | Flaw |
| "…the argument fails to consider the possibility that…" | Flaw |
| "…overlooks which of the following…" | Flaw |
| "…the argument's reasoning is questionable because it…" | Flaw |

A quick caution on the boundary cases. "Fails to consider the possibility that…" and "overlooks which of the following…" sit on the line between Flaw and Weaken. The tell is what the *choices* look like: if they are abstract descriptions of error types ("it overlooks the possibility that the cause and effect are reversed"), you are in Flaw and you answer with a *shape*; if they are concrete new facts about the world, you are in Weaken and you answer with *content*. Read the first two choices to confirm which game you are playing before committing.

> **Recall check.** What single feature of the answer *choices* tells you whether an "overlooks the possibility that…" stem is a Flaw question or a Weaken question? (Whether the choices are abstract error-descriptions — Flaw — or concrete real-world facts — Weaken.) Pull this distinction from memory now; the mid-test version of this question comes with time pressure, and a rule you only recognized passively tends to evaporate under the clock.

**The "description, not criticism" filter.** A flaw answer *describes the type of error* at a meta level; it does not restate the argument's content or list a factual disagreement. Choices that re-narrate the passage ("the argument says coffee shops help") or quibble with a fact ("coffee is not actually a mood booster") are nearly always wrong. The right answer is a *shape* — "confuses X with Y," "generalizes from a biased sample," "takes a necessary condition to be sufficient." A useful habit: read each choice and ask, "Could this exact sentence be glued onto a totally different argument about a totally different topic?" If yes, it is properly abstract and a live candidate. If it only makes sense for *this* passage's subject matter, it is almost certainly a content re-narration in disguise.

**Trap to watch.** The most seductive wrong answer describes a *real flaw* — just not the one this argument commits. The choices are a menu of standard errors, and several will name genuine fallacies. "Relies on an unrepresentative sample" is a perfect description of a sampling flaw, but if this argument's actual sin was causal overreach, that choice is wrong no matter how true-to-life it sounds. Name *this* argument's specific error first; then accept only the choice that matches *it*.

**Trap to watch.** The "describes something true but harmless" choice. Some choices accurately describe a move the argument makes — "it relies on the testimony of a single source," say — but that move is not actually a *flaw* given the conclusion. A description can be factually accurate about the passage and still be the wrong answer if the thing it describes does not undermine the reasoning. Ask not just "did the argument do this?" but "does doing this *break* the argument?" Both must be yes.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why must a correct flaw answer be phrased in abstract, content-free language rather than naming the argument's specific subject? (Because it is diagnosing the *category* of reasoning error; a choice tied to the specific content is usually re-narrating the argument, not identifying the logical move that failed.)

**Worked example.** (Hard — combining the traps.) "Critics say our city's new bike lanes are dangerous, but those critics are funded by the parking-garage industry, which loses money when people bike. So the bike lanes are perfectly safe." Two things are happening. The argument attacks the *source* of the criticism rather than addressing whether the lanes are dangerous — classic ad hominem. From "the critics are biased" it concludes "the lanes are safe," which does not follow even if the critics are hopelessly compromised. Now the trap: a choice reading "it relies on data from a single study" describes a real-sounding flaw, but the argument cites no study, so it is true-of-nothing and out. Another choice, "it assumes the parking-garage industry is dishonest," re-narrates content and is not the logical error. The correct, abstract answer: **"it rejects a claim by impugning the motives of those who make it, rather than by addressing the claim itself."** Diagnose the move (attack the arguer), then take only the choice that names that move generically.

**Step-by-step procedure.** Memorize this and run it on every Flaw question:

1. **Confirm the type.** Match the stem against the decoder. On boundary stems, glance at the first two choices to confirm they are abstract descriptions, not new facts.
2. **Split the argument.** Find the conclusion (the claim being supported) and the evidence (what is offered for it). Underline both mentally.
3. **Find the gap.** Ask, "What does the author *assume* to get from this evidence to this conclusion?" The flaw lives in that leap.
4. **Name it in five words.** Say the error to yourself in generic terms — "confuses correlation with causation," "sample is biased," "necessary treated as sufficient" — *before* reading any choice.
5. **Match, don't browse.** Scan for the one choice that states your five-word diagnosis abstractly. Reject content re-narrations and true-but-irrelevant fallacy names on sight.
6. **Confirm both halves.** For your finalist, verify (a) the argument actually makes this move and (b) the move actually breaks the reasoning. Both must hold.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Browsing before diagnosing.** Reading the choices first and letting them tell you what the flaw is — this is exactly the behavior the trap choices are engineered to exploit.
- **Choosing a real fallacy the argument did not commit.** The choice names a genuine error type, but not *this* argument's error. Accuracy about fallacies in the abstract is not enough; it must be *this* passage's move.
- **Picking a content re-narration.** A choice that restates the passage's subject ("assumes coffee improves mood") feels confirming but is describing the topic, not the logical error.
- **Accepting a true-but-harmless description.** The choice correctly describes something the argument does, but that something does not actually undermine the conclusion.

**Closing recap.**

- A Flaw answer is a *diagnosis* of the reasoning error, stated in abstract, content-free language — never a symptom, criticism, or factual disagreement.
- Over-learn the standard library: correlation/causation, unrepresentative sample, percent/number, necessary/sufficient, equivocation, ad hominem/authority, circular, part/whole.
- Run the procedure: confirm type → split argument → find the gap → name the error in five words → match → confirm both halves.
- Two killer traps: the *real-but-wrong* fallacy and the *true-but-harmless* description. Beat both by demanding that the choice match *your* diagnosis and actually break the argument.
- When you can name the break before reading a single choice, Flaw questions stop being puzzles and become recognition.
