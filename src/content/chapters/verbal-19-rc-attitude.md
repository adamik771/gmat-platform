## @rc-attitude

Author's Attitude and Tone questions ask you to do one thing precisely: name where the **author** stands. Not what the passage says, not what some expert quoted inside it believes — where the author's *own* voice lands on the spectrum from praise to neutral reporting to attack, and how *strongly* that voice commits. These questions are pure calibration. The right answer is the choice whose **direction and intensity both match** the author; almost every wrong answer fails on exactly one of those two axes.

**Mental model.** Tone has two dials, and you must set both. **Direction**: positive, negative, or neutral. **Intensity**: how strong the commitment — mild, moderate, or extreme. An answer that nails direction but overshoots intensity ("enthusiastic endorsement" when the author offers only "qualified support") is still wrong. Read the passage for the dials first, then dial-match the choices.

**The adjective filter.** The author's word choice gives the stance away — usually in the *modifiers* attached to noun phrases, not the nouns themselves. "Striking results" carries approval; "purported results" carries doubt. "Well-documented findings" is supportive; "allegedly demonstrated findings" is skeptical. Run every loaded adjective and adverb through this filter. The author who writes "an elegant but ultimately incomplete model" has handed you the answer: positive-leaning, moderate intensity, with a genuine reservation attached.

**The qualifier signal.** Hedging language encodes caution. Authors who are uncertain or critical write "may," "might," "seems to," "in some cases," "remains to be shown." Authors who are confident write "is," "does," "will," "demonstrates." Heavy qualifiers point to a cautious or measured stance; their absence points to a confident one. Count the hedges before you pick.

Match the stem to what it actually asks for:

| Stem wording | What it wants |
|---|---|
| "The author's attitude toward X is best described as..." | Direction + intensity of the author's own stance on X |
| "The tone of the passage is..." | Overall stance across the whole passage |
| "The author would most likely regard X as..." | Stance inferred from the author's framing of X |
| "Which best describes how the author views Y?" | Same as attitude — name the position on the spectrum |
| "The author's discussion of X suggests that the author..." | Attitude read off word choice and framing |

Map every answer choice onto this spectrum, from most positive to most negative:

| Tone | Tell-tale word choices |
|---|---|
| Enthusiastic | "crucial," "revolutionary," "compelling," "fundamental advance" |
| Supportive | "valuable," "well-supported," "sound," "strong evidence" |
| Neutral | "describes," "notes," "explains," "reports" |
| Measured / cautious | "plausible but limited," "suggests but does not prove" |
| Skeptical | "questionable," "unclear," "unconvincing," "remains to be shown" |
| Critical | "flawed," "oversimplified," "misleading," "overlooks the difficulty" |

**Worked example.** A passage on a new dating method for fossils closes: "The technique offers a welcome alternative to older approaches, though its reliance on uncontaminated samples limits its use to a narrow set of sites." Attitude toward the technique?
- A) "Unqualified enthusiasm." — Right direction, wrong intensity. "Welcome" is positive, but "though... limits" is a real reservation. Overshoots.
- B) "Cautious approval." — **Correct.** Positive direction ("welcome alternative"), moderate intensity, with the limitation flagged by "though."
- C) "Detached neutrality." — Wrong direction. "Welcome" is not neutral; the author is taking a side.
- D) "Pointed skepticism." — Reverses the tone. The author endorses the technique and merely notes a boundary.

**Worked example.** A passage reviews a popular economic theory: "Proponents present the model as settled, yet the data they cite are drawn almost entirely from a single decade, and competing explanations are dismissed rather than tested." Attitude toward the theory?
- A) "Admiring." — Reverses tone. "Dismissed rather than tested" is a complaint, not praise.
- B) "Neutral and descriptive." — Misses the loaded verbs. "Dismissed rather than tested" is not neutral reporting.
- C) "Skeptical of the theory's support." — **Correct.** The author doubts the evidence base ("single decade") and the method ("dismissed rather than tested"), but stops short of calling the theory false.
- D) "Contemptuous and dismissive." — Overshoots intensity. The author raises doubts, not contempt.

**Trap to watch.** Extreme labels are usually wrong. GMAT authors write in an academic, measured register; "vehement condemnation," "unbridled enthusiasm," "complete rejection," and "total endorsement" almost never fit. When two choices share the correct direction, prefer the milder one — "qualified support" over "strong endorsement," "measured skepticism" over "harsh criticism." The right answer lives near the middle of the spectrum far more often than at its poles.

**Be specific about the "toward what."** The stem names a target — *toward X*. An author can be enthusiastic about a broad theory yet skeptical of one claim inside it. Pin down exactly what X is and read the stance on *that*, not on the passage's general mood. Picking up the author's feeling about the wrong target is a quiet, common miss.

One orienting note: this type is the close cousin of Function questions, which ask what the author is *doing* structurally rather than how the author *feels*. Here your only job is the feeling and its strength.

> **Recall check.** What are the two dials you must set on every attitude question, and which kind of mismatch trips up most test-takers? (Direction and intensity; overshooting intensity — picking an extreme label when the author is measured.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the "prefer the milder choice" heuristic work so reliably on GMAT attitude questions? (Because these passages are written in an academic register that hedges and qualifies; the text rarely supplies the strong, unhedged language an extreme label would require, so the milder choice is the one you can actually cite from the words on the page.)
