## @rc-attitude

Author's Attitude and Tone questions ask you to do one thing precisely: name where the **author** stands. Not what the passage says, not what some expert quoted inside it believes — where the author's *own* voice lands on the spectrum from praise to neutral reporting to attack, and how *strongly* that voice commits. These questions are pure calibration. The right answer is the choice whose **direction and intensity both match** the author; almost every wrong answer fails on exactly one of those two axes. Master that single insight and this question type collapses from "guess the vibe" into a mechanical two-dial check you can run the same way every time.

**Mental model.** Tone has two dials, and you must set both. **Direction**: positive, negative, or neutral. **Intensity**: how strong the commitment — mild, moderate, or extreme. An answer that nails direction but overshoots intensity ("enthusiastic endorsement" when the author offers only "qualified support") is still wrong. Read the passage for the dials first, then dial-match the choices. The reason this two-dial frame matters is that the test writers build wrong answers by twisting one dial at a time: a "direction trap" reverses positive to negative, and an "intensity trap" keeps the right sign but pushes it to a pole. If you only check one dial, you will reliably fall for the other. Think of each answer choice as a coordinate — a sign and a magnitude — and your job as plotting the author at one point, then crossing off every coordinate that doesn't sit on top of it.

**The adjective filter.** The author's word choice gives the stance away — usually in the *modifiers* attached to noun phrases, not the nouns themselves. "Striking results" carries approval; "purported results" carries doubt. "Well-documented findings" is supportive; "allegedly demonstrated findings" is skeptical. Run every loaded adjective and adverb through this filter. The author who writes "an elegant but ultimately incomplete model" has handed you the answer: positive-leaning, moderate intensity, with a genuine reservation attached. The noun ("model," "results," "findings") is inert; the charge always rides on the words *around* it.

**The qualifier signal.** Hedging language encodes caution. Authors who are uncertain or critical write "may," "might," "seems to," "in some cases," "remains to be shown." Authors who are confident write "is," "does," "will," "demonstrates." Heavy qualifiers point to a cautious or measured stance; their absence points to a confident one. Count the hedges before you pick. A passage thick with "may" and "appears to" is almost never going to support an answer like "firmly convinced" — the words on the page won't back it. Treat the hedge count as your intensity gauge: many hedges pull you toward the middle of the spectrum, clean declaratives push you toward the ends.

> **Recall check.** What are the two dials you must set on every attitude question, and which kind of mismatch trips up most test-takers? (Direction and intensity; overshooting intensity — picking an extreme label when the author is measured.) Forcing yourself to *state* the two dials from memory now, before you read the procedure below, builds a retrieval path you can fire under time pressure — re-reading the definition would feel familiar but wouldn't.

Match the stem to what it actually asks for:

| Stem wording | What it wants |
|---|---|
| "The author's attitude toward X is best described as..." | Direction + intensity of the author's own stance on X |
| "The tone of the passage is..." | Overall stance across the whole passage |
| "The author would most likely regard X as..." | Stance inferred from the author's framing of X |
| "Which best describes how the author views Y?" | Same as attitude — name the position on the spectrum |
| "The author's discussion of X suggests that the author..." | Attitude read off word choice and framing |

Map every answer choice onto this spectrum, from most positive to most negative:

| Tone | Tell-tale word choices |
|---|---|
| Enthusiastic | "crucial," "revolutionary," "compelling," "fundamental advance" |
| Supportive | "valuable," "well-supported," "sound," "strong evidence" |
| Neutral | "describes," "notes," "explains," "reports" |
| Measured / cautious | "plausible but limited," "suggests but does not prove" |
| Skeptical | "questionable," "unclear," "unconvincing," "remains to be shown" |
| Critical | "flawed," "oversimplified," "misleading," "overlooks the difficulty" |

**The step-by-step procedure.** Memorize this five-step routine and run it the same way every time, even when a passage feels obvious:

1. **Pin the target.** Read the stem and underline exactly what X is. Attitude *toward what* — the whole theory, one claim inside it, a rival's method? You are scoring the author's stance on *that*, nothing else.
2. **Harvest the loaded words.** Scan the sentences that discuss X and collect the charged adjectives, adverbs, and verbs ("welcome," "purported," "dismissed," "elegant," "limited"). These, not the topic, carry the tone.
3. **Set the direction dial.** From those words alone, decide positive, negative, or neutral. If the charged words point both ways ("elegant but incomplete"), the author is mixed — note which side dominates and that a reservation exists.
4. **Set the intensity dial.** Count the hedges. Heavy qualifiers mean mild/moderate; clean, unhedged declaratives mean strong. Default toward the middle unless the text gives you pole-strength language.
5. **Dial-match and eliminate.** Cross off any choice that fails on *either* dial — wrong sign or wrong strength. When two survivors share the correct direction, keep the milder one.

**Worked example (straightforward).** A passage on a new dating method for fossils closes: "The technique offers a welcome alternative to older approaches, though its reliance on uncontaminated samples limits its use to a narrow set of sites." Attitude toward the technique?
- A) "Unqualified enthusiasm." — Right direction, wrong intensity. "Welcome" is positive, but "though... limits" is a real reservation. Overshoots.
- B) "Cautious approval." — **Correct.** Positive direction ("welcome alternative"), moderate intensity, with the limitation flagged by "though."
- C) "Detached neutrality." — Wrong direction. "Welcome" is not neutral; the author is taking a side.
- D) "Pointed skepticism." — Reverses the tone. The author endorses the technique and merely notes a boundary.

**Worked example (moderate).** A passage reviews a popular economic theory: "Proponents present the model as settled, yet the data they cite are drawn almost entirely from a single decade, and competing explanations are dismissed rather than tested." Attitude toward the theory?
- A) "Admiring." — Reverses tone. "Dismissed rather than tested" is a complaint, not praise.
- B) "Neutral and descriptive." — Misses the loaded verbs. "Dismissed rather than tested" is not neutral reporting.
- C) "Skeptical of the theory's support." — **Correct.** The author doubts the evidence base ("single decade") and the method ("dismissed rather than tested"), but stops short of calling the theory false.
- D) "Contemptuous and dismissive." — Overshoots intensity. The author raises doubts, not contempt.

Notice the pivot word doing the work in both examples. In the first, "though" introduces a limit *after* a positive; in the second, "yet" introduces doubt *after* reporting the proponents' confident framing. Pivot words — *though, yet, however, nonetheless, even so* — are where the author's real stance usually surfaces, because the clause **after** the pivot is the one the author owns. When you are short on time, jump straight to these words: they are the cheapest, highest-yield place to read the author's hand.

> **Recall check.** Where in a sentence does the author's true stance most often live, and why? (In the clause after a pivot word like "yet" or "though" — the author concedes the other view first, then states their own.) Pull this up from memory rather than scanning back; the act of generating the rule is what fixes it for the test.

**Worked example (target-specific).** A passage states: "Whewell's theory of the tides was an ambitious synthesis, and its central insight — that tidal patterns could be mapped globally — was genuinely original. His specific predictions for the North Sea, however, rested on coastal data so sparse that they amounted to little more than informed guesses." Attitude *toward Whewell's North Sea predictions*?
- A) "Admiring of their originality." — Wrong target. The originality praise attaches to the *global insight*, not the North Sea predictions. This is the quiet miss.
- B) "Skeptical of their evidentiary basis." — **Correct.** On *this* target, the author flags "sparse" data and "informed guesses." The stem pinned the predictions, not the theory at large.
- C) "Wholly dismissive of Whewell's work." — Overshoots and wrong scope. The author praises the broader synthesis; only the narrow predictions draw doubt.
- D) "Neutral." — Misses "little more than informed guesses," which is plainly critical.

**Worked example (hard — mixed register).** A passage on a conservation program reads: "The initiative has, to its credit, reversed the decline of two flagship species, and its supporters are right to celebrate that. Yet the same resources, critics note, might have protected a dozen less charismatic but more imperiled species — a trade-off the program's defenders have never seriously addressed." Attitude toward the program?
- A) "Enthusiastic endorsement." — Catches "to its credit" but ignores the unaddressed trade-off the author clearly thinks matters.
- B) "Balanced but with a pointed reservation." — **Correct.** The author grants a real success ("to its credit," "right to celebrate") yet ends on a charge the defenders "have never seriously addressed" — the author's own emphasis lands on the critique.
- C) "Hostile to the program." — Overshoots. A genuine credit is given; this is not hostility.
- D) "Detached and noncommittal." — Misses both the explicit credit and the closing reservation; the author is not neutral.
- The tell is structural: the praise is real but the passage *ends* on the unaddressed trade-off. Where the author chooses to leave you — the final, unhedged clause — usually carries the dominant stance.

**Worked example (hard — quoted-source decoy).** A passage on a medical study reads: "Its authors hail the trial as definitive proof that the drug halts progression. The trial, however, enrolled only forty patients and ran for eight weeks — a window too short to observe the relapses that typically appear in the second year." Attitude *of the author* toward the trial's claim?
- A) "Convinced of the drug's effectiveness." — Scores the *study's authors*, not the passage's author. The word "hail" belongs to *them*; the passage's author never adopts it.
- B) "Cautiously skeptical of the claim's support." — **Correct.** The passage author's own framing — "only forty patients," "too short" — undercuts the "definitive proof" the study authors assert.
- C) "Openly contemptuous of the researchers." — Overshoots. The author questions the *evidence*, not the researchers' integrity or competence.
- D) "Neutral summary of the trial." — Misses "too short to observe the relapses," which is the author editorializing against the claim.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the "prefer the milder choice" heuristic work so reliably on GMAT attitude questions? (Because these passages are written in an academic register that hedges and qualifies; the text rarely supplies the strong, unhedged language an extreme label would require, so the milder choice is the one you can actually cite from the words on the page.)

**Trap to watch.** Extreme labels are usually wrong. GMAT authors write in an academic, measured register; "vehement condemnation," "unbridled enthusiasm," "complete rejection," and "total endorsement" almost never fit. When two choices share the correct direction, prefer the milder one — "qualified support" over "strong endorsement," "measured skepticism" over "harsh criticism." The right answer lives near the middle of the spectrum far more often than at its poles. The exception that proves the rule: if the passage genuinely piles on unhedged, charged language with no concessions, a strong label *can* be right — but you must be able to quote the strong words. No quotable evidence, no extreme answer.

**Trap to watch.** Don't score the *quoted source* instead of the author. Passages often summarize what "proponents claim" or "critics argue," and a wrong answer will hand you the quoted party's feeling. The author's stance shows up in the framing *around* the quotation — words like "rightly," "supposedly," "fails to note," "as the evidence confirms." If you find yourself adopting the enthusiasm of someone the author is merely reporting, you have scored the wrong voice. The medical-trial example above is exactly this trap weaponized: "hail" and "definitive" are the *study authors'* words, and the passage author spends the rest of the sentence quietly dismantling them.

**Be specific about the "toward what."** The stem names a target — *toward X*. An author can be enthusiastic about a broad theory yet skeptical of one claim inside it. Pin down exactly what X is and read the stance on *that*, not on the passage's general mood. Picking up the author's feeling about the wrong target is a quiet, common miss — and, as the Whewell example showed, it is precisely the miss the test writers engineer. A useful habit: after you settle on a label, reread the stem's target one more time and ask, "Is this the stance toward *that exact thing*?"

One orienting note: this type is the close cousin of Function questions, which ask what the author is *doing* structurally rather than how the author *feels*. Here your only job is the feeling and its strength.

**Common mistakes.**
- **Overshooting intensity.** Picking "fierce criticism" or "ringing endorsement" when the author only hedged a doubt or granted mild approval. The single most frequent error on this type.
- **Scoring the wrong voice.** Reporting the enthusiasm of a quoted proponent or critic instead of the author's own framing around the quote.
- **Ignoring the pivot.** Reading only the first half of a "X, though Y" sentence and missing that the author's stance lives in the clause after the pivot.
- **Mis-targeting.** Answering for the passage's general mood when the stem pinned a specific claim, method, or person inside it.

**Recap.**
- Set **two dials** — direction (positive/negative/neutral) and intensity (mild/moderate/extreme) — and kill any choice that misses on *either*.
- Read the stance off **loaded modifiers, charged verbs, and hedges**, not the topic itself.
- The author's real position usually surfaces in the **clause after a pivot word** and in the **passage's final beat**.
- **Pin the target** the stem names and score the author on *that*, never the general mood or a quoted source.
- When two choices share the right direction, **take the milder one** unless strong, unhedged language is quotable.

## @attitude-from-evaluative-words

Attitude is the one question type where the core reading method pays off most directly, because the answer is decided by something you already tracked on the first read: the author's **stance**. If you ran the house routine — read for **function**, kept a one-line **skeleton**, and tagged **whose voice** owns each claim — you don't go hunting for the author's feeling when the question appears. You read it off the map you already built. This section is not a new technique; it is the reading process pointed at one target.

**Mental model.** Attitude lives in **evaluative language**, not in airtime. The right answer attaches to the author's own charged words — *flawed, compelling, overlooks, persuasive, decisive* — and never to whichever view got the most lines or happened to come last. Authors routinely spend two paragraphs building a position so they can take it apart in the third; word count is not endorsement, and final position is not endorsement. So when you reach an attitude stem, ignore how much space each view occupied and ask only: which words did the *author* spend on the target, and what charge do they carry? That is the same **point-of-view** discipline from the reading-process chapter — separate the author's voice from the reported voices — applied to the one question that punishes you hardest for blurring them.

**Worked example.** Read this short passage and tag the voices as you go. "Curators have long credited the museum's 1920s expansion to the vision of a single director. Hale's recent study argues, persuasively, that the expansion was driven instead by a tax change that made donations of art unusually attractive — a reading the surviving ledgers plainly support. Earlier accounts, by overlooking those ledgers, mistook a fiscal accident for a personal triumph." Skeleton: P-line one = old curatorial consensus; line two = Hale's tax explanation; line three = the author's verdict. Now find the author's *evaluative* words. "Persuasively" and "plainly support" sit on Hale's reading; "by overlooking... mistook" lands on the old consensus. The author isn't neutral and isn't merely reporting — the charged words show approval of Hale and a measured rejection of the consensus. Attitude toward Hale's study: **positive, moderate, persuaded.** Notice you never counted lines; you read the charge.

**Trap to watch.** Two traps share one root — scoring the wrong voice or the wrong amount. First, **attributing a reported view's tone to the author**: the enthusiasm in "Hale argues, persuasively" is half the reported scholar's energy and half the author's endorsement — but in a passage where the author *rejects* the scholar, that same enthusiasm belongs to the scholar alone, and lifting it onto the author reverses the real stance. Always re-ask: is this charged word the author's verdict, or a feeling the author is merely reporting? Second, **over-strong tone**. GMAT authors write in a measured academic register; they qualify, concede, and hedge. Labels like "contemptuous," "effusive," "wholly convinced," or "outright dismissal" almost never survive, because the text won't hand you the unhedged language they require. When two choices share the correct direction, the milder one is nearly always right — you can quote it from the page; the extreme one you'd have to import.

Then close the way you close every RC question: by **elimination**, not attraction. Don't fall in love with the choice that *sounds* most like the passage's mood. Set the author's stance from the evaluative words first, then cross off each choice that fails on direction or strength or owner — wrong sign, too hot, or scored to a reported voice. The survivor is your answer. Don't obsess over re-deriving the perfect adjective; find the four that are wrong and take the one left standing.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling up: what should you anchor the author's attitude to, and what two things must you refuse to let decide it? (Anchor to the author's own evaluative words — *flawed, compelling, overlooks, persuasive*; refuse to let airtime, or final position, decide it, and refuse to borrow a reported voice's tone.) Pulling this from memory is what makes you reach for the evaluative words under time pressure instead of for the paragraph that simply felt longest.
