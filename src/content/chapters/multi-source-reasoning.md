## @the-two-pass-navigation

Multi-Source Reasoning hands you 2-3 tabs — memos, tables, rule lists, research abstracts, meeting notes — and asks 3 questions per set. The tabs always contain far more information than any single question needs. That is deliberate. The test rewards students who extract only what is relevant, not students who try to absorb everything before question 1.

**Mental model.** Think of the tabs as a reference manual, not a chapter. Nobody reads a manual cover to cover before looking something up — you skim the table of contents, then jump to the page you need. MSR works the same way. Your job in the first pass is to learn *where things live*, not *what they say*.

Here is the trap most students fall into: they read every tab thoroughly before answering anything, burn three minutes, and remember almost none of it under pressure. MSR rewards selective reading driven by the question — not comprehensive reading driven by anxiety.

**Pass 1 — Orient (30-45 seconds total).** Scan all tabs. Build a map. Do not memorize content. For each tab, answer three quick questions:

- What kind of tab is this? (Memo, table, rule list, abstract, meeting notes?)
- What topic does it cover? ("Regional sales data" / "Board's strategic targets" / "CFO's risk assessment")
- What's the rough structure? (Rows and columns? Bullet points? Narrative prose?)

That is it. You are building a table of contents, not reading the book.

**Pass 2 — Dive in per question.** Read the question stem. Decide which tab(s) it needs. Read only those parts carefully. Answer. Then move on without re-reading tabs you have already mapped.

**Why two passes beat one careful read-through.** A 3-question set typically needs 1-2 specific data points per question — roughly 5-6 data points total out of the dozens scattered across three tabs. One thorough read processes 100% of the content to use maybe 15% of it. Two passes process 100% at low depth, then 15% at high depth. The total effort is far lower, and nothing important slips because you read the relevant 15% slowly and deliberately.

Students who deep-read all three tabs upfront often spend 3+ minutes on orientation alone, leaving under 90 seconds per question. That is not enough time for a synthesis or hypothetical question — the two types where the points actually are.

**Pass 1 tab-reading guide.**

| Tab type | What to note in 30 seconds |
|---|---|
| Memo / email | Who wrote it? Main claim? Any numbers mentioned? |
| Data table | Rows and columns? Units? Any totals row? |
| Rule / target list | How many rules? What scope? (budget, compliance, strategy) |
| Research abstract | What was studied? What result? |
| Meeting minutes | Decisions made? Open disputes? Who disagreed? |

**The "who said what" rule.** Narrative tabs often carry multiple voices — a VP's memo may quote a CFO, or meeting notes may summarize three committee members. Track who makes each claim. MSR frequently asks "based on Nguyen's memo" or "which committee member argued for X." Conflating sources is a common, avoidable error.

**Speed tip.** Numbers live in tables; reasons live in memos. When a question asks for a specific dollar figure or percentage, go straight to the table — the memo will only paraphrase it imprecisely. When it asks *why* something happened or what someone intended, go to the memo. Each source is authoritative for what it was built to convey, and routing by this rule alone cuts your search time in half.

**The time budget.**

| Phase | Time target |
|---|---|
| Pass 1 (all tabs) | 30-45 seconds |
| Per question (pass 2 + answer) | 90-120 seconds |
| Total for a 3-question set | 5-6 minutes |

If you are over 7 minutes on a single set, something broke — almost always re-reading tabs you did not need on question 2 or 3 because pass 1 was rushed or skipped.

**Micro-drill.** Picture a set with three tabs: (1) a VP's memo on a weak quarter, (2) a regional revenue table, (3) the board's strategic targets. For each question below, name the single tab you would open first — in one second, no hedging: (a) "What was Eastern's Q4 revenue?" (b) "Why did the VP say Central declined?" (c) "Did software revenue hit the board's 45% target?" Answers: (a) the table; (b) the memo; (c) two tabs — the board targets for 45%, the table for the actual share. If you reached for the memo on (a), re-read the Speed tip above.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** In one sentence, explain why orienting first actually *saves* time rather than adding a step. If you can say "because 30 seconds of mapping replaces two minutes of disorganized back-and-forth — I read the relevant 15% slowly instead of re-reading 100% in a panic," you have understood why the protocol works, not just what it is.

> **Recall check.** Close the screen. State the two-pass protocol in two sentences. Then answer: in pass 1, what specifically do you NOT try to do? (You do not memorize content — you build a navigation map.) The whole skill is distinguishing *orientation* from *comprehension*. Students who can't make that distinction re-read every tab on every question and run 2-3 minutes over time on each set.

## @matching-question-to-tab

The most common MSR question is the Simple Lookup: you need one specific piece of information from one specific tab. No computation, no synthesis. The skill being tested is pure navigation — reaching the right data point efficiently.

**Mental model.** A lookup question is a coordinate, not a puzzle. The stem names a tab (or a topic that points to a tab), a row (an entity), and a column (a metric or period). Your job is to land on that single cell. Treat it like reading a value off a map by its grid reference — you do not study the whole map, you go to the square.

**What lookup questions look like in the stem:**

- "According to [the memo / the revenue table / Tab 2]..."
- "Based on the table, what was [Region X]'s [metric] in [period]?"
- "The [person]'s [report] states that..."
- "What [amount / percentage / figure] is given for...?"

When you see any of these framings, your only job is to navigate precisely and read the correct cell or sentence.

**The three-second tab decision.** Before reading any tab carefully:

1. Does the stem name a specific tab or source? Go there directly.
2. Does it name a topic — a person, a region, a metric? Go to the tab you flagged for that topic in pass 1.
3. Ambiguous? Start with the data table. Tables answer more lookup questions than any other tab type.

**The specific-vs-general filter.**

| Question asks for | Look in |
|---|---|
| A specific number or percentage | Data table |
| A person's reasoning or intent | Narrative (memo, email) |
| Whether a rule was followed | Rules / targets tab |
| A reported result or finding | Abstract or meeting notes |

**Trap to watch.** Memos characterize data in prose; tables state it precisely. A memo says "Central region revenue declined." A lookup asking "what was Central's revenue decline?" needs the table — "$4.37M, down from $4.60M, a 5% decline" — not the memo's vague "declined." The memo and the table describe the same fact at different levels of precision. The question decides which you need; defaulting to the memo's words on a number question is how students miss easy points.

**Worked example.** A set has three tabs: a VP's internal memo, a regional revenue table, and the board's strategic targets. Watch how fast three different lookups resolve once navigation is the only step:

- "What was Eastern's revenue in Q4?" -> Table, Eastern row, Q4 column. Done in 15 seconds.
- "What rationale did the VP give for Central's decline?" -> Memo, Central discussion. Done.
- "What growth target did the board set for hardware?" -> Targets tab. Done.

The navigation *is* the skill. The reading, once you are in the right place, is trivial.

**Speed tip.** Read only the row and column you need. Scanning the whole table "for context" wastes time and seeds your memory with nearby numbers that contaminate the next question. Cover the rest of the table with your hand or your scratch paper if you have to.

**The re-read vs. recall decision.** You can sometimes answer a lookup from pass-1 memory if the fact was prominent ("which region had the highest revenue?"). For anything requiring a *specific number*, re-read. A remembered "$6.9M" might really be $6.96M or $6.09M — and on a Yes/No compliance check, that gap flips the answer.

**Micro-drill.** Same three-tab set (memo, revenue table, board targets). For each lookup, name the tab and the exact row/column or sentence you would read — out loud, in five seconds each: (a) "the board's minimum software-revenue share"; (b) "the VP's stated reason for Eastern's growth"; (c) "Western region's prior-year revenue." Then ask yourself: which of these three could you safely answer from memory, and which demands a re-read? (Only the prominent qualitative ones; any specific figure gets a re-read.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the GMAT include lookup questions if they seem easy? If you can say "because under time pressure, students who re-read all tabs instead of navigating will get lookups right but run out of clock on synthesis — efficient process is part of what's measured," you have understood the format. Lookups are the warm-up that reward process discipline, and they fund the time you need for the hard questions later in the set.

## @cross-tab-synthesis

Synthesis questions require data from two or three tabs that you collect and then combine. You are no longer just locating — you are computing across sources. This is where most MSR errors happen, and where process discipline matters most.

**Mental model.** Synthesis is a *shopping trip*, not a calculation. First you write your shopping list (every value the question needs). Then you walk the aisles (tab by tab) and put each item in the cart (on scratch paper). Only at the checkout do you total it up. Students who skip the list and try to "just compute as they go" forget an item or grab the wrong one — the math was never the problem.

**What synthesis questions look like in the stem:**

- "If the board's target is X and the table shows Y, did the company...?"
- "Based on all available information, which of the following...?"
- "Considering the memo's claim and the revenue table, what was...?"
- Any question needing a target (from a rules tab) and an actual (from a data table).
- Any question requiring you to sum, divide, or compare across sources.

**The three-step synthesis workflow.**

1. **List what you need.** Before touching a tab, enumerate every value required. Write it down: "Eastern revenue, enterprise-contract contribution, Eastern's prior-year revenue."
2. **Find each item.** Go tab by tab. Write each number next to its place on the list as you find it.
3. **Combine.** Do the arithmetic or logic on paper. Never in your head.

Step 2 is non-negotiable: write numbers as you find them. Students who try to hold 3-4 values in memory while switching tabs make errors — not because the math is hard, but because working memory is unreliable under time pressure when you are also navigating.

**Synthesis pattern 1: Target vs. Actual (compliance check).** One tab has a target or threshold; another has the actual result; the question asks whether the target was met.

**Worked example.** Compliance check. The board target: software revenue must be at least 45% of total. The table: software = $10.93M out of $24.30M total.

- Actual software share: 10.93 / 24.30 = 44.97%.
- Target: 45%. Does 44.97% meet it? No — it misses by 0.03 points.

If you had eyeballed 44.97% as "roughly 45%" and answered yes, you would be wrong. On compliance questions, do not round until *after* the comparison.

**Synthesis pattern 2: Extract, then adjust (remove or add a component).** One tab gives a total; another (or the question) gives a component to remove or add; you recompute under the adjusted figure.

**Worked example.** Carve-out. The table: Eastern's total revenue = $6.96M; prior year = $5.80M. The memo: enterprise contracts added $0.90M to Eastern's current revenue. Question: Eastern's growth rate from sources *other than* enterprise contracts?

- Adjusted current revenue: 6.96 − 0.90 = $6.06M.
- Organic growth: (6.06 − 5.80) / 5.80 = 0.26 / 5.80 ≈ 4.5%.

The headline growth (5.80 to 6.96, ≈ 20%) is mostly one contract. The organic 4.5% tells a completely different story — and that gap is exactly what the question is probing.

**Synthesis pattern 3: Aggregated metric (weighted, not simple, average).** When a question asks for a combined metric across entities of different sizes, you need weighted totals — not an average of percentages.

**Worked example.** Aggregation. "What percent of total revenue came from Software & Services?"

- Wrong approach: average each region's S&S percentage — this weights a $1M region the same as a $10M region.
- Right approach: sum S&S revenue across all regions, divide by total revenue across all regions. The denominator is total company revenue, not the count of regions.

This pattern shows up on any blend — average price, total growth rate, combined share. Default to weighted math whenever you combine across unequal entities.

**Synthesis pattern 4: Chain reasoning across three tabs.** Sometimes the logic runs through all three: Tab 1 names the entity, Tab 2 has its data, Tab 3 has the constraint. Before computing, write the chain — "entity from Tab 1 -> its metric from Tab 2 -> compared to the constraint in Tab 3" — then collect each piece and evaluate.

**Trap to watch.** When a memo says "6% decline" and the table shows 5.8%, they are not contradicting each other — they are different precision. The table is authoritative for numbers; the memo is authoritative for reasoning, attribution, and intent. Match the source to what the question asks.

**Takeaway.** Every synthesis question is "list, find, combine." The students who get these wrong almost never miss the arithmetic — they miss an item off the list or trust a number they held in their head. Externalize the data onto paper and synthesis stops being the hard part of the section.

**Micro-drill.** Name which synthesis pattern each stem is, before solving anything: (a) "If the influencer units were excluded, what was Product A's share?" (b) "Did the company hit the board's 45% software target?" (c) "What was the blended margin across all four regions?" Answers: (a) extract-then-adjust; (b) target vs. actual; (c) aggregated metric — weighted, not a simple average. Naming the pattern first tells you what to put on the shopping list.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** In one sentence, explain why pattern 3 (aggregation) almost always punishes the "average the percentages" instinct. If you can say "because a percentage carries no information about the size of the base it came from, so averaging percentages silently treats a tiny region and a huge one as equals," you will reach for weighted math automatically.

> **Recall check.** Without looking: state the three-step workflow, then explain why step 2 — writing numbers down — is the part you cannot skip. (Because tracking 3+ data points across tab switches exceeds working memory under timed conditions.) Writing a number takes two seconds; forgetting one and re-finding it costs thirty. The workflow exists because multi-tab synthesis is genuinely harder under pressure than it looks on paper.

## @conditional-and-hypothetical

Conditional questions specify a change to the situation and ask what would follow. They test whether you can update the data accurately under a new assumption — without accidentally changing things the question never touched.

**Mental model.** A hypothetical is one stone dropped in a pond. The stone is the change the question specifies. The ripples are the totals and ratios that *contain* the changed value. Everything outside the ripples stays perfectly still. Your whole job is to trace the ripples — no more, no less. Change too little and you miss a cascade; change too much and you corrupt a value the question left alone.

**What these questions look like in the stem:**

- "If [condition], what would [outcome] have been?"
- "Suppose [change]. How many targets would...?"
- "Had [entity] not been included, would...?"
- "If the [threshold] were [new value], which...?"

**Flavor 1: Data carve-out or addition.** The question removes or adds a component from a computed total, then asks you to recompute a metric.

1. Get the actual data from the tabs.
2. Apply the change (subtract, add, or replace a component).
3. Recompute the metric the question asks for.
4. Everything the question did not mention stays the same.

**Worked example.** Carve-out cascade. The table: Product A units = 42,000; total units across all products = 210,000. The memo: the influencer campaign drove 8,400 of Product A's units. Question: if the influencer units were excluded, what would Product A's share of total units be?

- Adjusted Product A units: 42,000 − 8,400 = 33,600.
- Adjusted total units: 210,000 − 8,400 = 201,600.
- Adjusted share: 33,600 / 201,600 ≈ 16.7%.

**Trap to watch.** The carve-out hits *both* the numerator and the denominator. Students who reduce only Product A's units compute 33,600 / 210,000 = 16.0% and pick the wrong choice. The removed component was part of the company total too, so the total must shrink with it. "Freeze everything else" means freeze what was not logically affected — not everything.

**Flavor 2: Rule or threshold change.** The question replaces a target, threshold, or constraint with a new value, then asks how the outcome changes.

1. Identify which entities currently pass or fail under the original rule.
2. Apply the new threshold.
3. Determine which entities switched (pass to fail, or fail to pass).
4. Recount.

**Worked example.** Threshold change. The board's hardware-mix target: at most 50%. The table: four regions at 38%, 44%, 52%, and 57% hardware. Under the 50% cap, two regions fail (52% and 57%). Question: if the cap were 60% instead, how many regions would meet it?

- Under 60%, all four regions are at or below 60% — all four qualify.
- Answer: 4.

**Speed tip.** On a threshold change, only check the entities *near* the boundary. A region at 38% is not going to flip when the cap moves from 50% to 60% — it was comfortably inside before and it still is. Work from "what could have switched," and you skip most of the recount.

**The two-part stem.** Every hypothetical stem has two halves — (1) what changed, and (2) what you are asked to compute. Confusing them is the most common error on this type. Before touching a tab, write both on scratch paper: "Change: [X]. Compute: [Y]."

**Micro-drill.** For each, state which value cascades and which stay frozen: (a) "If Region X's revenue rose by $1M, what is the new company total?" — X's revenue and every total containing X change; other regions' revenues freeze. (b) "If the growth target rose from 8% to 10%, how many regions still qualify?" — only regions between 8% and 10% can switch; recount just those. (c) "If Product B were excluded entirely, what is the new average price?" — both B's revenue and B's units leave the blend; recompute the weighted average without them.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** The carve-out trap shrinks the numerator but forgets to shrink the denominator. In one sentence, explain whether that produces a share that is too high or too low, and why. If you can say "too low — leaving the denominator too large makes the fraction smaller than it should be," you have actually traced the cascade. That is the whole trap: 33,600 / 210,000 = 16.0% sits just below the correct 33,600 / 201,600 = 16.7% — a deliberately close decoy one row away from the right answer.

> **Recall check.** Name the two flavors of hypothetical. For the carve-out type, state the cascade error and why it produces a wrong answer. (Reducing only the numerator while leaving the denominator unchanged; the removed component was also in the total, so both must drop.) This is the single highest-frequency mistake on conditional questions — drill it until the double adjustment is automatic.

## @yes-no-statement-checks

The Yes/No check presents 3-5 statements about the scenario. For each, you answer Yes (the information supports it) or No (the information contradicts it, or does not support it). The math is usually easy. The wording is where the question is won.

**Mental model.** Treat each statement as a tiny, self-contained court case. The statement is the claim; the tabs are the evidence; the *quantifier* is the standard of proof. "At least" and "more than" are different standards, the way "beyond a reasonable doubt" differs from "preponderance of evidence." You are not asked whether the claim feels true — only whether the evidence clears the exact bar the wording sets.

**The verification sequence — one statement at a time.**

1. Read the statement. Identify the specific, checkable assertion.
2. Identify which tab(s) hold the relevant data.
3. Extract the numbers or facts.
4. Check: does the data support the statement *as written*?
5. Mark Yes or No. Move to the next statement, carrying no assumptions from this one.

**Quantifier precision — the most common source of errors.**

| Quantifier | What "Yes" requires |
|---|---|
| "Exceeded by more than X" | Difference strictly > X (equality does not qualify) |
| "At least X" | Value ≥ X (equality qualifies) |
| "More than X" | Value > X (equality does not qualify) |
| "Every / all / each" | Verify EVERY instance — one exception makes it No |
| "At most X" | Value ≤ X |
| "Exactly X" | Value = X precisely (no rounding) |

**Worked example.** Precision trap. Statement: "Product B's revenue per unit exceeded $42." The table: Product B revenue = $5.88M; units = 140,000.

- Revenue per unit: 5.88M / 140,000 = $42.00 exactly.
- Does $42.00 *exceed* $42? No — "exceed" means strictly greater than.
- Answer: No.

The test writes the value as exactly $42.00 on purpose. A student who does the math correctly but reads "exceeded" loosely answers Yes. The statement would need "at least $42" or "no less than $42" for $42.00 to qualify.

**Trap to watch.** The "every / all / each" claim demands you check *every* instance. A statement like "every region achieved positive growth" covers all four (or five) regions. Most students verify two or three, see Yes, and stop. If a single region is flat or negative, the answer is No — no matter what the others did.

**Worked example.** The "every" trap. Statement: "All four regions met the board's 8% growth target." The table: North +12%, South +9%, East +20%, Central −5%.

- Three regions met it. Central did not.
- Answer: No.

Check North, South, East — all Yes — stop there, and you bubble Yes. The one case you skipped overturns the entire claim.

**The "can be determined" variant.** Some statements ask whether a claim *can be determined* from the information — a three-way judgment: Yes, No, or Cannot Be Determined. If the tabs contain the data, answer Yes or No from it. If the tabs are *silent* on what the statement claims, answer Cannot Be Determined. That is different from No: "No" means the data contradicts it; "Cannot Be Determined" means the data does not address it.

**Speed tip.** Circle the quantifier before you read the rest of the statement. The instant you know whether the bar is "strictly greater," "at least," or "every," you know exactly what test to run — and you stop yourself from doing the math first and rationalizing the wording afterward.

**Micro-drill.** Same data — North +12%, South +9%, East +20%, Central −5%; target 8%. Mark each Yes or No: (a) "At least three regions met the 8% target." (b) "Every region met the target." (c) "More than three regions grew at all." Answers: (a) Yes — exactly three is ≥ three; (b) No — Central failed; (c) Yes — all four grew except Central, so three grew; "more than three" needs four — re-check: North, South, East grew, Central shrank, so exactly three grew, and "more than three" requires four. Answer: No. If you rushed (c) to Yes, you proved the point — read the quantifier before the data.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the GMAT use a Yes/No table for MSR instead of a five-choice question? If you can say "because verifying 3-5 independent binary claims tests depth and completeness of process — a student with perfect math who checks only half the statements still loses points," you have understood the design. Precision and thoroughness *are* the skill here, not cleverness.

> **Recall check.** From memory, what does each quantifier require for a Yes: "exceeded," "at least," "more than," "every," "exactly"? Then state the difference between "No" and "Cannot Be Determined." If you can produce all of this cold, the near-miss traps lose their teeth — they only work on students who read quantifiers as decoration.

## @answer-choice-traps

MSR wrong answers are built from five recurring constructions. Recognizing them as patterns — rather than meeting each one fresh — lets you eliminate with intent instead of second-guessing.

**Trap 1: Right tab, wrong data point.** The answer uses a real number from the correct tab — but the wrong row, column, or year. A question asks for Eastern's current-year revenue; a wrong choice gives Eastern's prior-year revenue, sitting one column over in the same table. It works because the number is real and from the right region — the slip is dimensional, not a wrong source.

*Defense:* Before opening the tab, write the exact dimension you need — which entity, which period, which metric — and check that label against what you read.

**Trap 2: Right data, wrong operation.** The numbers are correct but the arithmetic answers a different question — usually a growth problem where one choice gives the absolute change and another the percentage change. Eastern grows from $5.80M to $6.96M: the decoy is $1.16M (absolute), the answer is $1.16M / $5.80M ≈ 20% (rate). The decoy is the intermediate result — a plausible place to stop.

*Defense:* Decide what the question wants — absolute change, percent change, or ratio — and write the formula *before* you fill in numbers.

**Trap 3: Plausible but unsupported (out of scope).** The choice sounds reasonable given the scenario but no tab actually says it. A memo reports declining revenue; a choice claims "the decline was due to increased competition." The memo gives numbers, not causes. Nothing in the tabs supports the competition claim.

*Defense:* Every MSR answer must be anchored to specific tab content. If you cannot point to the line, the claim is not supported — "sounds plausible" is not a Yes.

**Trap 4: Partially correct (fails on one instance you skipped).** True for most cases, false for one — frequent on "every / all / each" statements. Students verify three of four, see three Yeses, and pick Yes without checking the fourth.

*Defense:* On any universal quantifier, count the instances claimed, then verify every one. Treat it as a checklist, not a spot-check.

**Trap 5: Memory bleed (right answer, wrong set).** Across a session you work several MSR sets; a number from an earlier set's tabs bleeds into your recall for the current one. Or within a set, you remember a figure as coming from the wrong tab.

*Defense:* For any specific numerical answer, go back and read the number off the tab — unless you read it in the last ten seconds. Memory degrades fast under load. The tab is always right; your memory of the tab sometimes is not.

**Takeaway.** Four of the five traps are defeated by the same two habits you already built: write the exact dimension you need before you look, and read numbers off the tab instead of off your memory. The fifth — plausible-but-unsupported — is defeated by one question you ask of every answer: *which line in which tab says this?* If you cannot answer that, the choice is wrong, however reasonable it sounds.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Three of these five traps (1, 4, 5) are really the same failure wearing different costumes. Name the shared failure. If you can say "trusting memory or a rushed scan instead of going back to the exact cell," you see why the defense is always the same: externalize, then verify against the source.

## @summary

**The MSR workflow — two steps, every set.**

1. **Orient (30-45 seconds).** Scan all tabs. For each: what type, what topic, what structure? Build a table of contents, not a summary.
2. **Per question:** read the stem, identify which tab(s), extract the data, answer — then move on without re-reading what you have already mapped.

**The four question types — recognition and response.**

| Type | Stem signal | Key move |
|---|---|---|
| Simple Lookup | "According to..." / "What was [entity]'s [metric]?" | Navigate to one tab; read only the relevant row or sentence |
| Cross-Tab Synthesis | "Based on all information..." / target in one tab + actual in another | List every data point first; write each down before combining |
| Conditional / Hypothetical | "If [condition]..." / "Had [X] not been included..." | Change the specified value; cascade to affected totals; freeze the rest |
| Yes/No Statement Check | Statement table with a Yes/No column | Verify each statement independently; parse the quantifier strictly; check every instance claimed |

**The five highest-leverage habits.**

1. Orient before diving — 30 seconds of mapping prevents two minutes of back-and-forth.
2. Match the question to its tab before reading carefully — navigation is the core skill.
3. Write data points on scratch paper — never run multi-number synthesis in your head.
4. On "every / all / each," check every instance — one exception overturns the whole claim.
5. Circle the quantifier first — "exceeded," "at least," "more than," and "exactly" each set a different bar.

**The five wrong-answer patterns to watch.**

- Right tab, wrong data point (wrong row, column, or period).
- Right data, wrong operation (absolute change when the question wants a percentage).
- Plausible but unsupported (sounds reasonable; no tab says it).
- Partially correct (fails on the one instance you skipped).
- Memory bleed (a number recalled from the wrong set or wrong tab).

**Time budget.**

| Phase | Target |
|---|---|
| Pass 1 (orient all tabs) | 30-45 seconds |
| Per question (pass 2 + answer) | 90-120 seconds |
| 3-question set total | 5-6 minutes |

If a set runs past 7 minutes, find the moment it slipped — almost always re-reading tabs you did not need because pass 1 was rushed.

**What to do next.** Open this chapter's problem sets — Easy first to groove the two-pass protocol on low-stakes sets, then Medium. Every set shares its tabs across all its questions, so the orientation you do for question 1 pays off on 2 and 3 — you already hold the map. After each miss, write one sentence naming the question type you misread and one naming the habit that would have caught it (write the data down? circle the quantifier? check the last instance?). That annotation builds pattern-recognition faster than re-reading this chapter. Once you are clearing Medium comfortably, take the Hard set under a strict 6-minutes-per-set clock — then carry the same reflex into the full Data Insights practice and your next mock, where MSR sits alongside the other DI formats under real time pressure.
