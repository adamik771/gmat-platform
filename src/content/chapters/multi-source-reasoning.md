## @the-two-pass-navigation

**What this section builds:** The foundational MSR workflow. Learn it once; apply it on every set.

Multi-Source Reasoning gives you 2–3 tabs — memos, tables, rule lists, research abstracts, meeting notes — and asks 3 questions per set. The tabs contain far more information than any single question needs. That is by design: the test rewards students who extract only what is relevant, not students who try to absorb everything upfront.

**Mental model.** Think of your first pass through the tabs as writing a table of contents, not reading the book. You are recording *where* information lives, not *what* it says in detail. The actual reading happens in Pass 2, when a question tells you precisely what to look for.

**Pass 1 — Orient (30–45 seconds total).** Scan all tabs. For each tab, answer three questions:

- What kind of tab is this? (Memo, table, rule list, abstract, meeting notes?)
- What topic does it cover? ("Regional sales data" / "Board's strategic targets" / "CFO's risk assessment")
- What is the rough structure? (Rows and columns? Bullet points? Narrative prose?)

That is all. You are not memorizing content. You are building a navigation map.

**Pass 2 — Dive in per question.** Read the question stem. Decide which tab(s) it needs. Go directly there. Read only the relevant row, paragraph, or section. Answer.

**Why this beats one careful read-through.**

A 3-question set typically needs 5–6 specific data points out of the dozens spread across three tabs. One careful read-through processes 100% of the tabs to use maybe 15% of the content. Two passes process 100% at low depth, then the needed 15% at high depth — total effort is significantly lower, and your attention is not scattered across irrelevant numbers when you need to focus on a specific figure.

Students who deep-read all tabs upfront often spend 3+ minutes orienting alone, leaving under 90 seconds per question. That is not enough time for synthesis or hypothetical questions.

**Trap to watch.** Spending more than 60 seconds on Pass 1 is a signal that you have shifted from orientation to comprehension. If you find yourself re-reading sentences to understand an argument during Pass 1, stop. Your only job is to answer the three tab-survey questions and move on. The argument will matter when a question asks about it — and you will have the tab map to go back to it quickly.

**Pass 1 tab-reading guide.**

| Tab type | What to note in 30 seconds |
|---|---|
| Memo / email | Who wrote it? Main claim? Any numbers mentioned? |
| Data table | Rows and columns? Units? Any totals row? |
| Rule / target list | How many rules? What scope? (budget, compliance, strategy) |
| Research abstract | What was studied? What result? |
| Meeting minutes | Decisions made? Open disputes? Who disagreed? |

**The "who said what" distinction.** Narrative tabs often carry multiple voices — a VP's memo may quote a CFO, or meeting notes may summarize three committee members. Track which claim belongs to which source. MSR questions frequently ask "based on Nguyen's memo" or "which committee member argued for X." Conflating sources is a common, avoidable error.

**The "numbers live in tables" heuristic.** Memos characterize data in prose but imprecisely. When a question asks for a specific dollar figure or percentage, go to the data table. When it asks for rationale or intent, go to the narrative tab. Each source is authoritative for what it is specifically designed to convey.

**The time budget.**

| Phase | Time target |
|---|---|
| Pass 1 (all tabs) | 30–45 seconds |
| Per question (Pass 2 + answer) | 90–120 seconds |
| Total for a 3-question set | 5–6 minutes |

If a set runs past 7 minutes, diagnose where the time went. In nearly every case: re-reading tabs you did not need on question 2 or 3, because Pass 1 was rushed or skipped. A 30-second investment in orientation pays for itself multiple times over.

> **Recall check.** In two sentences, state the two-pass protocol. Then answer: in Pass 1, what specifically do you *not* try to do? (Answer: you do not try to memorize content or understand arguments — you build a navigation map of where information lives.) The test of whether you have internalized this: when you feel the impulse to slow down and re-read during Pass 1, you catch it and keep moving.

## @matching-question-to-tab

**Question type: Simple Lookup.** The most common MSR question type. You need one specific piece of information from one specific tab. The skill tested is navigation — reaching the right data point efficiently. No computation required.

**What lookup question stems look like:**

- "According to [the memo / the revenue table / Tab 2]..."
- "Based on the table, what was [Region X]'s [metric] in [period]?"
- "The [person]'s [memo/report] states that..."
- "What [amount / percentage / figure] is given for..."

When you see these framings, your only job is to navigate precisely to the correct tab and read the correct cell or sentence.

**The three-second tab decision.** Before reading any tab carefully:

1. Does the stem name a specific tab or source? Go there directly.
2. Does it name a topic — a person, a region, a metric? Go to the tab you flagged for that topic in Pass 1.
3. Ambiguous? Start with the data table. Tables answer more lookup questions than any other tab type.

**Pro tip.** Lookup questions are intentionally fast — 20 to 30 seconds each if you navigate correctly. If a lookup question is taking you more than a minute, the problem is navigation, not reasoning. You are searching rather than locating. That is the cost of a weak Pass 1. Your Pass 1 investment is what makes lookup questions feel easy.

**The specific-vs-general filter.**

| Question asks for | Look in |
|---|---|
| A specific number or percentage | Data table |
| A person's reasoning or intent | Narrative (memo, email) |
| Whether a rule was followed | Rules / targets tab |
| A reported result or finding | Abstract or meeting notes |

**The re-read vs. recall decision.** For lookup questions, you can sometimes answer from Pass 1 memory if the data point was prominent. For anything requiring a specific number, go back to the tab. A remembered "$6.9M" might be $6.96M or $6.09M — and on Yes/No questions, that difference can determine the answer.

**Trap to watch.** Memos frequently characterize data in prose: "Central region revenue declined significantly." A lookup question asking "what was the Central region's revenue decline?" requires the table number, not the memo's characterization. The memo says "declined significantly"; the table says "$4.37M, down from $4.60M — a 5% decline." These are different levels of precision. The question tells you which level you need. Do not substitute a memo description for a table figure when the question is asking for a specific number.

**Partial-tab reading discipline.** When you go to the data table for a lookup, read only the relevant row and column. Scanning the whole table introduces distracting numbers that can contaminate your answer — you may misremember which figure you were retrieving.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the GMAT include lookup questions if they seem straightforward? (Answer: under time pressure, students who re-read all tabs rather than navigating precisely will answer lookup questions correctly but run out of time on synthesis questions. Lookup questions reward process discipline, not just knowledge. Efficient navigation is part of what the format is testing — how you handle lookups determines how much time you have left for what follows.)

## @cross-tab-synthesis

**Question type: Cross-Tab Synthesis.** Requires data from 2–3 tabs that you combine — locating, collecting, and computing. This is where most MSR errors occur, and where scratch paper is non-negotiable.

**What synthesis question stems look like:**

- "If the board's target is X and the table shows Y, did the company..."
- "Based on all available information, which of the following..."
- "Considering the memo's claim and the revenue table, what was..."
- Any question requiring a target from one tab and an actual from another
- Any question where you need to sum, divide, or compare across sources

**Mental model.** Treat a synthesis question like a shopping list. Before going to any tab, read the question and write down every item you need to collect: "Eastern revenue (Tab 2), enterprise contract contribution (Tab 1), Eastern prior-year revenue (Tab 2)." Then collect them one by one, writing each value as you find it. Only combine once all items are on paper. Students who try to hold multiple values in memory while tab-switching make arithmetic errors — not because the math is hard, but because working memory under time pressure is unreliable.

**The three-step synthesis workflow:**

1. List every data point the question needs — write it down before touching any tab.
2. Find each item — go tab by tab, write the number the moment you find it.
3. Combine — do the arithmetic on paper.

Step 2 is the one you cannot skip. Writing takes two seconds; recollecting a forgotten number costs thirty and disrupts your entire computation chain.

**Synthesis pattern 1: Target vs. Actual (compliance check).**

One tab has a target or threshold. Another has the actual result. The question asks whether the target was met.

**Worked example.** Tab 3: board target — software revenue must be at least 45% of total. Tab 2: software revenue = $10.93M; total revenue = $24.30M.

- Actual software share: $10.93M ÷ $24.30M = 44.97%
- Target: 45%
- 44.97% < 45%. Misses by 0.03 percentage points. Answer: No.

**Trap to watch.** On compliance questions, the answer is often right at the boundary — the test deliberately places the actual value within 0.1 to 0.5 percentage points of the threshold. Students who round intermediate values land on the wrong side. If you had approximated 44.97% as "roughly 45%," you would answer incorrectly. Keep full precision through the computation; round only at the final comparison, and only if "approximately" appears in the question.

**Synthesis pattern 2: Extract, then adjust (remove or add a component).**

One tab gives a total. Another (or the question itself) gives a component to remove or add. You compute the metric under the adjusted figure.

Tab 2: Eastern region revenue = $6.96M; prior year = $5.80M. Tab 1: enterprise contracts contributed $0.90M to Eastern's current revenue. Question: "What was Eastern's growth rate from non-enterprise sources?"

- Adjusted current revenue: $6.96M − $0.90M = $6.06M
- Organic growth: ($6.06M − $5.80M) ÷ $5.80M = $0.26M ÷ $5.80M ≈ 4.5%

The headline growth of roughly 20% is driven by a single enterprise contract. The 4.5% organic rate tells a completely different story. This is the insight the question probes.

**Synthesis pattern 3: Aggregated metric (weighted, not simple, average).**

When a question asks for a combined metric across multiple entities of different sizes, you need weighted totals — not averages of percentages.

Wrong approach: average each region's software share percentage. That weights a $1M region identically to a $10M region.

Correct approach: sum software revenue across all regions ÷ total revenue across all regions.

This applies to any aggregation across entities of different scales: average price, total growth rate, blended margin, combined share. Default to weighted arithmetic whenever entities differ in size.

**Synthesis pattern 4: Chain reasoning across three tabs.**

Sometimes the logic runs through all three: Tab 1 identifies the entity → Tab 2 has its data → Tab 3 has the relevant constraint.

Before computing, write the chain: "I need [entity from Tab 1] → its [metric from Tab 2] → compared against [constraint from Tab 3]." Collect each piece, then evaluate.

**The "conflicting sources" rule.** Sometimes a memo says "6% decline" and the table shows 5.8%. The table is authoritative for numbers; the memo is authoritative for reasoning, attribution, and intent. If the question asks for a specific figure, use the table. If it asks why something occurred, use the memo.

> **Recall check.** Without looking: state the three-step synthesis workflow. Then explain why step 2 — writing numbers as you find them — is the step you cannot skip. (Answer: multi-tab collection under time pressure exceeds reliable working memory. Writing takes two seconds; recovering a lost number costs thirty and breaks your computation chain. The discipline is the whole point of the workflow.)

## @conditional-and-hypothetical

**Question type: Conditional / Hypothetical.** These questions specify a change to the situation and ask what would follow. They test whether you can update data accurately under a new assumption — without accidentally changing things the question left unchanged.

**What these question stems look like:**

- "If [condition], what would [outcome] have been?"
- "Suppose [change]. How many targets would..."
- "Had [entity] not been included, would..."
- "If the [rule / threshold] were [new value], which..."
- "Assuming [X], what is the revised [metric]?"

**The two flavors of hypothetical.**

**Flavor 1: Data carve-out or addition.**

The question removes or adds a component from a computed total, then asks you to recompute a metric.

Template: get the actual data → apply the specified change → cascade to affected totals → recompute the metric → leave everything the question did not mention unchanged.

Tab 2: Product A total units = 42,000. Total units across all products = 210,000. Tab 1: influencer campaign units for Product A = 8,400.

Question: "If the influencer campaign units were excluded, what would Product A's share of total unit sales have been?"

- Adjusted Product A units: 42,000 − 8,400 = 33,600
- Adjusted total units: 210,000 − 8,400 = 201,600
- Product A's adjusted share: 33,600 ÷ 201,600 ≈ 16.7%

**Trap to watch.** The cascade error — reducing only the numerator while leaving the denominator unchanged — is the most common mistake on carve-out questions. When 8,400 units are removed from Product A, they are also removed from the company total. The student who computes 33,600 ÷ 210,000 ≈ 16.0% picks the wrong answer. Before computing any carve-out, ask: does this change also affect the denominator? On carve-out questions, the answer is almost always yes.

**Flavor 2: Rule or threshold change.**

The question replaces a target, threshold, or constraint with a new value, then asks how the outcome changes.

Template: identify which entities pass or fail under the original rule → apply the new threshold → determine which entities switched → recount.

Tab 3: board's hardware mix target = maximum 50%. Tab 2: four regions at 38%, 44%, 52%, and 57%. Under 50%: two regions fail (52%, 57%). Question: "If the board's target were 60%, how many regions would meet it?" Under 60%: all four qualify. Answer: 4.

**Key takeaway.** In any hypothetical, change only what the question specifies. Cascade only to values logically downstream of that change. If Region X's revenue increases by $1M: adjust Region X's revenue, adjust any totals that include Region X, recompute ratios built from those totals. Do not touch any other region's individual figures.

**Reading hypothetical stems precisely.** The stem has two parts: (1) what changed, and (2) what to compute. Write both on scratch paper before touching any tab — "Change: [X]. Compute: [Y]." Conflating the two is the most common setup error on this question type.

**Trap to watch.** Hypothetical questions often produce answers close to round numbers. If you round $8,400 to $8,000 during computation, your final answer shifts enough to land on the wrong answer choice. Keep full precision through all intermediate steps; round only at the final comparison, and only if "approximately" appears in the question.

> **Recall check.** What are the two flavors of hypothetical question? On the data carve-out type: what is the cascade error, and what check prevents it? (Answer: the cascade error is reducing only the numerator while leaving the denominator unchanged, because the removed component was also part of the total. The check: before computing, ask explicitly whether the change also affects the denominator. On carve-out questions, it almost always does — and missing it shifts the answer enough to select a wrong choice.)

## @yes-no-statement-checks

**Question type: Yes/No Statement Check.** The question presents 3–5 statements about the multi-tab scenario. For each, you answer Yes (the available information supports it as written) or No (the available information contradicts it, or is insufficient to confirm it).

**What these questions look like:**

- A table with 3–4 rows, each containing a statement and a Yes / No selection
- Stems like "Based on the available data, indicate whether each of the following statements is accurate"

**Mental model.** Treat each statement as a claim you are asked to verify against evidence — not "does this sound plausible?" but "does the data in the tabs support this specific claim, worded exactly this way?" Any gap between what the tabs say and what the statement claims is a No. Plausibility is not support.

**The verification sequence — one statement at a time:**

1. Read the statement. Identify the specific, checkable assertion.
2. Identify which tab(s) hold the relevant data.
3. Extract the numbers or facts.
4. Check: does the data support the statement as written, including its quantifier?
5. Mark Yes or No. Move to the next statement independently.

Each statement is completely independent. Do not carry assumptions or partial results from one to the next.

**Quantifier precision — the most frequent source of errors.**

| Quantifier | What "Yes" requires |
|---|---|
| "Exceeded by more than X" | Difference strictly > X (equality does not qualify) |
| "At least X" | Value ≥ X (equality qualifies) |
| "More than X" | Value > X (equality does not qualify) |
| "Every / all / each" | Must verify every instance — one exception makes it No |
| "At most X" | Value ≤ X |
| "Exactly X" | Value = X precisely; no rounding |

**Trap to watch.** "Exceeded" means strictly greater than. A statement claiming "Product B's revenue per unit exceeded $42" where the actual figure is exactly $42.00 is a No. The test writes the statement this way intentionally — students who read "exceeded" loosely as "was at least" will mark Yes on a No. Before answering any statement with a comparative quantifier, pause on the exact word and apply its precise logical meaning.

**Worked example.** Tab 2: Product B revenue = $5.88M; units sold = 140,000.

Revenue per unit: $5.88M ÷ 140,000 = $42.00 exactly.

Statement: "Product B's revenue per unit exceeded $42."

$42.00 does not exceed $42 — "exceeded" means strictly greater than.

Answer: **No.**

**The "every" trap in detail.** A statement like "every region achieved positive revenue growth" requires checking all four (or five) regions — not just the first few you look at.

**Trap to watch.** Most students check two or three instances, see positive results, and stop. The test places the one exception in the position you are most likely to skip — the last row, the smallest region, the edge case. Before answering any statement with "every," "all," or "each," count how many instances the claim covers, then verify all of them. Treat it as a checklist, not a spot-check.

**The "cannot be determined" variant.** Some questions ask whether a statement *can be determined* from the available information. Three-way judgment: Yes (tabs support it), No (tabs contradict it), or Cannot Be Determined (tabs are silent on what the statement claims). "No" and "Cannot Be Determined" are distinct. "No" means the data says otherwise. "Cannot Be Determined" means the data does not address the claim at all.

**Synthesis within statements.** Many statements require combining data from two tabs. "Software revenue grew faster than hardware revenue" requires software growth rate and hardware growth rate — two separate lookups, then a comparison. Apply the same collection discipline as cross-tab synthesis: list what you need, find each item, then compare.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the GMAT use Yes/No format for MSR rather than five-choice format? (Answer: multi-tab verification produces 3–5 independent binary judgments, each requiring a locate-and-check operation. The format tests depth of process — a student who checks only 2 of 4 statements carefully will miss some, even if their math was correct on those two. Completeness and precision together are what the format measures.)

## @answer-choice-traps

MSR wrong answers are built around five recurring patterns. Recognizing them as patterns — not surprises — lets you eliminate deliberately rather than second-guess at the end of a set.

**Trap to watch.** Right tab, wrong data point. The answer uses a real number from the correct tab — but from the wrong row, column, or year. A question asks for Eastern region's current-year revenue; a wrong choice gives Eastern's prior-year revenue, visible one column over in the same table. The trap works because the number is real and from the right region — the error is a dimensional slip, not a wrong source. Defense: before going to the tab, write down the exact dimension the question requires — which entity, which period, which metric. Verify your answer matches all three before selecting it.

**Trap to watch.** Right data, wrong operation. The correct numbers are retrieved; the arithmetic is wrong. Common form: a question asks for a percentage growth rate; one wrong choice gives the absolute dollar change. Eastern grew from $5.80M to $6.96M — wrong answer: $1.16M (the absolute difference); correct answer: $1.16M ÷ $5.80M ≈ 20% (the growth rate). The wrong answer is the intermediate calculation, a plausible stopping point. Defense: confirm what the question asks for — absolute change, percentage change, or ratio — before computing. Write the formula on scratch paper, then fill in the numbers.

**Trap to watch.** Plausible but unsupported (out of scope). An answer makes a claim that sounds reasonable given the scenario but is not supported by any tab. Common on synthesis and Yes/No questions. A memo describes declining revenue in one region; a wrong answer says "the decline was due to increased competition." The memo gives numbers, not causes. Nothing in the tabs supports the competition claim. Defense: every answer to an MSR question must be anchored in specific tab content. If you cannot point to the line in the tab, the claim is not supported. "Sounds plausible" is not a Yes.

**Trap to watch.** Partially correct (fails on one instance you did not check). An answer is true for most instances but false for one. Frequent on "every / all / each" Yes/No statements. Students verify three of four cases, see three yeses, and select Yes without checking the one exception that overturns the claim. Defense: on any statement with a universal quantifier, count the instances it covers, then verify every one before answering. The missed case is the point of the question.

**Trap to watch.** Memory bleed (a number from the wrong set or the wrong tab). Within a session you may work multiple MSR sets. A figure from an earlier set's tabs can contaminate retrieval for the current set. Or within a single set, a number from one tab gets recalled as if it came from another. Defense: for any specific figure, go back to the tab and read the number directly rather than recalling it from memory — unless you read it in the last ten seconds. The tab is always authoritative; your memory of the tab sometimes is not.

> **Recall check.** Name all five wrong-answer patterns without looking. Then: for the "partially correct" trap, what is the specific behavior that produces the error? (Answer: verifying only a subset of the instances a universal quantifier covers, then answering as if all cases were confirmed. The test places the exception in a position likely to be skipped — the last row, the smallest region, the edge case. The defense is to count instances first, then verify all of them before committing to Yes.)

## @summary

**The MSR workflow — two steps, on every set.**

1. **Orient (30–45 seconds).** Scan all tabs. For each: what type? what topic? what structure? Build a navigation map, not a content summary.
2. **Per question:** Read stem → identify required tab(s) → extract data → answer.

**The four question types — recognition and response.**

| Type | Stem signal | Key move |
|---|---|---|
| Simple Lookup | "According to..." / "What was [entity]'s [metric]?" | Navigate to the correct tab; read only the relevant row or sentence |
| Cross-Tab Synthesis | "Based on all information..." / requires target + actual from different tabs | List all data points first; write each value before combining |
| Conditional / Hypothetical | "If [condition]..." / "Had [X] not been included..." | Apply the change; cascade to affected totals; freeze everything else |
| Yes/No Statement Check | Statement table with Yes/No column | Verify each statement independently; parse quantifiers strictly; check every instance |

**The five wrong-answer patterns — at a glance.**

- Right tab, wrong data point (wrong row, column, or period)
- Right data, wrong operation (absolute change when the question wants percentage, or vice versa)
- Plausible but unsupported (sounds reasonable; no tab actually says it)
- Partially correct (fails on one instance you skipped)
- Memory bleed (a number from the wrong set or wrong tab)

**Time budget.**

| Phase | Target |
|---|---|
| Pass 1 (orient all tabs) | 30–45 seconds |
| Per question (Pass 2 + answer) | 90–120 seconds |
| 3-question set total | 5–6 minutes |

If a set runs past 7 minutes, identify the moment it slipped. Almost always: re-reading tabs you did not need because Pass 1 was rushed or skipped.

**The five highest-leverage habits.**

1. Orient before diving — 30 seconds of tab-mapping prevents 2 minutes of disorganized re-reading.
2. Match question to tab before reading carefully — navigation is the core skill.
3. Write data points on scratch paper — never do multi-number synthesis in your head.
4. Check every instance on "every / all / each" statements — one exception overturns the entire claim.
5. Parse quantifiers precisely — "exceeded," "at least," "more than," and "exactly" are not interchangeable.

**What to do next.**

The two-pass protocol becomes a reflex only through repetition on actual question sets. Work through the problem sets below in order — easy through hard. By the hard set, the orientation pass should feel like an automatic 30-second scan, not a procedure you consciously invoke.

After the problem sets, your error log is your fastest teacher. Tag every MSR miss with one of the five wrong-answer patterns above. Within three tagged misses of the same type, you will start catching that pattern before it catches you.

If MSR is a significant weak area: the Cross-Tab Synthesis and Conditional / Hypothetical sections are where the hardest points concentrate. Re-read those, then work the hard problem set again. Your analytics page will show which question types are pulling your accuracy down — use that to decide where to focus next.
