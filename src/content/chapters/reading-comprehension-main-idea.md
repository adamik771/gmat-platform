## @main-idea-test

Main idea questions ask: "What is the primary purpose of the passage?" or "Which of the following best summarizes the passage's central argument?"

**Mental model.** The main idea is not the *topic* — it's the author's *claim about* the topic, and it has to span the entire passage. Picture the passage as an arc: setup, development, payoff. The right answer traces the whole arc; every wrong answer either covers one paragraph, floats above the passage as generic topic, or gets the author's stance backwards.

**The test.** The correct answer captures the author's central thesis — the single claim that unifies every paragraph. It is *not* a summary of the topic; it is the specific *claim* the author is making about the topic.

**The three filters for eliminating wrong main-idea answers.**

1. **Too narrow.** Describes only one paragraph or one aspect of the passage. Wrong.
2. **Too broad.** Describes the general topic area without the author's specific claim. Wrong.
3. **Off-stance.** Gets the author's attitude wrong (e.g., says the author "advocates" when the author actually "critiques"). Wrong.

Only the answer that is *specific enough* to capture the author's claim, *broad enough* to cover all paragraphs, AND *matches the author's stance* is correct.

**Example.** Passage: Introduces subscription economy, discusses the "leaky bucket" customer retention problem, surveys three strategies (dark patterns, personalization, genuine switching costs). Concludes that the most successful companies build real switching costs.

Candidate main-idea answers:

- A) "Advocates subscription models over traditional transactions." — Too narrow AND wrong stance (passage is critical, not advocating).
- B) "Examines the challenges subscription businesses face in retaining customers and the strategies they use." — **Correct.** Captures the problem (retention challenge) and the range of responses (three strategies). Right scope, right stance.
- C) "Criticizes companies that use dark patterns." — Too narrow (dark patterns is one brief mention).
- D) "Argues that subscription businesses are ultimately unsustainable." — Overstates the critique.
- E) "Compares the revenue predictability of subscription vs traditional models." — Describes only the first paragraph.

**Micro-drill.** Passage arc: P1 introduces the rise of remote work; P2 cites evidence it raises measured productivity; P3 argues those gains may be illusory because they ignore lost mentoring and slower innovation; the author concludes firms should weigh the hidden long-term costs. For each candidate, name the filter it fails or mark it correct. 60 seconds:

1. "Describes the recent rise of remote work." → ___
2. "Argues that remote work eliminates a company's capacity to innovate." → ___
3. "Questions whether remote work's measured productivity gains hold up once hidden long-term costs are counted." → ___

Answers: (1) **Too broad** — that's the topic, not the author's claim. (2) **Off-stance / too strong** — the author says gains *may* be illusory and that costs deserve weighing, not that innovation is *eliminated*. (3) **Correct** — covers the full arc (productivity gains *and* hidden costs) in the author's measured, questioning voice.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why is the main idea not the same as the topic? If you can explain "because the topic is *what the passage is about*, while the main idea is *what the author claims about that topic*," you've internalized the distinction and will stop picking overly broad "the passage discusses X" answers.

## @main-idea-stance

Two fast checks turn main idea from a judgment call into a mechanical elimination: the answer's verb, and where in the passage the claim lives.

**The "verb test" on main-idea answers.** Main-idea answers typically start with a verb: "explains," "argues," "examines," "critiques," "defends," "compares." The verb has to match the author's actual move. If the passage presents multiple views without endorsing one, the verb is *explores* or *surveys*, not *argues*. If the passage argues for one position, the verb is *contends* or *argues*.

**The "author's attitude" match.** Main idea answers have to match the author's stance:

- Neutral / descriptive passage → "discusses," "examines," "outlines"
- Mildly positive → "supports," "endorses"
- Critical → "criticizes," "challenges," "questions"
- Balanced / exploratory → "weighs," "compares," "considers"

If the passage is critical and the answer says "supports," it's wrong.

**The "where's the main idea located" question.** Main ideas are most often in the first paragraph (as a thesis statement) or the last paragraph (as a conclusion). Sometimes both. The middle paragraphs provide evidence and counter-views. If you can't find the main idea in paragraph 1, check the last paragraph.

**The "both halves" trap on main-idea answers.** Some passages have a two-part structure: first discussing X, then discussing Y. Main-idea answers that mention only X or only Y are wrong. The right answer captures both halves.

> **Recall check.** Without looking, state the three filters that eliminate wrong main-idea answers. Now: if a passage presents three competing theories without endorsing any, which verb should the correct answer use — "argues that" or "surveys"? (Answer: "surveys" — the author isn't advancing one position, so an "argues that" answer overstates the author's move.) The verb test alone resolves a large share of close two-answer splits.

## @summary

The main idea is the author's specific claim, spanning the whole passage, in the author's own stance. Find it from your scope-and-stance read; verify it with the filters and the verb test.

**The process.**

1. From your read, state the passage's arc in one sentence.
2. Eliminate too-narrow (one paragraph), too-broad (topic only), and off-stance answers.
3. Check the surviving answer's verb against what the author actually does.

**What to do next.** Drill the problem sets below. When you miss one, name which filter the wrong answer beat you with — narrow, broad, or stance. Next chapter: **Specific Detail** — where the discipline flips from big-picture to precise line-citation.
