## @function

**"The author mentions X in order to..."** or "The function of the second paragraph is to..." or "The passage's reference to Y serves to..."

**Mental model.** A function question is asking about the *job* of a sentence, not its meaning. Read off your structure pass: every element was included to introduce, support, counter, qualify, or transition. The right answer names that job in the abstract; every paraphrase of the content is a trap.

**The template.** The answer is the *role* a specific element plays in the passage's structure — not what the element *is*, but what the *author is doing* by including it.

**Common functions to recognize:**

| Function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Provide an example | Illustrate a general claim with a concrete case |
| Introduce a counterargument | Present a view the author will rebut |
| Strengthen the main claim | Add evidence or an analogy |
| Qualify or limit the main claim | Acknowledge a boundary or exception |
| Transition between ideas | Shift from one claim to another |
| Establish a baseline | Set up a standard against which something is measured |
| Illustrate a contrast | Show how two things differ |

**The "role, not content" test.** A function answer describes what the author is DOING, not what the text SAYS. If an answer just paraphrases the content, it's wrong.

**Example.** Passage discusses subscription economy challenges. In paragraph 3, the author mentions that some companies use "dark patterns" to prevent cancellation.

Function question: "The author mentions dark patterns in order to..."

- A) "Describe a common business practice." — Too neutral; misses the author's *critical* use.
- B) "Illustrate a response to the retention problem that contrasts with more effective approaches." — **Correct.** The author is contrasting dark patterns with "genuine switching costs."
- C) "Define a technical term in the subscription industry." — Wrong; the author isn't teaching vocabulary.
- D) "Argue that dark patterns are illegal." — Wrong; the passage makes no legal claim.

**The "both sides of a contrast" clue.** When the author mentions something alongside a contrast word ("however," "in contrast," "while"), the function is usually to set up or illustrate the contrast.

**Micro-drill.** Name the function each sentence performs, using the roles table. 60 seconds:

1. In a passage praising a technology's maturation: "To be sure, early adopters paid far more for markedly inferior versions." → ___
2. After a general claim that some nations transition energy systems rapidly: "Consider Iceland, which shifted largely to geothermal within two decades." → ___
3. In a passage arguing a flood-control policy worked: "Critics counter that the data come from a single, unusually dry year." → ___

Answers: (1) **Qualify / concede** — "To be sure" grants a limitation before the author defends the technology. (2) **Provide an example** — "Consider Iceland" illustrates the general claim with a concrete case. (3) **Introduce a counterargument** — "Critics counter" presents a view the author will likely rebut. Each is read from the *signal phrase* and the role, not the content.

## @attitude

**"The author's attitude toward X is best described as..."**

**The template.** Identify the author's stance — enthusiasm, skepticism, neutral, critical, measured, etc. Match to the answer choice with the closest match.

**The attitude spectrum:**

| Tone | Word choices | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Enthusiastic | "crucial," "revolutionary," "compelling" | "represents a fundamental advance" |
| Supportive | "valuable," "well-supported," "sound" | "provides strong evidence" |
| Neutral | "describes," "notes," "explains" | "reports that X is the case" |
| Measured / cautious | "plausible but limited," "possible though incomplete" | "the evidence suggests but does not prove" |
| Skeptical | "questionable," "unclear," "unconvincing" | "remains to be demonstrated" |
| Critical | "flawed," "oversimplified," "misleading" | "overlooks the central difficulty" |

**Reading for tone: the adjective filter.** The author's word choice gives stance away. "Striking results" ≠ "purported results." "Well-documented" ≠ "allegedly shown." Pay attention to *modifiers* on noun phrases — they encode the author's attitude.

**The "qualifying language" signal.** Authors who are critical or uncertain use qualifying language: "may," "might," "seems to," "in some cases." Authors who are confident use direct language: "is," "does," "will." Heavy qualifiers = cautious stance; no qualifiers = confident stance.

**The "extreme answers usually wrong" pattern on attitude questions.** Answers like "enthusiastic endorsement" or "vehement condemnation" are usually wrong on GMAT RC. Authors rarely take extreme positions in these passages — they're typically measured. The right answer usually falls in the middle of the spectrum: "cautious support," "measured skepticism," "qualified endorsement."

**The "at what" question.** Be specific about what the attitude is *toward*. The author might be enthusiastic about a theory but skeptical of a specific claim within it. "The author's attitude toward X" requires identifying what X is and reading the author's stance *specifically on X*.

> **Recall check.** Without looking, list five positions on the attitude spectrum (enthusiastic, supportive, neutral, measured, skeptical, critical — any five). Now: which end of the spectrum is most likely the right answer on a GMAT attitude question? (Answer: the middle — cautious, measured, qualified tones. Extreme positions are rare.) This bias toward the middle is what saves you from over-picking "strongly endorses" or "harshly criticizes" answers.

## @summary

Function and attitude are both read straight from how the author writes. Function = the job a sentence does in the structure; attitude = the tone the author's modifiers reveal.

**Function — the process.** Locate the element, name its role in the abstract (example, counter, qualify, transition, contrast), and reject any answer that merely paraphrases the content.

**Attitude — the process.** Read the modifiers and qualifiers, place the tone on the spectrum, and favor the middle — extreme tones are rarely correct. Pin down what the attitude is *toward*.

**What to do next.** Drill the problem sets below; on function misses, check whether you picked a content paraphrase instead of a role, and on attitude misses, whether you reached for an extreme tone.
