## @shape-of-the-section

Before you study a single argument, know exactly what you are walking into. GMAT Focus Verbal is **23 questions in 45 minutes**. Do the division: `45 / 23` comes out just under two minutes per question — about `1:57` on paper. That number is the section's whole personality. It is enough time to think clearly once — the budget assumes your first pass at a stimulus is your only full pass.

The section contains exactly **two question types: Critical Reasoning and Reading Comprehension**. That is the full list. **Sentence Correction is gone** — the Focus Edition removed it entirely, and with it the grammar-heavy half of old-GMAT Verbal prep. If you have inherited older materials full of idiom lists and subject-verb drills, close them. None of it is on this test anymore.

Register what that removal means, because it changed who this section favors. Sentence Correction was the one place where a native speaker's ear was worth raw points. That edge no longer exists: CR and RC reward comprehension speed and structural reasoning, and both are trained, not inherited. More on this below — it is the most underrated fact about Focus Verbal.

The rest of the mechanics:

- **Scoring.** Verbal is scored on its own **60-90 scale** and carries **exactly the same weight** as Quant and Data Insights in your 205-805 total. A Verbal point buys exactly as much as a Quant point — budget your prep hours accordingly.
- **Adaptivity.** The section adapts to your answers — accurate work buys harder questions. Do not try to read difficulty as feedback; just answer the question on the screen.
- **Review and edit.** You can bookmark questions as you go, revisit them at the end of the section, and **change up to three answers**. That is a genuine strategic tool — the timing chapter at the end of the path sets exact rules for it.
- **Question delivery.** CR questions arrive self-contained: one short argument per screen. RC arrives in clusters — one passage feeding several questions — so your time per question is *supposed* to be uneven.

> **Recall check.** Two numbers and a tool, from memory: the per-question time budget, and how many answers the end-of-section review lets you change. (About `1:57` each; up to three changes, fed by the bookmarks you set as you go.) Say it before reading on; retrieval now is what makes it automatic later.

## @what-each-family-rewards

The two question families look different on screen, but the test grades them with the same muscle. Here is what each one actually pays for.

**Critical Reasoning** gives you a short argument — a few sentences of evidence driving toward a conclusion — and a stem that names a precise logical task: find the assumption, strengthen the argument, weaken it, draw the inference, evaluate it, name the flaw, resolve the paradox, describe the boldface, complete the argument. The argument changes every time; the task list does not. Each of those tasks is a recurring template with its own chapter later in the path, and CR mastery is mostly the ability to recognize which template is on screen and run its move instead of improvising.

**Reading Comprehension** gives you a dense passage and several questions about it. What it rewards is **structure-first reading**: knowing why each paragraph exists, where the author stands, and which sentence carries the load — *before* you see a single question. The questions then test whether you built that map. Students who read RC the way they read a textbook, absorbing detail, arrive at the questions with everything memorized and nothing usable.

| | Critical Reasoning | Reading Comprehension |
|---|---|---|
| You get | One short argument, self-contained | One dense passage, a cluster of questions |
| The stem asks | A named logical task | A question about purpose, structure, or content |
| It rewards | Seeing the gap between evidence and conclusion | A map of what each paragraph is doing |
| It punishes | Answering from feel instead of from the task | Reading for detail, then hunting blind |

Now the part that should shape your study plan: **both families are skills, not knowledge.** A Quant content gap patches in an evening — learn the exponent rules, drill a set, gap closed. Verbal does not patch; it conditions. Drilling works, but like training mileage, not like installing software: reps with feedback gradually change *how you read*, and the change compounds over weeks. There is no formula sheet to cram in the final stretch.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why would a reading skill respond to spaced, repeated practice when it barely responds to a single long cram session? Put the mechanism in your own words — if you believe the *why*, you will actually run Verbal continuously instead of postponing it, which is the single most common Verbal planning mistake.

The practical implication: start Verbal early and never put it down — the guided path interleaves Verbal between Quant and DI for exactly this reason.

## @the-non-native-angle

This is the section I wish someone had written for me when I was scoring 565. English is not my first language, and I was certain my Verbal problem was "my English." So I checked: untimed, my accuracy was fine. I could dissect the arguments, I could follow the passages. Under the clock, everything collapsed — because I was reading every stimulus one and a half times. The gap was never comprehension. It was **comprehension speed**: how fast you extract the structure on the first pass. And speed, unlike a childhood spent speaking English, is trainable.

That distinction is worth money on this test, so verify it for yourself instead of taking my word. The platform's error log makes you name what actually broke on every miss — not just "wrong," but whether the reasoning failed, the reading failed, or the clock did. Tag every Verbal miss honestly and watch where they cluster.

- If your misses cluster on the **reasoning** — you mapped the argument wrong, or the right answer's scope surprised you — the logic itself is the gap, and the CR task-family chapters fix that directly.
- If they cluster on **misreads and pacing** — an ignored qualifier, a detail pulled out wrong, questions that ran long, panic guesses at the end — and for most non-native speakers they do — your reasoning is fine and your reading throughput is the work. That is the good news: throughput responds to volume better than almost any other skill on this exam.

In review, never close a miss with "got it wrong because English." That is a surrender, not a diagnosis. Find what happened: a misparsed sentence or a missed qualifier is a **misread**, and the fix is slowing down on that sentence *type*; running long or guessing under the clock is a **pacing miss**, and the fix is pacing rules, not vocabulary; falling for a tempting wrong answer is a **trap**, and the answer-trap chapters exist for it. Each cause has a different cure, and "my English" points to none of them.

And remember what the format already told you: the Focus Edition deleted the only question type that asked whether English *sounds right* to you. Logic has no native speakers.

> **Recall check.** Which two clusters of error-log causes diagnose a reading-speed gap, as opposed to a logic gap? (The misread and pacing clusters — ignored qualifiers, extraction slips, time sinks, panic guesses. If your misses live there, drill throughput, not grammar.)

## @the-verbal-game-plan

The platform's curriculum is one guided path of 50+ chapters, and Verbal is woven through it in rotation with Quant and Data Insights rather than parked in a block. The spacing is deliberate: a skill consolidates between exposures, so meeting Verbal every few chapters for months beats meeting it every day for three weeks.

Within that rotation, the Verbal stretch is governed by a handful of ordering rules — not a rigid CR-block-then-RC-block — and the rules are the strategy:

1. **Verbal Foundations first** — the very next chapter. Active reading and argument anatomy: the operating system everything else runs on.
2. **CR argument structure before any task family.** Once the anatomy is in place, the nine task families arrive one at a time — assumption, strengthen, weaken, inference, evaluate, flaw, paradox, boldface, complete the argument. One template per chapter, drilled until naming the task is reflex.
3. **The RC reading process before any RC question skill.** RC does not wait for CR to finish: partway through the task families, the path brings in the RC reading-process chapter, then feeds in the question skills — main idea, detail, inference, application, function, attitude — alongside the remaining CR chapters, so both families consolidate in parallel instead of one rotting while you finish the other.
4. **Each answer-trap chapter after its own family.** CR answer traps once the CR templates are installed; RC answer traps once the RC skills are. Wrong answers are written from recipes, and the recipes are as learnable as the solution methods — but traps only make sense once you know what right answers look like.
5. **Mixed timing, last.** Full-speed mixed sets arrive at the end of the path on purpose: pacing built on weak accuracy just produces wrong answers faster.

Which brings us to the pacing reality of 23 questions. The `1:57` average is a budget, not a metronome: an RC passage costs reading time before its first question, and the question cluster repays it — a clean section has slow and fast stretches by design. Until the timing chapter, your pacing job is awareness only: note your time per question in practice, tag overruns as time sinks in the error log, and resist the urge to rush. Accuracy first, speed second — that ordering is baked into the path.

Last piece: the measuring stick. Your real progress check is the **six official mba.com practice exams**, logged on the mock page. The platform's full-length simulations are extra reps between officials, not replacements. Chapters build the skill; officials measure it.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does the path put each answer-trap chapter after its own family's chapters, and timing after everything? Explain the dependency in one sentence — seeing why the rules exist is what stops you from skipping ahead to "test tricks" that have nothing to attach to.

## @summary

Here is the entire section in one screen. Lock the hard facts first:

| Fact | Value |
|---|---|
| Questions | 23 |
| Time | 45 minutes — just under `2:00` per question |
| Question types | Critical Reasoning and Reading Comprehension only |
| Sentence Correction | Removed from the Focus Edition |
| Section score | 60-90, equal weight with Quant and DI in the 205-805 total |
| Review tool | Bookmark questions; change up to three answers at section end |

And the four ideas this chapter installed:

- **Two families, one muscle.** CR is argument logic under a fixed menu of recurring tasks; RC is structure-first reading of dense passages. Both reward knowing what the text is *doing*.
- **Skill, not knowledge.** Verbal conditions through spaced reps instead of patching overnight like Quant content. Run it continuously; never park it for the final month.
- **Speed, not comprehension, is the non-native gap.** The test deleted the question type that rewarded a native ear; what is left is trainable throughput. Your error log will tell you, with data, whether that is your gap: watch whether your misses cluster on misreads and pacing rather than on the reasoning.
- **The path's ordering rules are the strategy.** Foundations first; CR structure before its task families; the RC reading process before the RC question skills; CR and RC interleaved so both consolidate in parallel; each answer-trap chapter after its own family; mixed timing last — with the six official mba.com exams as the measuring stick throughout.

> **Recall check.** Without scrolling: how many questions and minutes, which two question types, and which two miss clusters point to a reading-speed gap rather than a logic gap? (23 in 45; CR and RC; misreads and pacing.) Retrieving these cold is the rehearsal that makes them available on test day.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** A friend preparing for the old GMAT tells you to start with grammar books. In two sentences, explain why that advice is now wrong and what you would do instead. Teaching the change is the fastest way to be sure you understood it.
