## @summary

- Preserve the argument's direction, tone, and scope rather than choosing a fact that is merely related.
- Read the completed argument from start to finish and confirm that the transition now works.

## @cr-complete

Complete-the-argument questions hand you a passage that stops mid-thought — it ends in a blank, usually right after a connector like "therefore," "since," or "because." Your job is to pick the answer that the passage's own statements force into that blank. This is not a creativity test, and it is not a "what would sound smart here" test. The right answer is the one already implied by the sentences in front of you; the four wrong answers add something the passage never licensed. The whole skill collapses to a single discipline: read what is given, name what is missing, and supply only what the given material already guarantees. Get those three moves right and this becomes one of the most mechanical, most winnable question types on the Critical Reasoning section.

**Mental model.** A complete-the-argument question is a fill-in-the-blank where the passage has already done the reasoning and left you to supply the one piece it was building toward. Treat the blank as the destination of a sentence the author started. Read what came before, identify the missing structural part, and choose the answer the existing pieces *demand* — nothing stronger, nothing extra. The author handed you a partly assembled machine; your job is to drop in the one part that makes it run, not to redesign it. You are not the author here — you are finishing the author's sentence, and the author has already decided where it goes. Your only freedom is to find that one predetermined destination.

The word immediately before the blank is the single most important clue, because it tells you whether the blank wants a **conclusion** or a **missing premise**. Read that connector first, before you touch a single answer choice. Everything downstream — which answers you keep, which you cut — depends on getting this one read right. Skip it and you are guessing; honor it and half the answer choices disqualify themselves before you finish reading them.

| Connector before the blank | What the blank needs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently | the **conclusion** | The premises came first; the blank is what they add up to. |
| since, because, for, as, given that | a **premise / supporting reason** | The conclusion already sat in the passage; the blank explains *why* it holds. |
| but, however, yet | a **contrasting fact** that completes the tension | The author set up an expectation; the blank supplies the twist. |
| in order to, so that | a **goal or condition** the prior action serves | The blank states the purpose the rest of the sentence is built around. |
| overlooks / fails to consider that | the **possibility that reconciles** a clash | The blank names the gap the argument ignored. |

So before you read the options, glance at that connector and name the job out loud: "This blank wants the conclusion" or "This blank wants the reason." Naming it out loud matters more than it sounds — it locks your read before the answer choices get a chance to push you around. Wrong answers are engineered to be plausible *for the wrong job*; if you have already committed to "this blank wants a premise," a beautifully worded conclusion-flavored trap loses its pull. The test-writer's whole leverage is the hope that you will read the blank as "what sounds like a satisfying ending" instead of "what structural role does the grammar demand here." Take that leverage away on the first read.

> **Recall check.** What is the single most important word in a complete-the-argument passage, and what does it tell you? (The connector immediately before the blank; it tells you whether the blank wants a conclusion or a supporting premise. Pull this from memory now — forcing the retrieval cements the habit far better than re-reading the table, because you are practicing the exact move you will make on test day.)

**Step-by-step procedure.** Memorize this and run it on every complete-the-argument question, in order:

1. **Read the connector** right before the blank and classify the job: conclusion, premise, contrast, or reconciling possibility.
2. **Map the existing pieces.** Note every premise and any conclusion already on the page. Underline the evidence mentally; count the distinct claims.
3. **Gauge the strength of the evidence.** Is it a trend, a correlation, a single study, a "may"? The completion can be no stronger than what feeds it.
4. **Predict the blank in your own words** before reading any choice. One short sentence.
5. **Match, don't browse.** Compare each choice to your prediction and to the job you named. Eliminate anything doing the wrong job.
6. **Run survivors through the logical-link filter** (logical follow, full coverage, no outside assumption).
7. **Pick the answer the passage already guarantees** — the one that needs zero added assumptions and honors every premise.

**Worked example (straightforward).** "The company's profits have increased every quarter since it launched the new product line. However, customer satisfaction has been steadily declining. Therefore, ______." The connector is *therefore*, so the blank wants the **conclusion** — the claim both facts add up to. A strong completion: "the company's profit gains may not be sustainable, since declining satisfaction typically precedes customer churn." Notice it accounts for *both* pieces of evidence (profits up, satisfaction down) and lands on a forward-looking claim that follows from them. Compare the traps: "profits will definitely drop" is too strong (the passage gives a trend, not a certainty); "customers are unhappy" is too narrow (it ignores the profit evidence); "the company should fire its CEO" imports an outside idea the passage never raised.

**Worked example (straightforward).** "Migratory songbirds navigate partly by the Earth's magnetic field. In a recent study, birds exposed to weak radio static during migration veered off course far more often than unexposed birds, because ______." Here the connector is *because*, so the blank wants a **premise** that explains the veering — a mechanism, not a new conclusion. The conclusion (static disrupts navigation) is already on the table. The right answer reads like: "the radio static interfered with the birds' ability to detect the magnetic field." That supplies the missing causal link the passage was reaching for. A wrong answer that just restates the conclusion ("the birds got lost more easily") adds no reason; a wrong answer about the birds' diet imports an unrelated factor.

**The logical-link filter.** The correct completion follows from the stated premises *alone*. If you catch yourself thinking "well, this would work if we also assumed…," that answer is wrong — you're patching it with an unstated assumption the passage didn't give you. Run every surviving answer through three checks:

- **Logical follow** — does it actually result from the premises, or just sound related?
- **Full coverage** — does it account for *all* the relevant evidence, not just one clause?
- **No outside assumption** — does it stay inside the passage, or smuggle in a new idea?

**Worked example (medium).** "A city installed cameras at its busiest intersections to reduce accidents. In the year after installation, the number of accidents at those intersections fell by 18 percent. The city's transportation director concluded that the cameras caused the drop. This conclusion is questionable, since ______." The connector is *since*, so the blank wants a **premise** — but a specific kind: a reason the causal conclusion is shaky. Predict it: something that offers an alternative explanation for the 18 percent drop. The right answer reads like: "accident rates fell by a similar amount that year at comparable intersections with no cameras." That supplies the missing reason the director's causal claim is doubtful — if camera-free intersections improved just as much, the cameras can't claim the credit. Watch the traps: "the cameras were expensive to install" is irrelevant to *causation*; "drivers dislike being monitored" adds an outside attitude; "accidents rose the following year" changes the timeframe the argument is about. Only the comparison-site answer attacks the logical link the passage actually depends on.

> **Recall check.** When the blank follows "since" or "because," what role are you supplying, and what role is already on the page? (You supply a premise — a supporting reason or mechanism; the conclusion is already stated in the passage. Retrieving this beats glancing back at the connector table because on test day you will not have the table — you will have only what you have rehearsed.)

**Trap to watch.** The most common miss is the **over-strong completion**. The passage gives you a trend, a correlation, or a "may," and the tempting answer converts it into "will definitely," "proves," or "always." A blank built on modest evidence cannot support an absolute claim. When two answers compete, the one hedged with "may," "suggests," or "is likely" usually wins, because it matches the strength of the evidence. The second-most-common miss is the **half-coverage answer** that nails one premise and silently drops the other — strong completions honor every piece the passage put in play.

**Worked example (hard).** "Critics argue that remote work erodes team cohesion because employees rarely interact spontaneously. Yet at firms that switched to remote work, internal surveys showed that employees reported *higher* trust in their colleagues than before the switch. The critics' argument therefore overlooks the possibility that ______." Two connectors are in play, so read carefully. The decisive one is *therefore*, and the phrase "overlooks the possibility that" tells you the blank must name a possibility that reconciles the critics' claim with the surprising survey result. Classify the job: the blank needs a completion that explains *how cohesion could hold or rise without spontaneous interaction*. Predict it: maybe trust is built through something other than spontaneous run-ins. The right answer reads like: "team cohesion can be sustained through deliberate, scheduled interaction rather than spontaneous contact." That honors both pieces — the critics' premise (less spontaneity) and the survey (more trust) — by exposing the hidden assumption that *only* spontaneity builds cohesion. Now the traps. "Remote employees work longer hours" — true-sounding but irrelevant to cohesion. "Some firms returned to the office" — changes the subject. "The surveys were poorly designed" — this attacks the evidence rather than completing the author's reconciling thought, and the stem asked what the critics *overlook*, not whether the data is bad. The hard part here is that the over-strong-versus-hedged test does not settle it; you have to track *which job the stem assigned* and supply exactly that. This is why step 1 (read the connector and the framing phrase) is non-negotiable.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an answer that requires an extra unstated assumption automatically fail a complete-the-argument question? (Because the question asks what the *given* premises complete — the right answer is fully supported by the passage as written; needing an added assumption means the passage did not actually establish it. Explaining this to yourself, rather than nodding at it, forces you to connect the rule to the underlying logic, which is what makes it stick.)

**Worked example (hard, premise-completion).** "Geologists can estimate a rock layer's age by measuring the ratio of a radioactive isotope to its decay product, on the assumption that ______." The connector is the framing phrase "on the assumption that," which asks for the **premise the method depends on** — the condition that must hold for the dating technique to work at all. Predict it: the decay product must come only from the decay, and the decay rate must be steady. The right answer reads like: "the decay product was not present in the rock at the time the layer formed and was not added or removed afterward." That is the load-bearing assumption — if the rock started with some decay product, the ratio would overstate its age. A trap like "the radioactive isotope is rare in the Earth's crust" sounds geological but does nothing for the *ratio* logic; "the rock has never been exposed to high temperatures" introduces an outside variable the passage never tied to the method. The right answer is the one the method silently *requires*, not merely a true statement about rocks.

**Worked example (hard, contrast-completion).** "For decades, town planners assumed that widening a congested highway would ease traffic. But in city after city, newly widened highways filled with cars within a few years and congestion returned to its former level. This pattern is best explained by the fact that ______." The decisive connector is *but*, and the stem asks for the fact that *explains* the surprising reversal — the blank completes the tension between the planners' expectation and the observed outcome. Predict it: extra road capacity must somehow generate extra driving. The right answer reads like: "the added capacity drew in drivers who had previously avoided the route or traveled at other times." That resolves the paradox by naming the mechanism — induced demand — that makes the widening self-defeating. Traps: "highway construction is costly" is irrelevant to *why congestion returned*; "drivers prefer wider roads" sounds related but doesn't explain the *return* to former congestion; "public transit ridership fell" adds a new actor the passage never measured. Only the induced-demand answer completes the explanatory link the *but* set up.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Skipping the connector** and reading the blank as "what sounds like a good ending." Without classifying the job first, you cannot tell a conclusion-trap from a premise-trap — they are designed to look equally reasonable.
- **Over-strengthening.** Turning a "may" or a trend into "will," "proves," or "always." The completion can never be stronger than the evidence feeding it.
- **Half-coverage.** Picking an answer that satisfies one premise and quietly ignores another the passage put in play. Strong completions account for *every* relevant clause.
- **Importing outside ideas.** Bringing in cost, ethics, attitudes, or new actors the passage never mentioned. If you had to assume it, the passage did not establish it.

**Closing recap.**

- Read the **connector right before the blank first** and name the job: conclusion (therefore/thus), premise (since/because), contrast (but/however), or reconciling possibility (overlooks that).
- **Predict the completion in your own words** before browsing answers, so the traps cannot steer you.
- Keep only answers that **logically follow, cover all the evidence, and add no outside assumption.**
- **Match strength to evidence** — modest premises support hedged ("may," "likely") completions, not absolutes.
- Complete-the-argument is the structural mirror of boldface: boldface asks you to *label* the parts on the page, complete asks you to *supply* the one part that is missing. Find the connector, name the missing role, and pick the answer the passage already guarantees.
