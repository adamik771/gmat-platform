## @cr-complete

Complete-the-argument questions hand you a passage that stops mid-thought — it ends in a blank, usually right after a connector like "therefore," "since," or "because." Your job is to pick the answer that the passage's own statements force into that blank. This is not a creativity test. The right answer is the one already implied by the sentences in front of you; the four wrong answers add something the passage never licensed.

**Mental model.** A complete-the-argument question is a fill-in-the-blank where the passage has already done the reasoning and left you to supply the one piece it was building toward. Treat the blank as the destination of a sentence the author started. Read what came before, identify the missing structural part, and choose the answer the existing pieces *demand* — nothing stronger, nothing extra.

The word immediately before the blank is the single most important clue, because it tells you whether the blank wants a **conclusion** or a **missing premise**. Read that connector first, before you touch a single answer choice.

| Connector before the blank | What the blank needs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently | the **conclusion** | The premises came first; the blank is what they add up to. |
| since, because, for, as, given that | a **premise / supporting reason** | The conclusion already sat in the passage; the blank explains *why* it holds. |
| but, however, yet | a **contrasting fact** that completes the tension | The author set up an expectation; the blank supplies the twist. |

So before you read the options, glance at that connector and name the job out loud: "This blank wants the conclusion" or "This blank wants the reason."

**Worked example.** "The company's profits have increased every quarter since it launched the new product line. However, customer satisfaction has been steadily declining. Therefore, ______." The connector is *therefore*, so the blank wants the **conclusion** — the claim both facts add up to. A strong completion: "the company's profit gains may not be sustainable, since declining satisfaction typically precedes customer churn." Notice it accounts for *both* pieces of evidence (profits up, satisfaction down) and lands on a forward-looking claim that follows from them. Compare the traps: "profits will definitely drop" is too strong (the passage gives a trend, not a certainty); "customers are unhappy" is too narrow (it ignores the profit evidence); "the company should fire its CEO" imports an outside idea the passage never raised.

**Worked example.** "Migratory songbirds navigate partly by the Earth's magnetic field. In a recent study, birds exposed to weak radio static during migration veered off course far more often than unexposed birds, because ______." Here the connector is *because*, so the blank wants a **premise** that explains the veering — a mechanism, not a new conclusion. The conclusion (static disrupts navigation) is already on the table. The right answer reads like: "the radio static interfered with the birds' ability to detect the magnetic field." That supplies the missing causal link the passage was reaching for. A wrong answer that just restates the conclusion ("the birds got lost more easily") adds no reason; a wrong answer about the birds' diet imports an unrelated factor.

**The logical-link filter.** The correct completion follows from the stated premises *alone*. If you catch yourself thinking "well, this would work if we also assumed…," that answer is wrong — you're patching it with an unstated assumption the passage didn't give you. Run every surviving answer through three checks:

- **Logical follow** — does it actually result from the premises, or just sound related?
- **Full coverage** — does it account for *all* the relevant evidence, not just one clause?
- **No outside assumption** — does it stay inside the passage, or smuggle in a new idea?

**Trap to watch.** The most common miss is the **over-strong completion**. The passage gives you a trend, a correlation, or a "may," and the tempting answer converts it into "will definitely," "proves," or "always." A blank built on modest evidence cannot support an absolute claim. When two answers compete, the one hedged with "may," "suggests," or "is likely" usually wins, because it matches the strength of the evidence. The second-most-common miss is the **half-coverage answer** that nails one premise and silently drops the other — strong completions honor every piece the passage put in play.

> **Recall check.** Before reading the answers, what two things do you check first? (1) the connector right before the blank, to learn whether it wants a conclusion or a premise; (2) name that job in one sentence before touching the choices.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an answer that requires an extra unstated assumption automatically fail a complete-the-argument question? (Because the question asks what the *given* premises complete — the right answer is fully supported by the passage as written; needing an added assumption means the passage did not actually establish it.)

One relationship worth noting: complete-the-argument is the structural mirror of boldface questions — both reward you for knowing which part of an argument is which (premise, conclusion, contrast). But where boldface asks you to *label* parts already on the page, complete asks you to *supply* the one part that's missing. Find the connector, name the missing role, and pick the answer the passage already guarantees.
