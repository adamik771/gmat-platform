## @cr-weaken

A Weaken question hands you an argument that *looks* settled and asks you to crack it. Your job: find the one answer choice that, **if true**, makes the conclusion *less* likely to follow from the evidence. You are not proving the conclusion false — you are widening the gap between what the author **showed** and what the author **claimed**. That distinction is the whole game. A weakener can leave every stated fact intact and still gut the argument, because what it attacks is the *inference*, the silent step from evidence to conclusion. Notice the consequence: you should expect the credited answer to introduce *new* information from outside the passage. A choice that merely restates a premise, or that quibbles with a number the author gave you, is almost never the move. The action is always at the joint between evidence and conclusion, never inside the evidence itself.

**Mental model.** Every CR argument is evidence plus a leap to a conclusion, and that leap rests on an unstated assumption. A weakener is anything that, if true, makes the leap *shakier* — it does not have to demolish the argument, only damage it. Picture the conclusion sitting on a bridge of assumptions; the right answer kicks out a support. The wrong answers either touch nothing structural or, worse, reinforce the bridge. Keep that image literal: you are looking for the choice that removes a plank, not the choice that argues with the riverbank. The riverbank is the evidence — solid, stipulated, not yours to fight. The planks are the assumptions, and exactly one answer pulls one out from under the author's feet.

Most weaken arguments are **causal**: the author sees X and Y move together and concludes "X caused Y." That single move is where almost every correct weakener lives. Memorize the five ways to attack it and you will recognize the answer on sight.

- **Alternative cause.** Something *other* than X also changed and could have produced Y. This is the most common and most powerful weakener.
- **Reverse causation.** Y actually caused X, not the other way around.
- **Confounding variable.** A third factor correlates with both X and Y, so the link between them may be an illusion.
- **Flawed / unrepresentative data.** The evidence itself is suspect — sample too small, biased selection, cherry-picked period.
- **Broken link.** The mechanism the author assumes does not actually work that way, so the evidence never reaches the conclusion.

These five are not random; they are an exhaustive map of how a "X caused Y" claim can fail. Either something else did it (alternative cause), the arrow points the other way (reverse causation), nothing actually connects them (confounder), the data never showed a real pattern to begin with (flawed data), or the chain from cause to effect doesn't hold (broken link). When you can name *which* of the five a choice is using, you are no longer guessing — you are diagnosing. And the diagnostic habit pays off twice: it tells you what to *predict* before you read the choices, and it gives you language to *reject* the four decoys, each of which fails to land on any of the five.

**Worked example.** *(straightforward)* Argument: "After launching a loyalty program, the chain's revenue rose 8%. The loyalty program drove the increase." The correct weakener: *"During the same period, the chain also ran its largest-ever advertising campaign."* Now a second plausible cause exists, and the evidence no longer isolates the program. The conclusion's gap just got wider — an **alternative cause** is exactly what "weaken" demands. Note what the choice does *not* claim: it never says the loyalty program failed. It simply removes the program's exclusive credit, and that is enough. Contrast a tempting decoy: *"The loyalty program cost more to run than management expected."* That sounds critical of the program, but cost has nothing to do with whether the program *caused the revenue rise*. It is irrelevant to the causal leap, and irrelevant is dead.

**Worked example.** *(moderate)* Argument: "Neighborhoods with more green space have lower asthma rates, so green space reduces asthma." The correct weakener: *"Green-space neighborhoods also have substantially higher median incomes."* Income is now a third variable that could independently explain *both* the green space and the lower asthma — wealthier residents have better healthcare and cleaner housing. The correlation may be real while the *causal* claim collapses; that is a **confounding variable** at work. Notice the same argument could be cracked by **reverse causation** instead: *"Families with asthmatic children are advised to move to greener areas"* — the effect (asthma) is steering who lives where. One correlation, two distinct ways to attack it: this is why naming the pattern matters more than memorizing example sentences.

> **Recall check.** What are the five weakener patterns for a causal argument? (Alternative cause, reverse causation, confounding variable, flawed/unrepresentative data, broken link.) Force the list from memory before reading on — pulling it out cold is what builds the recognition reflex; re-skimming the bullet list above just builds familiarity, which feels like learning but isn't.

**Worked example.** *(moderate)* Argument: "A company surveyed its newsletter subscribers and found 92% were satisfied with the product, so customers are overwhelmingly satisfied." The correct weakener: *"Subscribers to the newsletter are mostly long-time repeat buyers."* The sample is not representative of *customers* — it is skewed toward exactly the people most likely to be happy. The evidence never measured the population the conclusion is about. This is **flawed / unrepresentative data**, and it is easy to miss because the 92% number *feels* like hard proof. The crack is in *who got counted*, not in the percentage. A reliable tell for this pattern: the conclusion talks about a broad group ("customers") while the evidence quietly came from a narrower one ("subscribers"). Whenever the population in the evidence is smaller or self-selected relative to the population in the conclusion, suspect unrepresentative data.

Train your eye to flag the stem's verb the instant you read it. The connective tissue is always doubt, undermine, weaken, damage, question.

> **Recall check.** In a "X caused Y" argument, which single weakener pattern is the most common and the one to check first? (Alternative cause — some *other* change could have produced Y.) Retrieving the priority order now means that under timed pressure your first scan of the choices already has a target, instead of you re-deriving the hierarchy mid-question.

**Worked example.** *(broken-link variant)* Argument: "Studies show people who take vitamin D supplements have stronger bones. Therefore the city should add vitamin D to the public water supply to reduce fractures." The correct weakener: *"Vitamin D strengthens bones only when absorbed alongside dietary fat, which is absent from water intake."* Every fact about supplements stays true, but the **mechanism** the plan relies on doesn't operate in the proposed delivery method — the evidence about pills never reaches the conclusion about water. Plan-based stems (note "the city should…") love the broken-link attack: the right answer often shows *why the plan won't produce the intended effect*, not that the underlying science is wrong. Whenever a stem proposes an action to achieve a goal, pre-load this question: *Is there a reason the action won't deliver the goal?* That is the broken link, and it is the plan-argument's favorite failure mode.

**Worked example.** *(hard)* Argument: "Regions that adopted the new traffic-camera system saw accident rates fall by 15% the following year, while regions without cameras saw no change. The cameras clearly reduced accidents." The correct weakener: *"The camera regions had recorded unusually high accident rates the year before adoption, well above their long-run average."* This is **regression to the mean** masquerading as cause — an abnormally bad year is naturally followed by a more typical (lower) one, cameras or not. It also quietly poisons the comparison: the no-camera regions had no spike to fall back from, so "no change" is expected. This is the kind of choice that looks like an irrelevant historical footnote until you see it removes the baseline that made the 15% look causal. Hard weakeners hide the alternative cause inside a *timing* or *baseline* fact rather than naming a second event outright — train yourself to ask, of any number, "compared to *when*, and was that starting point normal?"

> **Recall check.** A choice says the plan's proposed method can't physically deliver the intended effect, even though the underlying science is sound — which pattern is that, and on what stem type does it cluster? (Broken link, clustering on plan/proposal stems — "the company should," "the city should.") Naming the pattern *and* the stem family it favors is what lets you predict the attack before you read a single choice.

**Trap to watch.** The **opposite-direction trap.** Under time pressure your hand drifts toward the answer that *supports* the argument — it reads as "relevant and true," so it feels right. On a weaken question that is the single most seductive wrong answer, because the test-writers build it deliberately. Before you commit, run a two-second sanity check: *Am I supposed to support or undermine this argument?* That two seconds prevents a thirty-second recovery.

Two quieter traps round out the set. The **irrelevant choice**: most wrong answers on weaken questions are simply true statements that never touch the gap. Read each choice and ask, *does this tell me anything about whether the evidence actually supports the conclusion?* If no, eliminate immediately — do not get pulled in by a fact merely because it shares the topic. And the **partial weakener**: a choice that dents a minor premise or a side detail while leaving the *central* leap untouched. The credited answer always attacks the main conclusion's load-bearing assumption, not a peripheral one — so read all five choices before committing, because a "good enough" weakener in slot B is often beaten by a sharper one in slot D.

**Step-by-step procedure.** Memorize this and run it the same way every time:

1. **Read the stem first** and confirm the task is *weaken* — note the verb (weaken / undermine / doubt / damage / question).
2. **Find the conclusion.** It is the claim the author wants you to accept, often signposted by *therefore, thus, so, clearly, this shows*.
3. **Find the evidence** and state the **leap** in your own words: "Author saw ___ and concluded ___."
4. **Name the assumption** the leap depends on — usually "nothing else explains this" or "the arrow points this way."
5. **Predict the attack.** If it's causal, ask which of the five patterns fits best before looking at choices.
6. **Scan all five choices,** keeping only the one that makes a careful reader *trust the assumption less*. Eliminate irrelevants and supporters on sight.
7. **Confirm direction.** Re-ask: does my pick *widen* the gap? If it narrows it, you fell into the opposite-direction trap — reverse course.

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an *alternative cause* weaken without proving the conclusion false? (Because the conclusion claimed X was *the* driver; offering a second viable cause shows the evidence no longer singles out X, so the inference is unjustified even if X happened to be true.) Explaining the *why* to yourself, rather than nodding at the rule, is what lets you generate the attack on a fresh argument instead of only recognizing it on a familiar one.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Attacking a premise instead of the leap.** Arguing with a stated fact ("but the revenue figure might be wrong") is usually not the move — weaken the *inference*, the unstated bridge.
- **Choosing the strongest-feeling fact over the gap-relevant one.** A dramatic, topical statement that doesn't touch the conclusion's assumption is the classic irrelevant trap.
- **Settling on the first decent weakener.** Slot B's mild dent loses to slot D's direct hit — always read all five.
- **Forgetting to recheck direction on hard questions.** The harder the argument, the more a supporter is dressed up to look like an attacker. Run the two-second direction check.

**Recap.**

- A weakener makes the conclusion *less* likely to follow — it **widens the evidence-to-conclusion gap**, it does not disprove the conclusion.
- Most weaken arguments are **causal**; attack the "X caused Y" leap with one of the **five patterns**, led by **alternative cause**.
- Always **lock onto the conclusion**, name the **assumption**, then ask which choice makes that assumption less trustworthy.
- Beware the **opposite-direction trap** (supporters), **irrelevant choices**, and **partial weakeners** — read all five and confirm direction.
- Weaken is the mirror of Strengthen — same gap, opposite move. Here you are always *widening* it; the choice that does is your weakener.
