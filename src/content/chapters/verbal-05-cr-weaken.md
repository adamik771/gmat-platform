## @cr-weaken

A Weaken question hands you an argument that *looks* settled and asks you to crack it. Your job: find the one answer choice that, **if true**, makes the conclusion *less* likely to follow from the evidence. You are not proving the conclusion false — you are widening the gap between what the author **showed** and what the author **claimed**.

**Mental model.** Every CR argument is evidence plus a leap to a conclusion, and that leap rests on an unstated assumption. A weakener is anything that, if true, makes the leap *shakier* — it does not have to demolish the argument, only damage it. Picture the conclusion sitting on a bridge of assumptions; the right answer kicks out a support. The wrong answers either touch nothing structural or, worse, reinforce the bridge.

Most weaken arguments are **causal**: the author sees X and Y move together and concludes "X caused Y." That single move is where almost every correct weakener lives. Memorize the five ways to attack it and you will recognize the answer on sight.

- **Alternative cause.** Something *other* than X also changed and could have produced Y. This is the most common and most powerful weakener.
- **Reverse causation.** Y actually caused X, not the other way around.
- **Confounding variable.** A third factor correlates with both X and Y, so the link between them may be an illusion.
- **Flawed / unrepresentative data.** The evidence itself is suspect — sample too small, biased selection, cherry-picked period.
- **Broken link.** The mechanism the author assumes does not actually work that way, so the evidence never reaches the conclusion.

**Worked example.** Argument: "After launching a loyalty program, the chain's revenue rose 8%. The loyalty program drove the increase." The correct weakener: *"During the same period, the chain also ran its largest-ever advertising campaign."* Now a second plausible cause exists, and the evidence no longer isolates the program. The conclusion's gap just got wider — an **alternative cause** is exactly what "weaken" demands.

**Worked example.** Argument: "Neighborhoods with more green space have lower asthma rates, so green space reduces asthma." The correct weakener: *"Green-space neighborhoods also have substantially higher median incomes."* Income is now a third variable that could independently explain *both* the green space and the lower asthma — wealthier residents have better healthcare and cleaner housing. The correlation may be real while the *causal* claim collapses; that is a **confounding variable** at work. Notice the same argument could be cracked by **reverse causation** instead: *"Families with asthmatic children are advised to move to greener areas"* — the effect (asthma) is steering who lives where.

Train your eye to flag the stem's verb the instant you read it. The connective tissue is always doubt, undermine, weaken, damage, question.

| Stem wording | What it's asking |
|---|---|
| "…most **weakens** the argument…" | Weaken — find the gap-widener |
| "…most **undermines** the conclusion…" | Weaken |
| "…casts the most **doubt** on…" | Weaken |
| "…if true, gives the **strongest reason to question**…" | Weaken |
| "…would be most **damaging** to the plan/claim…" | Weaken |
| "…most calls into **question** the author's reasoning…" | Weaken |

**Trap to watch.** The **opposite-direction trap.** Under time pressure your hand drifts toward the answer that *supports* the argument — it reads as "relevant and true," so it feels right. On a weaken question that is the single most seductive wrong answer, because the test-writers build it deliberately. Before you commit, run a two-second sanity check: *Am I supposed to support or undermine this argument?* That two seconds prevents a thirty-second recovery.

Two quieter traps round out the set. The **irrelevant choice**: most wrong answers on weaken questions are simply true statements that never touch the gap. Read each choice and ask, *does this tell me anything about whether the evidence actually supports the conclusion?* If no, eliminate immediately — do not get pulled in by a fact merely because it shares the topic. And the **partial weakener**: a choice that dents a minor premise or a side detail while leaving the *central* leap untouched. The credited answer always attacks the main conclusion's load-bearing assumption, not a peripheral one — so read all five choices before committing, because a "good enough" weakener in slot B is often beaten by a sharper one in slot D.

> **Recall check.** What are the five weakener patterns for a causal argument? (Alternative cause, reverse causation, confounding variable, flawed/unrepresentative data, broken link.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why does an *alternative cause* weaken without proving the conclusion false? (Because the conclusion claimed X was *the* driver; offering a second viable cause shows the evidence no longer singles out X, so the inference is unjustified even if X happened to be true.)

Weaken is the mirror of Strengthen — both operate on the same evidence-to-conclusion gap — but here you are always *widening* it. Lock onto the conclusion, name the assumption it rests on, then ask which choice would make a careful reader trust that assumption less. The answer that does is your weakener.
