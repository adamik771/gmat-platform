## @rc-main-idea

Main idea questions ask: "What is the primary purpose of the passage?" or "Which of the following best summarizes the passage's central argument?" or "The author's main point is that…" The wording varies; the demand never does. You are being asked for the **single claim that unifies every paragraph** — not the topic, not the most interesting detail, not the strongest-sounding sentence. This is the most common Reading Comprehension question type, and it rewards a disciplined process more than fast reading. Get the process automatic and you bank these points; wing it and you'll fall for the same three or four engineered traps every time.

**The test.** The correct answer captures the author's central thesis — the one claim that ties the whole passage together. It is *not* a summary of the topic; it is the specific *claim* the author is making about the topic. "The passage is about subscription businesses" is a topic. "Subscription businesses survive by building real switching costs rather than tricks" is a claim. Main-idea questions reward the claim and punish the topic, every time. A useful mental model: the topic answers *what is this about?* while the main idea answers *so what is the author telling me about it?* Only the second question's answer can be correct.

**Where the main idea lives.** Main ideas are most often in the **first paragraph** (as a thesis statement) or the **last paragraph** (as a conclusion). Sometimes both — the author states a thesis up front and restates it, sharpened, at the end. The middle paragraphs almost always carry evidence, examples, and counter-views, not the thesis itself. So if you can't find the main idea in paragraph 1, jump to the **last paragraph** before re-reading the middle. The author's final move (a "thus," "ultimately," "the better explanation is…") is frequently the thesis in plain sight. Signpost words are your fastest locator: words like *therefore*, *consequently*, *the key point is*, and *what this suggests* tend to sit right beside the thesis.

> **Recall check.** Where in a passage is the main idea most likely to appear, and which paragraphs usually do *not* contain it? (First or last paragraph; the middle paragraphs usually carry evidence and counter-views, not the thesis.) Pulling this from memory now wires the search habit in far better than re-reading it does — you want this to fire automatically under timed pressure.

**The three filters for eliminating wrong main-idea answers.** Almost every wrong answer fails exactly one of these. Learn to name which one.

1. **Too narrow.** Describes only one paragraph or one aspect of the passage. True about a slice, but it doesn't cover the whole arc. Wrong.
2. **Too broad.** Describes the general topic area without the author's specific claim. It could headline a different passage entirely. Wrong.
3. **Off-stance.** Gets the author's attitude wrong — says the author "advocates" when the author actually "critiques," or "proves" when the author merely "considers." Wrong.

Only the answer that is **specific enough** to capture the author's claim, **broad enough** to cover all paragraphs, **and matches the author's stance** is correct. Think of it as a three-lock door: an answer has to open all three locks. Most wrong answers open two and fail the third — which is exactly why they're tempting. The "two right, one wrong" structure is deliberate; the test isn't trying to fool you with nonsense, it's trying to fool you with *almost*.

**Worked example (straightforward).** Passage: introduces the subscription economy, discusses the "leaky bucket" customer-retention problem, surveys three strategies (dark patterns, personalization, genuine switching costs), and concludes that the most successful companies build real switching costs.

Candidate answers:

- A) "Advocates subscription models over traditional transactions." — **Off-stance and too narrow.** The passage is analytical, not promotional; and it never argues subscriptions beat transactions.
- B) "Examines the challenges subscription businesses face in retaining customers and the strategies they use." — **Correct.** Captures the problem (retention) and the range of responses (three strategies). Right scope, right stance.
- C) "Criticizes companies that use dark patterns." — **Too narrow.** Dark patterns is one brief mention inside a survey of three strategies.
- D) "Argues that subscription businesses are ultimately unsustainable." — **Off-stance.** Overstates the critique into a doom claim the author never makes.
- E) "Compares the revenue predictability of subscription vs traditional models." — **Too narrow.** That's roughly paragraph one only.

Notice B is the least flashy option. Main-idea answers usually are — they're complete, so they read as measured rather than dramatic.

**The "verb test."** Main-idea answers typically open with a verb: *explains, argues, examines, critiques, defends, compares, proposes.* The verb has to match the author's actual move. If the passage lays out multiple views without endorsing one, the verb is *explores* or *surveys* — not *argues*. If the passage commits to one position, the verb is *contends* or *argues* — not *describes*. Mismatch the verb and you've failed the stance lock before you even read the rest of the choice. This is the single fastest elimination available: scan the opening verb of each choice first, kill the ones whose verb can't be right, and you often cut five options to two in seconds.

**The "author's attitude" map.** Match the answer's verb to the author's stance:

| Author's stance in passage | Verbs a correct answer may use |
|---|---|
| Neutral / descriptive | discusses, examines, outlines, describes |
| Mildly positive | supports, endorses, favors |
| Critical | criticizes, challenges, questions, disputes |
| Balanced / exploratory | weighs, compares, considers, surveys |
| Committed argument | argues, contends, demonstrates, maintains |

If the passage is critical and the answer says "supports," it's wrong — no matter how accurate the rest of the sentence sounds.

**Worked example (medium — the stance trap).** Passage: a historian notes that a popular theory crediting the printing press for the Reformation is "appealing but incomplete," then spends two paragraphs showing pre-existing literacy networks and political grievances were already in motion, concluding the press "accelerated rather than caused" the movement.

- A) "Argues that the printing press caused the Reformation." — **Off-stance.** This is the view the author *qualifies*, not endorses.
- B) "Demonstrates that political grievances alone explain the Reformation." — **Too narrow and overstated** — "alone" overshoots; grievances are one factor among several.
- C) "Argues that the printing press accelerated, rather than caused, a movement already underway." — **Correct.** Matches the author's committed-but-qualified stance and covers all paragraphs.
- D) "Describes the spread of literacy in early modern Europe." — **Too narrow** (one strand) and wrong verb (the author argues; this says merely "describes").

The lesson: when an author *qualifies* a popular claim, the main idea is the qualification, not the popular claim. Test-makers bank on you grabbing the famous-sounding option A.

> **Recall check.** Name the three filters that kill a wrong main-idea answer. (Too narrow, too broad, off-stance.) Say them out loud before peeking — naming the failure mode is how you turn vague "this feels off" into a fast, defensible elimination.

**Worked example (medium — the too-broad trap).** Passage: explains that early economists assumed perfectly rational actors, then shows how a series of experiments documenting predictable irrationality forced a rethink, and closes by noting that behavioral economics now sits alongside, not above, classical models.

- A) "Discusses the field of economics." — **Too broad.** Could headline a textbook chapter on anything; states no claim and covers no specific arc.
- B) "Argues that classical economics has been entirely discredited." — **Off-stance / overstated.** The author ends on *alongside, not above* — coexistence, not replacement.
- C) "Traces how experimental findings of irrationality reshaped, without overturning, the rational-actor model." — **Correct.** Specific claim, full arc, accurate stance.
- D) "Describes several experiments in behavioral economics." — **Too narrow.** The experiments are evidence for the larger point, not the point.

Here A and D fail at opposite ends of the same axis: A is too big to be a claim, D is too small to be the claim. The correct answer always threads between them.

**The "both halves" trap.** Some passages have a two-part structure: first X, then a pivot ("However," "More recently," "But this view has been challenged") to Y. Main-idea answers that mention only X or only Y are wrong, even though each is genuinely in the passage. The right answer captures **both halves and the relationship between them.** Train your eye to flag the pivot word the moment you hit it — that word is usually where the real thesis turns.

**Worked example (hard — the both-halves trap).** Passage: paragraphs 1–2 explain the long-standing consensus that a species' extinction was driven by climate change; paragraph 3 opens "Recent isotope analysis complicates this," and paragraphs 3–4 present evidence that human hunting was the larger factor; the final sentence calls for "integrating both pressures."

- A) "Explains how climate change drove the species to extinction." — **Too narrow** — only the first half; ignores the pivot.
- B) "Argues that human hunting, not climate, caused the extinction." — **Off-stance / overstated.** The author says "complicates" and ends on *integration*, not replacement.
- C) "Presents the climate consensus, then introduces evidence that human hunting was a larger factor, suggesting both pressures should be integrated." — **Correct.** Covers both halves and the author's reconciling stance.
- D) "Surveys recent isotope-analysis techniques in paleontology." — **Too broad/off-topic** — method is a tool in the passage, not its point.

Choice A is the classic both-halves miss: it's *true*, it's well-written, and it's wrong because the passage didn't stop there. Choice B is the trap's twin — it grabs the *second* half and overstates the pivot into a full reversal. Both are seductive precisely because the passage really does say what they describe.

**Trap to watch.** The "true but not the main idea" answer. The most dangerous wrong choices are *accurate* — they state something the passage genuinely says. Accuracy is not the test; **scope and centrality** are. Before you pick, ask: does this cover paragraph 1 *and* the last paragraph, or just a slice? If it covers a slice, it's a detail dressed up as a thesis. The feeling you're hunting for is not "yes, the passage said that" (many wrong answers pass that bar) but "yes, *everything* in the passage exists to support this."

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why is the main idea not the same as the topic? If you can say "because the topic is *what the passage is about*, while the main idea is *what the author claims about that topic*," you've internalized the distinction — and you'll stop picking broad "the passage discusses X" answers that name the topic but state no claim.

**The procedure to memorize.** Run this every main-idea question, in order:

1. **Recall the arc, not the words.** In one sentence, what does the author *claim*? Build this from the first and last paragraphs before looking at choices.
2. **Fix the stance.** Is the author neutral, positive, critical, exploratory, or committed? Pick the verb family now.
3. **Predict.** Phrase your own answer in ~8 words: "[verb] that [claim across all paragraphs]." Do this *before* reading the options.
4. **Run each choice through the three filters.** Too narrow? Too broad? Off-stance? Eliminate on the first failure; don't keep reading a dead choice.
5. **Check both halves.** If the passage pivoted, confirm the survivor covers X *and* Y and their relationship.
6. **Verb-match the survivor.** Confirm its opening verb matches the stance you fixed in step 2. If it doesn't, you mis-eliminated — restart at step 4.

> **Recall check.** Before reading the five choices, what two things should you have already done? (Recalled the author's one-sentence claim from the first and last paragraphs, and phrased your own ~8-word prediction.) Predicting first is what makes the wrong answers look wrong instead of plausible — retrieve the habit now so it survives the clock.

**Worked example (hard — the prediction payoff).** Passage: argues that a city's celebrated drop in crime is widely credited to aggressive policing, then marshals data showing the decline began before the policing changes and tracked a national trend, concluding the policing "received credit that the broader trend deserved."

Predict first: *Argues that the policing was credited for a crime drop that a pre-existing national trend actually explains.* Now the choices:

- A) "Praises the city's aggressive policing for reducing crime." — **Off-stance.** This is the claim the author *undercuts*.
- B) "Argues that a national trend, not local policing, primarily drove the city's crime decline." — **Correct.** Matches the prediction almost word for word.
- C) "Reports that crime declined in the city over the period studied." — **Too narrow.** A fact inside the passage, not its argument.
- D) "Compares crime rates across several major cities." — **Too broad / off-topic.** The national trend is context, not a city-by-city comparison.

Because you predicted, B leapt out and A's bait lost its pull. That is the entire point of step 3: a prediction converts a four-way judgment call into a fast match.

**Common mistakes.**

- **Grabbing the most dramatic option.** Correct main-idea answers are measured because they have to be complete. Drama usually signals overstatement (off-stance).
- **Rewarding accuracy over scope.** A choice can be 100% true about one paragraph and still be too narrow. True ≠ main.
- **Matching keywords instead of claims.** A choice that repeats the passage's vocabulary can still get the *relationship* wrong. Match the move, not the nouns.
- **Stopping at the pivot.** On two-part passages, picking the first half ("climate caused it") and ignoring the "However" that reframes everything.

**Recap.**

- The main idea is the author's **claim about the topic**, not the topic itself.
- It lives in the **first or last paragraph**; the middle carries evidence.
- Kill wrong answers with three filters: **too narrow, too broad, off-stance.**
- **Verb-match** the answer's opening verb to the author's stance before committing.
- On two-part passages, the correct answer covers **both halves** and their relationship — never just the louder one.
- **Predict before you read the choices** — a one-sentence prediction turns the question into a matching task and disarms the "true but narrow" bait.
