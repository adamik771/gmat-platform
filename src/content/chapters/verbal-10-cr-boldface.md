## @cr-boldface

Two portions of an argument arrive marked in **bold**, and the question asks one thing: what *role* does each bold portion play? This is not a comprehension test. It is a structural-labeling test. You are not asked whether the argument is good, whether the bold claim is true, or what the author should do next. You are asked to name the **function** of each bolded sentence inside the argument's machinery.

**Mental model.** Think of an argument as a small assembly line. Every sentence is doing one job: it is the thing being built (the **main conclusion**), a part feeding the line (a **premise**), a half-built sub-assembly that then feeds the final product (an **intermediate conclusion**), a rival product the author is knocking off the shelf (an **opposing view / counter-claim**), or a small "yes, that's fair" the author grants before pivoting (a **concession**). Boldface just shines a light on two stations and asks: which job is happening here?

The five roles, memorized cold, are your entire vocabulary:

- **Main conclusion** — the primary claim the whole argument advances.
- **Premise (evidence)** — a fact or claim offered in *support* of a conclusion.
- **Intermediate / subsidiary conclusion** — a claim the author *draws from* evidence and then *uses as* evidence for the main conclusion. It is both a conclusion and a premise.
- **Opposing view / counter-claim** — a position the author mentions, usually to dispute or refute.
- **Concession** — something the author grants to the other side before arguing their own point.

The method is three moves, in order:

1. **Find the main conclusion first, ignoring the bold entirely.** Read the argument as if nothing were bolded. Ask: what single claim is everything else here built to support? Lock that down before you look at a single bold phrase.
2. **Label each bold portion against that conclusion.** For each one ask: does it *support* the main conclusion (premise), *is* it the main conclusion, does it *oppose* the author's position (counter-claim), or is it a *stepping-stone* claim that supports the main conclusion but is itself supported by something earlier (intermediate conclusion)?
3. **Match to answer choices.** The choices use a tiny, repeated vocabulary — "supports," "is the conclusion," "is a claim the author disputes," "is evidence for the author's position." Translate your label into theirs.

**Worked example.** *"Some economists argue that raising the minimum wage will sharply reduce employment. But the most recent data from four comparable states show no such drop after their wage increases. The proposed increase, therefore, should not be expected to cost jobs."* Suppose bold-1 is "**raising the minimum wage will sharply reduce employment**" and bold-2 is "**the proposed increase should not be expected to cost jobs**." Find the main conclusion by ignoring the bold: it is the final sentence. So bold-2 *is* the main conclusion. Bold-1 is the view the author argues *against* — the opposing claim, flagged by "Some economists argue" and the pivot "But." The right answer: "Bold-1 is a position the author disputes; bold-2 is the conclusion the author draws."

**Worked example.** *"The new drug clears the infection faster in trials. **Faster clearance means fewer days a patient can transmit the illness.** So **adopting the drug would slow the disease's spread through the community.**"* Main conclusion: the last sentence about slowing spread. Bold-2 *is* that main conclusion. Bold-1 sits in the middle — it is *drawn from* the trial result and is then *used to support* the spread claim. That dual job makes it an **intermediate conclusion**, not a plain premise. Spotting that "it is supported *and* it supports" distinction is what hard boldface questions hinge on; the trap answer here calls bold-1 merely "evidence," ignoring that the trial result props it up first.

| Stem wording | What it signals |
|---|---|
| "…the role played by the boldface portions…" | Boldface — label the function of each |
| "…the two portions in boldface play which of the following roles…" | Boldface — two-role match |
| "…the first boldface portion and the second boldface portion…" | Boldface — watch for asymmetric roles |
| "Some argue / critics claim … but / however / yet …" | A cited view the author is about to dispute |

**Trap to watch.** The **cited-view trap**. When an argument quotes an opposing position to knock it down, students label that bold portion as the author's evidence or even the conclusion — because it is stated forcefully and sits up front. Ask of every bold sentence: *whose claim is this?* Signal words — "some argue," "critics claim," "it is widely held," followed by "but," "however," "yet" — mark a view the author is about to dispute. The author's own conclusion is what the passage *builds toward*, not what it opens by attacking.

**Trap to watch.** Collapsing **intermediate conclusion** into plain **premise**. A raw premise is supported by nothing else in the passage; an intermediate conclusion is itself supported by an earlier fact and then does support-work of its own. If a bold claim has its own backing earlier in the argument, it is not a raw premise — and the answer choice that calls it merely "evidence" is the trap.

> **Recall check.** Name the five roles a bold portion can play. (Main conclusion; premise/evidence; intermediate conclusion; opposing view/counter-claim; concession.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why must you find the main conclusion *before* labeling any bold portion? (Because every other role is defined *relative to* the conclusion — "supports it," "opposes it," "is a step toward it" — so without the conclusion fixed, the labels have no anchor and you will mis-assign direction.)

The discipline that wins boldface: read structurally, not for content. You are a building inspector walking the assembly line, tagging each station with its job. Find the product first, then label every other station by what it does *for* or *against* that product. (Boldface has a mirror type — complete-the-argument, which asks you to supply a missing conclusion rather than label existing roles — but everything you need here is the labeling discipline above.)
