## @summary

- Classify each bold statement first as a fact, claim, evidence, objection, or conclusion.
- Then identify whose position it represents and how it relates to the argument's main conclusion.
- Match both roles together; an answer with one correct half and one wrong half is fully wrong.

## @cr-boldface

Two portions of an argument arrive marked in **bold**, and the question asks one thing: what *role* does each bold portion play? This is not a comprehension test. It is a structural-labeling test. You are not asked whether the argument is good, whether the bold claim is true, or what the author should do next. You are asked to name the **function** of each bolded sentence inside the argument's machinery. Everything below is built to make that one move fast and automatic.

**Mental model.** Think of an argument as a small assembly line. Every sentence is doing one job: it is the thing being built (the **main conclusion**), a part feeding the line (a **premise**), a half-built sub-assembly that then feeds the final product (an **intermediate conclusion**), a rival product the author is knocking off the shelf (an **opposing view / counter-claim**), or a small "yes, that's fair" the author grants before pivoting (a **concession**). Boldface just shines a light on two stations and asks: which job is happening here? Notice that the light tells you *nothing* about the job — a bold sentence can be the most important claim in the passage or the least, the author's own or someone else's. The bolding is a spotlight, not a verdict. The single biggest source of wrong answers is treating the spotlight *as* a verdict: assuming that because a sentence is bolded and forceful, it must be the conclusion.

The five roles, memorized cold, are your entire vocabulary:

- **Main conclusion** — the primary claim the whole argument advances.
- **Premise (evidence)** — a fact or claim offered in *support* of a conclusion.
- **Intermediate / subsidiary conclusion** — a claim the author *draws from* evidence and then *uses as* evidence for the main conclusion. It is both a conclusion and a premise.
- **Opposing view / counter-claim** — a position the author mentions, usually to dispute or refute.
- **Concession** — something the author grants to the other side before arguing their own point.

A concession deserves a closer look, because it is the role students most often miss. A concession is a true-or-fair point the author *accepts* but that cuts *against* the author's conclusion — and the author accepts it anyway, then pivots ("Granted, X; nevertheless, Y"). It is not the opposing view's main thrust; it is a small piece of common ground. The tell is a softening word — "admittedly," "to be sure," "it is true that," "of course" — usually followed by a "but" that resumes the author's case. The key contrast to burn in: an opposing view is a claim the author **denies**; a concession is a claim the author **accepts** and then argues *around*. Same direction (against the conclusion), opposite treatment by the author.

> **Recall check.** Without looking up, list the five roles a bold portion can play and say which two are the same sentence wearing two hats. (Main conclusion; premise/evidence; intermediate conclusion; opposing view/counter-claim; concession — the intermediate conclusion is both conclusion *and* premise. Retrieving the list now, from memory, builds a far stronger trace than re-reading the bullets above; the small struggle is the learning.)

The method is three moves, in order:

1. **Find the main conclusion first, ignoring the bold entirely.** Read the argument as if nothing were bolded. Ask: what single claim is everything else here built to support? Lock that down before you look at a single bold phrase.
2. **Label each bold portion against that conclusion.** For each one ask: does it *support* the main conclusion (premise), *is* it the main conclusion, does it *oppose* the author's position (counter-claim or concession), or is it a *stepping-stone* claim that supports the main conclusion but is itself supported by something earlier (intermediate conclusion)?
3. **Match to answer choices.** The choices use a tiny, repeated vocabulary — "supports," "is the conclusion," "is a claim the author disputes," "is evidence for the author's position." Translate your label into theirs.

A more memorizable version of step 2, when you reach a bold portion and want to fix its role fast, is this short numbered drill you can run on every bold sentence:

1. **Whose claim is it?** Author's, or someone else's the author cites? If someone else's and the author pushes back, it is an opposing view or a concession — go to step 1a, then stop.
2. **1a — accepted or denied?** If the author grants it as true and pivots ("admittedly … but"), it is a **concession**. If the author denies or undercuts it ("some claim … yet the data show"), it is an **opposing view**.
3. **Does anything in the passage support this claim?** If yes, it is a conclusion of some kind (main or intermediate); if nothing supports it, it is a raw premise.
4. **Does this claim itself support something later?** If yes *and* it had its own support, it is an **intermediate conclusion**. If it supports something but is the end of the chain, it is the **main conclusion**.
5. **Re-read the answer choice in those words** — "the author disputes it," "evidence the author uses," "the conclusion the author draws" — and reject any choice that gets the *direction* (for/against) wrong, even if the noun is right.

**Worked example (straightforward).** *"Some economists argue that raising the minimum wage will sharply reduce employment. But the most recent data from four comparable states show no such drop after their wage increases. The proposed increase, therefore, should not be expected to cost jobs."* Suppose bold-1 is "**raising the minimum wage will sharply reduce employment**" and bold-2 is "**the proposed increase should not be expected to cost jobs**." Find the main conclusion by ignoring the bold: it is the final sentence, flagged by "therefore." So bold-2 *is* the main conclusion. Bold-1 is the view the author argues *against* — the opposing claim, flagged by "Some economists argue" and the pivot "But." The right answer: "Bold-1 is a position the author disputes; bold-2 is the conclusion the author draws."

**Worked example (medium).** *"The new drug clears the infection faster in trials. **Faster clearance means fewer days a patient can transmit the illness.** So **adopting the drug would slow the disease's spread through the community.**"* Main conclusion: the last sentence about slowing spread. Bold-2 *is* that main conclusion. Bold-1 sits in the middle — it is *drawn from* the trial result and is then *used to support* the spread claim. That dual job makes it an **intermediate conclusion**, not a plain premise. Spotting that "it is supported *and* it supports" distinction is what hard boldface questions hinge on; the trap answer here calls bold-1 merely "evidence," ignoring that the trial result props it up first.

**Worked example (medium, concession).** *"**Admittedly, the museum's new wing cost far more than projected.** But attendance has doubled since it opened, and the additional ticket revenue already exceeds the overrun. **The expansion was, on balance, a sound investment.**"* Main conclusion: the last sentence — the expansion was sound. Bold-2 *is* it. Bold-1 is a point that cuts *against* that conclusion (it cost too much), yet the author grants it as true before pivoting on "But." That is a **concession**, not an opposing view the author refutes — the author never denies the cost overrun; the author accepts it and argues the benefits outweigh it. The trap answer labels bold-1 "a claim the author disputes." The author disputes nothing about the cost; "admittedly" is the giveaway.

> **Recall check.** What single question separates a *concession* from an *opposing view the author refutes*? (Does the author *accept* the claim as true and argue around it — concession — or *deny* it — opposing view? Force yourself to answer before reading on; this contrast is exactly where mid-difficulty boldface questions live, and recalling it now is worth more than skimming the paragraph again.)

**Worked example (hard, asymmetric roles).** *"**Critics of the highway expansion insist it will simply fill with new traffic and relieve no congestion.** Yet in three cities that widened comparable roads, peak-hour travel times fell and stayed lower for a decade. **The induced-demand objection, while not baseless, is overstated for projects of this scale.**"* Step 1, ignore the bold: the main conclusion is the last sentence — the objection is overstated. Bold-2 *is* the main conclusion, with one wrinkle: "while not baseless" is a *built-in concession* folded into the conclusion sentence, but the sentence's thrust is still the author's own judgment, so it is the main conclusion. Bold-1 is the critics' position, stated up front and then undercut by "Yet" and the three-city evidence — an **opposing view the author argues against**. The asymmetry is the whole point: bold-1 (someone else's claim, disputed) and bold-2 (the author's own conclusion) play *opposite* roles, and the correct answer must capture both directions. A trap choice calls bold-1 "evidence the author uses to support the conclusion" — exactly backwards; the author uses the three-city data, not the critics' claim, as support.

**Worked example (hard, double conclusion-y bait).** *"**Because solar panel prices have fallen by 80% in a decade, rooftop installation is now cheaper than grid power in most regions.** Utilities therefore face shrinking demand. **They will need to rethink their pricing models or risk losing residential customers entirely.**"* Find the main conclusion: the final sentence about utilities rethinking pricing. Bold-2 *is* it. Bold-1 looks like a premise because it opens the argument, but it has its own support inside it — "Because prices have fallen 80%" supports "installation is now cheaper" — and that cheaper-than-grid claim then drives "utilities face shrinking demand," which drives the final claim. So bold-1 is an **intermediate conclusion**: supported by the price-drop fact and supporting the chain that follows. The trap labels it "the main conclusion" (it is forceful and first) or "a premise" (it has its own backing). Neither fits; it is the middle link.

| Stem wording | What it signals |
|---|---|
| "…the role played by the boldface portions…" | Boldface — label the function of each |
| "…the two portions in boldface play which of the following roles…" | Boldface — two-role match |
| "…the first boldface portion and the second boldface portion…" | Boldface — watch for asymmetric roles |
| "Some argue / critics claim … but / however / yet …" | A cited view the author is about to dispute |
| "Admittedly / to be sure / it is true that … but …" | A concession — granted, then pivoted away from |
| "Because / since X, Y. Therefore Z." | A chain — Y is likely an intermediate conclusion |

**Trap to watch.** The **cited-view trap**. When an argument quotes an opposing position to knock it down, students label that bold portion as the author's evidence or even the conclusion — because it is stated forcefully and sits up front. Ask of every bold sentence: *whose claim is this?* Signal words — "some argue," "critics claim," "it is widely held," followed by "but," "however," "yet" — mark a view the author is about to dispute. The author's own conclusion is what the passage *builds toward*, not what it opens by attacking. Position on the page is the bait: in these arguments the loudest, earliest sentence is the one the author is there to demolish.

**Trap to watch.** Collapsing **intermediate conclusion** into plain **premise**. A raw premise is supported by nothing else in the passage; an intermediate conclusion is itself supported by an earlier fact and then does support-work of its own. If a bold claim has its own backing earlier in the argument, it is not a raw premise — and the answer choice that calls it merely "evidence" is the trap.

> **Recall check.** Quick: which two of the three diagnostic questions — *whose claim is it?*, *is it supported?*, *does it support something?* — must you answer to tell an intermediate conclusion from a raw premise? (The last two: an intermediate conclusion *is supported* by something earlier **and** *supports* something later; a raw premise does neither — it only supports. Retrieving the pair now, rather than re-skimming, is what makes the distinction stick under time pressure.)

> **Self-explanation prompt.** Why must you find the main conclusion *before* labeling any bold portion? (Because every other role is defined *relative to* the conclusion — "supports it," "opposes it," "is a step toward it" — so without the conclusion fixed, the labels have no anchor and you will mis-assign direction.)

**Common mistakes.**

- **Reading for content, not structure.** Students decide whether the argument is *convincing* and pick the answer that matches their opinion. Boldface never asks if the argument is good — only what each part *does*.
- **Letting "right noun, wrong direction" slide.** A choice can correctly call bold-1 a "claim" or "position" but get the for/against backwards (says the author *supports* it when the author *disputes* it). Check direction on every choice.
- **Treating forceful or first-placed sentences as the conclusion.** Emphasis and position are bait. The conclusion is what the passage *builds toward*, found by structure, not volume.
- **Flattening intermediate conclusions to premises.** If a bold claim has its own support earlier in the passage, "evidence" is the wrong label even though it does feed a later claim.

**Recap.**

- Boldface asks for the **role** each bold portion plays, not whether the argument is sound or the claim true.
- The five roles: **main conclusion, premise, intermediate conclusion, opposing view, concession** — memorized cold.
- Method: **find the main conclusion first (ignoring the bold), label each bold portion relative to it, then translate your label into the answer's wording.**
- The two killers are the **cited-view trap** (someone else's disputed claim mistaken for the author's) and the **intermediate-conclusion-as-premise** collapse — both solved by asking *whose claim is this?* and *does anything support it?*
- Always verify **direction** (for vs. against) on each answer choice before selecting.

The discipline that wins boldface: read structurally, not for content. You are a building inspector walking the assembly line, tagging each station with its job. Find the product first, then label every other station by what it does *for* or *against* that product. (Boldface has a mirror type — complete-the-argument, which asks you to supply a missing conclusion rather than label existing roles — but everything you need here is the labeling discipline above.)
