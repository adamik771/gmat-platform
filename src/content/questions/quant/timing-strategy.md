## Q1
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Timing Strategy

Thirty seconds into a Quant question, you have no path to the answer — but you notice the five choices are concrete numbers listed in ascending order. What is the best next move?

- A) Push on with the algebra — switching now wastes the thirty seconds already invested
- B) Test the answer choices directly, starting from the middle one
- C) Guess immediately and bookmark — thirty seconds with no path means the question is unwinnable
- D) Re-read the problem from the beginning at a slower pace
- E) Close your eyes for a few seconds to reset, then try the same approach again

**answer:** B
**hint_nudge:** The 30-second checkpoint asks two things: do I have a path, and if not, does a faster method exist? Look at what the answer choices offer.
**hint_strategy:** Concrete, sorted choices are the trigger condition for one specific method — one that doesn't need you to find a path at all.
**hint_setup:** Sorted numeric choices mean you can backsolve: plug the middle choice into the stem's condition and let too-big/too-small cut the field.
**explanation:** The 30-second checkpoint has three outcomes: path found (execute), no path but a faster method exists (switch), no path and no alternative (guess, bookmark, move). This situation is the middle case. Sorted concrete choices are exactly the trigger for backsolving — testing the middle choice against the stem's condition, which doesn't require the forward path you failed to find. The answer is B. Choice A is the sunk-cost reflex: the thirty seconds are spent either way, and more of the same approach buys nothing. Choice C skips a step — guess-and-bookmark is correct only when no faster method exists, and here one plainly does. D and E spend clock without changing method, which is what the checkpoint exists to prevent.
**fastest_path:** No path + sorted numeric choices = switch to backsolving from the middle choice.
**common_trap:** Treating the checkpoint as a binary "solve or skip" (choice C) and skipping questions that a method switch would crack in under a minute.
**takeaway:** The checkpoint's middle branch is where points are won: before you bail on a question, scan the choices for a backsolve, plug-in, or estimation opening.

## Q2
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Timing Strategy

The GMAT Quant section gives you 45 minutes for 21 questions. You finish the first 7 questions in 10 minutes 30 seconds. Relative to even pacing, where do you stand?

- A) 4 minutes 30 seconds behind
- B) Exactly on pace
- C) 1 minute 30 seconds ahead
- D) 4 minutes 30 seconds ahead
- E) 7 minutes 30 seconds ahead

**answer:** D
**hint_nudge:** Work out what even pacing says 7 questions should cost, then compare to what you actually spent.
**hint_strategy:** Seven questions is exactly one third of 21 — so the even-pace budget for them is one third of 45 minutes.
**hint_setup:** One third of 45 minutes is 15 minutes. You used 10:30 — find the difference.
**explanation:** Even pacing spends time in proportion to questions: 7 of 21 questions is one third of the section, so the budget is one third of 45 minutes — 15 minutes. You spent 10:30, so you are 15:00 − 10:30 = 4:30 ahead. The answer is D. The useful habit isn't the per-question figure (45/21 is an awkward 2:08.6) but the checkpoint fractions: a third of the way through should cost about 15 minutes, halfway about 22:30, two thirds about 30. Being 4:30 ahead is the system working — that surplus is the budget you'll spend on the hard problems waiting in the back half, not a license to slow down and "use it up" on the next easy one.
**fastest_path:** 7 of 21 is a third; a third of 45 is 15; 15:00 minus 10:30 is 4:30 ahead.
**common_trap:** Computing against the awkward per-question average instead of the clean one-third checkpoint, and burning seconds on the meta-arithmetic itself.
**takeaway:** Track pace at the fraction checkpoints — one third in 15 minutes, half in 22:30 — and treat any surplus as capital reserved for the hard questions ahead.

## Q3
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Timing Strategy

On the GMAT, every Quant question is worth the same toward your score. Why does pacing doctrine still say to protect the easy questions rather than fight for the hard ones?

- A) Easy questions are secretly weighted more heavily by the scoring algorithm
- B) Hard questions are usually unscored experimental questions
- C) A missed easy question and a missed hard question cost the same point — but the easy miss was avoidable, and the time saved on easy questions is what funds careful work everywhere else
- D) Easy questions always appear first, so rushing them ruins your early-section score disproportionately
- E) Examiners review easy-question errors more strictly than hard-question errors

**answer:** C
**hint_nudge:** Compare two ways of losing the same point: a hard question you fought and lost, and an easy one you rushed and fumbled.
**hint_strategy:** Think in expected points per minute. A minute spent securing a near-certain easy point buys more than a minute spent dragging a hard question from hopeless to unlikely.
**hint_setup:** The hard point was probably lost either way; the easy point was yours unless you gave it away. Which loss did your time allocation actually cause?
**explanation:** Equal point values are exactly why triage works. When you sink three minutes into a hard problem and still miss it, you lose a point you were unlikely to earn — but the two rushed easy questions that minute-shortage causes later are points you already had and gave back. The easy miss is a self-inflicted loss; the hard miss was mostly priced in. The answer is C. Time, not knowledge, is the scarce resource: a minute banked on an easy question converts to careful, slip-free work later at near-certain value, while the same minute spent "almost there" on a stubborn problem converts at long odds. A and B invent scoring mechanics; D and E dress the right conclusion in wrong reasons.
**fastest_path:** Compare what each lost point cost you: the hard one was long odds regardless; the easy one was a sure point traded away.
**common_trap:** Reasoning that equal values mean equal time deserts — pride-fighting the hard question while the recoverable points quietly expire behind it.
**takeaway:** Bank time on the questions you're sure of and cap losses on the ones you're not; the surplus, not heroics, is what raises the score.

## Q4
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Timing Strategy

At the 30-second checkpoint, which of the following situations calls for guessing, bookmarking, and moving on immediately?

- A) You have a clear path to the answer, but it will take another 90 seconds to execute
- B) You have no path, but the choices are concrete sorted numbers you could test
- C) You have no path, and no faster method — backsolving, plugging in, estimating — applies
- D) You have an answer, but want to re-verify the arithmetic before committing
- E) The problem looks long and you haven't finished reading it yet

**answer:** C
**hint_nudge:** Guess-and-move is the checkpoint's last resort, not its default. What has to be true for it to fire?
**hint_strategy:** Walk the decision tree: path found means execute; no path but a faster method exists means switch. The bail-out branch is what remains.
**hint_setup:** Eliminate every option where a path exists or a method switch is available — the one left over is the guess-bookmark-move case.
**explanation:** The checkpoint decision tree has three branches. Path found — execute without second-guessing: that covers A (90 more seconds on a clear path is a fine trade) and D (verify briefly and commit). No path but a faster method available — switch: that's B, the backsolving trigger. The bail-out branch fires only when both conditions fail: no path and no alternative method. That is C, and the right response is to eliminate what you can, guess, bookmark for end-of-section review, and bank the saved minutes. The answer is C. E isn't a checkpoint state at all — you can't evaluate paths on a problem you haven't read. The discipline cuts both ways: bailing with a path is panic and burns a winnable point; grinding without a path or a switch is the time-sink the checkpoint exists to kill.
**fastest_path:** Bail only when both questions — "do I have a path?" and "does a faster method apply?" — come back no.
**common_trap:** Bailing on slow-but-solvable questions (A) while grinding on hopeless ones — using the guess as an escape from effort rather than from deadlock.
**takeaway:** Guess-bookmark-move is the checkpoint's third branch, not its first: it fires only after both the path check and the method-switch check fail.

## Q5
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Timing Strategy

You are 2 minutes 45 seconds into a hard Quant question and feel you are almost there — probably 30 more seconds. Your pre-set per-question ceiling is 2:45. What does pacing discipline say to do?

- A) Give it the 30 seconds — being this close means the investment is about to pay off
- B) Cut it now: pick the best remaining choice, bookmark it, and move — the ceiling was set before the question started, and "almost there" is how the spiral talks
- C) Start the problem over with a fresh method, since the current one took too long
- D) Spend up to 4 minutes — hard questions justify extra time when you are making progress
- E) Abandon your answer work and leave it blank to save time

**answer:** B
**hint_nudge:** Ask who is estimating that "30 more seconds" — the planner who set the ceiling calmly, or the solver who is 2:45 deep in the problem?
**hint_strategy:** The ceiling exists precisely because in-the-moment estimates are unreliable: every sunk-cost spiral is a chain of credible-sounding "just 30 more seconds."
**hint_setup:** Compare the costs: 30 more seconds at uncertain payoff, versus the same time protecting questions you can definitely convert — and note the bookmark means the question isn't even gone.
**explanation:** The ceiling's entire value is that it was set in advance, by a calm planner, instead of negotiated in the moment by a solver under sunk-cost pressure. At 2:45 deep, "30 more seconds" is exactly what the spiral always says — and it said it at 2:00 and 2:30 too. The disciplined move is B: eliminate what you can, pick the best remaining choice, bookmark, and move. The bookmark is what makes this cheap — GMAT Focus lets you revisit and change up to three answers at section's end, so a guess on a nearly-solved question is a prime review candidate, not a forfeited point. A and D renegotiate the limit mid-spiral, which is the same as having no limit; C restarts a clock that's already spent; E is strictly worse than guessing, since a blank can't be right and a bookmark preserves your work either way.
**fastest_path:** The ceiling decides, not the feeling: best remaining guess, bookmark, move — the review screen keeps the question alive.
**common_trap:** Granting "just 30 more seconds" because you feel close — the one extension always becomes three, and the overrun comes out of easy questions later.
**takeaway:** Set the per-question ceiling before the section and obey it mechanically; the bookmark-and-review safety net is what makes cutting losses nearly free.
**related_reading:** quant-30-timing
