## Q1
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Exponent Rules

What is the value of 2⁵ * 2³?

- A) 2⁸
- B) 2¹⁵
- C) 4⁸
- D) 4¹⁵
- E) 2²

**answer:** A
**fastest_path:** Same base → add exponents: 2⁵ × 2³ = 2⁸.
**explanation:** Product of same-base powers: a^m × a^n = a^(m+n). So 2⁵ × 2³ = 2^(5+3) = 2⁸. The base stays at 2; only the exponents combine.
**mistake_b:** Multiplied exponents: 5 × 3 = 15 → 2¹⁵.
**mistake_c:** Multiplied bases: 2 × 2 = 4, then added exponents → 4⁸.
**mistake_d:** Multiplied bases AND exponents → 4¹⁵.
**mistake_e:** Subtracted exponents instead of adding → 2².
**common_trap:** Multiplying the bases as well as the exponents.
**takeaway:** Same base, multiplication: add exponents (a^m × a^n = a^(m+n)). The base never changes during this operation.

## Q2
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Roots & Radicals

Simplify √50.

- A) 5
- B) 5*√2
- C) 2*√5
- D) 10*√5
- E) 25*√2

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** 50 = 25 × 2 → √50 = 5√2.
**explanation:** Pull out the largest perfect-square factor. 50 = 25 × 2. So √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2.
**mistake_a:** Computed only √25 = 5 (forgot the √2).
**mistake_c:** Reversed factor placement: 2√5 from 50 = 4 × 12.5 (incorrect setup).
**mistake_d:** Misfactored as 100 × 0.5; got 10√5.
**mistake_e:** Pulled out 2 instead of 25; got 25√2 (squared the wrong factor).
**common_trap:** Pulling out the smaller factor (e.g., √2) instead of the largest perfect square.
**takeaway:** To simplify √n, factor n into (perfect square × remaining). Memorize perfect squares: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100.

Express 0.00045 in scientific notation.

**answer:** C

**takeaway:** Scientific notation: 1 ≤ |coefficient| < 10, integer exponent. Move decimal to put exactly one non-zero digit before the decimal point; count moves for the exponent (negative for small numbers).

## Q5

**topic:** Exponent Rules

What is the value of 3⁻²?

- A) -9
- B) -6
- C) -1/9
- D) 1/6
- E) 1/9

**answer:** E
**fastest_path:** Negative exponent → reciprocal: 3⁻² = 1/3² = 1/9.

**mistake_a:** Treated 3⁻² as −(3²) = −9.
**mistake_b:** Treated 3⁻² as (−3)² × something = −6 (sloppy sign manipulation).
**mistake_c:** Got the reciprocal but kept the negative: −1/9.
**mistake_d:** Computed 1/(3 × 2) = 1/6 (multiplied base × exponent in denominator).
**common_trap:** Treating negative exponents as making the result negative.
**takeaway:** a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. Negative exponents flip into denominators; they never produce negative values from positive bases.

## Q6
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Exponent Rules

Which of the following equals (x⁴ * y⁻²) / (x⁻¹ * y³)?

- A) x³ * y⁻⁵
- B) x⁵ * y⁻⁵
- C) x⁵ * y⁻¹
- D) x³ * y⁻¹
- E) x⁵ * y

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** Subtract exponents: x^(4−(−1)) × y^(−2−3) = x⁵ × y⁻⁵.
**explanation:** Same-base division: a^m / a^n = a^(m−n). For x: 4 − (−1) = 5 → x⁵. For y: (−2) − 3 = −5 → y⁻⁵. Result: x⁵ × y⁻⁵.
**mistake_a:** x: 4 − 1 = 3 (forgot the double-negative).
**mistake_c:** y: −2 + 3 = 1 (added instead of subtracted), got y⁻¹.
**mistake_d:** Both errors: x³ and y⁻¹.
**mistake_e:** y exponent: subtracted wrong way, got y⁺¹.
**common_trap:** Mishandling double-negatives — subtracting a negative is *adding*.
**takeaway:** a^m / a^n = a^(m−n). When the denominator's exponent is negative, the subtraction becomes addition (m − (−n) = m + n).
**related_reading:** reading-quant-04-algebra-and-equations

## Q7
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Roots & Radicals

Simplify √12 + √27.

- A) √39
- B) 3*√13
- C) 5*√3
- D) 6*√3
- E) 13*√3

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** Simplify: √12 = 2√3, √27 = 3√3. Sum = 5√3.
**explanation:** Radicals only add when they share the same radicand. Simplify each: √12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3; √27 = √(9 × 3) = 3√3. Both share √3, so add coefficients: 2√3 + 3√3 = 5√3.
**mistake_a:** Computed √(12 + 27) = √39 (cannot add radicands inside one root).
**mistake_b:** Computed wrong simplification; got 3√13.
**mistake_d:** Coefficient addition slip: 2 + 3 = 6 instead of 5.
**mistake_e:** Added radicands and coefficients: (2+3)√(12+27) → 5√39 (wrong); rounded.
**common_trap:** Adding inside the radical (√a + √b ≠ √(a + b)) — this is one of the most common mistakes.
**takeaway:** Add radicals only after simplifying to matching radicands. √a + √b = √(a + b) is *false*; treat radicals like variables — only add when they're the same "kind."
**related_reading:** reading-quant-04-algebra-and-equations

## Q8
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Fractional Exponents

What is the value of 27^(2/3)?

- A) 3
- B) 6
- C) 9
- D) 18
- E) 81

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** 27^(2/3) = (27^(1/3))² = 3² = 9.
**explanation:** Fractional exponent rule: a^(m/n) = (a^(1/n))^m. So 27^(2/3) = (27^(1/3))² = 3² = 9. Take the root first (smaller numbers); raising to the power second.
**mistake_a:** Computed 27^(1/3) = 3 and stopped (didn't square).
**mistake_b:** Computed 27 × 2/3 = 18, halved to ~6.
**mistake_d:** Computed 27 × 2/3 = 18.
**mistake_e:** Computed 27² / 3 = 729/3 = 243 (raised to 2 first, then divided by 3 — wrong).
**common_trap:** Treating a^(m/n) as a × m/n or as a^m / n.
**takeaway:** a^(m/n) = (a^(1/n))^m = (n-th root of a) raised to m. Take root first when n divides cleanly; small numbers are easier.
**related_reading:** reading-quant-04-algebra-and-equations

## Q11

**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Exponent Rules

If (2^a)³ = 2¹², what is a?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 6
- E) 9

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** (2^a)³ = 2^(3a) = 2¹² → 3a = 12 → a = 4.

**mistake_a:** Computed 12 / 6 = 2 (used wrong divisor).

**mistake_e:** Computed 12 − 3 = 9.
**common_trap:** Confusing (a^m)^n = a^(mn) with a^m × a^n = a^(m+n).
**takeaway:** Power of a power → multiply: (a^m)^n = a^(mn). Different from same-base product (add exponents). Memorize both.

## Q12
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Roots & Radicals

Rationalize the denominator: 6 / (√3 + 1).

- A) 6*√3 - 6
- B) 3*√3 - 3
- C) 3*√3 + 3
- D) 2*√3 + 2
- E) √3 - 1

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** Multiply by conjugate (√3 − 1)/(√3 − 1): denom = 3 − 1 = 2; numer = 6(√3 − 1)/2 = 3√3 − 3.
**explanation:** To clear a sum/difference with a radical in the denominator, multiply by the *conjugate* (flip the sign between terms). Numerator: 6 × (√3 − 1) = 6√3 − 6. Denominator: (√3 + 1)(√3 − 1) = 3 − 1 = 2 (difference of squares). Result: (6√3 − 6)/2 = 3√3 − 3.
**mistake_a:** Computed 6(√3 − 1) without dividing by 2.
**mistake_c:** Used the same sign instead of conjugate.
**mistake_d:** Divided by 3 instead of 2; got 2√3 − 2.
**mistake_e:** Cancelled too aggressively; got √3 − 1.
**common_trap:** Forgetting to use the conjugate's flipped sign — multiplying by (√3 + 1)/(√3 + 1) doesn't clear the radical.
**takeaway:** Rationalize sums/differences with radicals using the *conjugate*: (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b². The result has no radical in the denominator.
**related_reading:** reading-quant-04-algebra-and-equations

## Q18
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Data Sufficiency
**topic:** Sign Analysis with Exponents

Is x > 0?

(1) x⁵ > 0.
(2) x² < x.

- A) Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) alone is not sufficient.
- B) Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) alone is not sufficient.
- C) BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER statement ALONE is sufficient.
- D) EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- E) Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** (1) Odd power preserves sign: x⁵ > 0 → x > 0. (2) x² < x → 0 < x < 1 → x > 0. Both alone sufficient → D.
**explanation:** Statement (1): x⁵ > 0. Odd powers preserve sign — if x were negative, x⁵ would be negative. So x > 0. Sufficient.

Statement (2): x² < x rearranges to x − x² > 0 → x(1 − x) > 0. This holds when x and (1 − x) have the same sign. Both positive: 0 < x < 1. Both negative: x < 0 AND x > 1 (impossible). So 0 < x < 1, which is x > 0. Sufficient.

Each alone works → D.
**mistake_a:** Concluded only Statement 1 sufficient, missed Statement 2's resolution.
**mistake_b:** Concluded only Statement 2 sufficient.
**mistake_c:** Required both statements.
**mistake_e:** Concluded together insufficient.
**common_trap:** Assuming Statement 2 admits negative x without checking the sign diagram.
**takeaway:** Odd powers preserve sign (x⁵ > 0 ⟺ x > 0). For x² < x, factor: x(1 − x) > 0 → 0 < x < 1 (both positive case).
**related_reading:** reading-di-02-data-sufficiency-logic

**mistake_e:** Found x = 2 correctly but then computed 3^(x+1) = 27 or misread the question.
**common_trap:** Two compounding traps: (1) not recognizing the quadratic-in-disguise requiring substitution, and (2) answering x = 2 instead of 3^x = 9 because the question asks for 3^x, not x.
**takeaway:** When you see a^(2x) and a^x in the same equation, substitute u = a^x to convert to a standard quadratic in u. Reject any negative root (a^x > 0 for a > 0). Re-read the question — it may ask for a^x, not x.

## Q39
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Fractional Exponents

If x > 1, which of the following is always greater than x?

- A) x^(1/2)
- B) x^(3/2)
- C) x^(−1)
- D) x^(2/3)
- E) 2/x

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** x^(3/2)/x = x^(1/2) > 1 for all x > 1 → x^(3/2) > x always. Every other option falls below x for some x > 1. → B.
**explanation:** Check each option for x > 1. A) x^(1/2): for x = 4, √4 = 2 < 4 — less than x. B) x^(3/2): divide by x → x^(3/2)/x = x^(1/2) > 1 for all x > 1 → x^(3/2) > x always. C) x^(−1) = 1/x < 1 < x for x > 1 — always less. D) x^(2/3): divide by x → x^(2/3)/x = x^(−1/3) < 1 for x > 1 → less than x. E) 2/x: for x = 3, 2/3 < 3. (It exceeds x only for 1 < x < √2, so not always.)
**mistake_a:** Chose x^(1/2) thinking "any positive exponent of x must produce something bigger than x" — but fractional exponents between 0 and 1 shrink the value relative to x.
**mistake_d:** Chose x^(2/3) for the same reason as A — the exponent is positive but less than 1, so the result lies between 1 and x, never above x.
**mistake_e:** Tested x = 1.2 (2/1.2 ≈ 1.67 > 1.2 ✓) and stopped — but x = 3 gives 2/3 < 3. "Always" requires ruling out every counterexample.
**mistake_c:** 1/x < 1 < x for x > 1 — clearly not greater than x.
**common_trap:** Assuming any positive exponent on x > 1 gives a result above x. Only exponents strictly greater than 1 guarantee this. Exponents between 0 and 1 give values between 1 and x.
**takeaway:** For x > 1: exponent > 1 → result > x; 0 < exponent < 1 → 1 < result < x; negative exponent → result < 1. "Always" questions require a counterexample test for every option you consider eliminating.

## Q40
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Data Sufficiency
**topic:** Exponent Manipulation

What is the value of 2^a × 3^b?

(1) 2^(a+1) × 3^b = 48
(2) a and b are positive integers.

- A) Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) alone is not sufficient.
- B) Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) alone is not sufficient.
- C) BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER alone is sufficient.
- D) EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- E) Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

**answer:** A
**fastest_path:** (1) Factor: 2^(a+1) × 3^b = 2 × (2^a × 3^b) = 48 → 2^a × 3^b = 24. Sufficient without knowing a or b individually. (2) Many integer pairs satisfy the question → not unique → insufficient. → A.
**explanation:** Statement (1): Rewrite the left side using 2^(a+1) = 2 × 2^a: 2 × (2^a × 3^b) = 48 → 2^a × 3^b = 24. This algebraic step yields the exact target value in one move, without needing to solve for a and b separately. Sufficient.

Statement (2): a and b are positive integers, but many pairs work: (a=1,b=1) → 6; (a=2,b=1) → 12; (a=3,b=1) → 24; (a=1,b=2) → 18; etc. The value is not unique. Insufficient.
**mistake_b:** Concluded only Statement 2 is sufficient — perhaps thinking "positive integers" uniquely pins down the expression.
**mistake_c:** Required both statements, assuming Statement 1 cannot determine 2^a × 3^b without knowing a and b.
**mistake_d:** Concluded each alone sufficient.
**mistake_e:** Concluded together insufficient.
**common_trap:** Thinking Statement (1) requires solving for a and b individually. The insight is that the target expression 2^a × 3^b appears directly inside Statement (1) and can be isolated with one algebraic step.
**takeaway:** In DS, scan each statement for whether the expression you need can be isolated algebraically — you often do not need individual variable values to determine a product or sum. Factor first, solve for the target quantity directly.

## Q41
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Exponent Systems

If a^(x+y) = 6 and a^(x−y) = 2, where a > 0 and a ≠ 1, what is the value of a^(2x)?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 8
- D) 12
- E) Cannot be determined

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** Multiply the two equations: a^(x+y) × a^(x−y) = a^(2x) = 6 × 2 = 12.
**explanation:** Rather than trying to solve for a, x, and y individually (impossible with two equations and three unknowns), recognize what combination of the given expressions produces the target exponent 2x. Adding the exponents: (x + y) + (x − y) = 2x. Multiplying the two equations therefore gives a^(x+y) × a^(x−y) = a^(2x) = 6 × 2 = 12. (Dividing instead gives a^(x+y)/a^(x−y) = a^(2y) = 6/2 = 3 — a useful result, but not what is asked.)
**mistake_a:** Divided the equations: a^(x+y)/a^(x−y) = a^(2y) = 3. Chose 3 — but this is a^(2y), not a^(2x).
**mistake_b:** Averaged the two given values: (6 + 2)/2 = 4.
**mistake_c:** Chose 8 via unclear reasoning (e.g., 2³ = 8).
**mistake_e:** Concluded "cannot be determined" because a, x, and y cannot each be found individually. This is the most dangerous trap — the expression a^(2x) is uniquely determined even though the individual variables are not.
**common_trap:** Two layered traps: (1) attempting to find a, x, y separately (impossible), which leads to giving up; and (2) dividing instead of multiplying, which gives a^(2y) = 3 — an answer that appears in the choices and looks credible.
**takeaway:** When two exponential equations share the same base, check whether adding or subtracting their exponents yields the target exponent. Multiplying the equations adds exponents (product rule); dividing subtracts them. Choose the operation that matches the target.
