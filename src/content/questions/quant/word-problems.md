## Q1
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Rate/Time/Distance

A train travels at 80 miles per hour. How many miles will it travel in 45 minutes?

- A) 36
- B) 45
- C) 60
- D) 80
- E) 120

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** 45 min = 3/4 hr. D = 80 × 3/4 = 60.
**explanation:** Convert time first: 45 minutes = 45/60 = 3/4 of an hour. Then D = R × T = 80 × (3/4) = 60 miles. The entire challenge is the unit conversion from minutes to hours; the multiplication is one step once the fraction is set up.
**mistake_a:** Treated 45 minutes as 0.45 hours by moving the decimal point instead of dividing by 60: 80 × 0.45 = 36. The correct conversion is 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75, not 0.45.
**mistake_b:** Grabbed the time value (45) directly as the answer in miles without applying the rate — confused the given duration in minutes with the distance in miles.
**mistake_d:** Grabbed the speed (80 mph) as the answer — read "how fast" instead of "how far."
**mistake_e:** Treated 45 minutes as 1.5 hours (multiplied 45 by 2 instead of dividing by 60): 80 × 1.5 = 120. The conversion is 45/60 = 0.75, not 45 × 2/60 = 1.5.
**common_trap:** decimal-confusion — writing 45 minutes as 0.45 hours by moving the decimal point rather than dividing by 60. The correct fraction is 45/60 = 3/4 = 0.75.
**takeaway:** To convert minutes to hours, divide by 60. 45 min = 45/60 = 3/4 hr = 0.75 hr. Never just shift the decimal point.

## Q2
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Profit/Loss

A merchant buys a product for $40 and sells it for $52. What is the merchant's profit as a percentage of the cost?

- A) 12%
- B) 20%
- C) 23%
- D) 25%
- E) 30%

**answer:** E
**fastest_path:** Profit/cost = 12/40 = 30%.
**explanation:** Profit = 52 − 40 = 12. Profit % of cost = (12/40) × 100 = 30%.
**mistake_a:** Bubbled the dollar profit (12) as a percent.
**mistake_b:** Estimated 20%.
**mistake_c:** Computed profit/sell price = 12/52 ≈ 23%.
**mistake_d:** Slip → 25%.
**common_trap:** Computing profit as % of *selling price* (12/52 ≈ 23%) instead of % of *cost* (12/40 = 30%).
**takeaway:** Always identify the *base* of a percent. "Profit % of cost" → base is cost.

## Q3
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Age Problems

Maria is currently 3 times as old as her daughter. If her daughter is 8 years old, how old will Maria be in 6 years?

- A) 24
- B) 27
- C) 30
- D) 32
- E) 36

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** Maria now = 24. +6 yrs → 30.
**explanation:** Maria's current age = 3 × 8 = 24. In 6 years: 24 + 6 = 30.
**mistake_a:** Bubbled Maria's current age (24) rather than her age in 6 years.
**mistake_b:** Added only 3 years instead of 6: 24 + 3 = 27. Possibly confused "6 years" with the daughter's current age divided by 2, or simply made an off-by-three error on the addition.
**mistake_d:** Added 8 (the daughter's current age) instead of 6 to Maria's current age: 24 + 8 = 32.
**mistake_e:** Computed Maria's future age by multiplying 3 by the daughter's future age (8 + 6 = 14)... no, 3×14 = 42; OR computed 3 × (8 + 4) = 36, adding 4 instead of 6 to the daughter's age before multiplying. The question says Maria is *currently* 3× the daughter's age — the 3× relationship does not persist in future years.
**common_trap:** Bubbling the current age (24) instead of the age in 6 years.
**takeaway:** Read carefully: "currently" vs "in N years." Answer the future age, not the current age.

## Q4
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Work Rate

A printer can print 240 pages in 8 minutes. At this rate, how many pages can it print in 15 minutes?

- A) 360
- B) 400
- C) 420
- D) 450
- E) 480

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** Rate = 30 ppm. 30 × 15 = 450.
**explanation:** Rate = 240/8 = 30 pages/min. In 15 min: 30 × 15 = 450.
**mistake_a:** Used 12 minutes instead of 15 for the second time: 30 × 12 = 360. This often occurs when a student picks up a number from the problem (the given 8-minute window and the new 15-minute window) and grabs 12 — perhaps confused by "15 − some number."
**mistake_b:** Misread the 8-minute denominator as 9 when computing the rate: 240/9 ≈ 26.7 ppm, then 26.7 × 15 ≈ 400. Or: set up the proportion 240/8 = x/15 but computed 240 × 15 = 3600 then divided by 9 instead of 8, giving 400.
**mistake_c:** Used 14 minutes instead of 15 (off-by-one on the target time): 30 × 14 = 420.
**mistake_e:** Used 16 minutes instead of 15 (off-by-one in the other direction): 30 × 16 = 480. Can also arise from computing the rate as 240/7.5 = 32 (misreading "8" as "7.5"), then 32 × 15 = 480.
**common_trap:** Computing rate then mis-multiplying — typical: 30 × 12 = 360 (grabbed 12 from context) or 240/9 = 26.7 × 15 ≈ 400 (misread the divisor).
**takeaway:** Two-step rate problems: rate = total/time, then output = rate × new time. Verify each step.

## Q5
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Rate/Time/Distance

Train A leaves Station X heading east at 70 mph. Two hours later, Train B leaves Station X heading east at 90 mph. How many hours after Train B departs will it overtake Train A?

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7
- D) 8
- E) 9

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** Head start = 140 mi. Closing rate = 20 mph. T = 140/20 = 7 hr.
**explanation:** When B departs, A has gone 70 × 2 = 140 mi. Closing rate = 90 − 70 = 20 mph. T = 140/20 = 7 hr.
**mistake_a:** Set up 90T = 70T + 140 correctly but dropped a term: 20T = 100 (arithmetic slip) → T = 5.
**mistake_b:** Computed head start as 70 × 2 = 140 miles correctly, then used closing rate of 20 mph correctly, but made an arithmetic error: 140/20 ≈ 6 (dropped 1 unit from the mental division).
**mistake_d:** Used B's absolute speed as the closing rate instead of the difference: 140/90 ≈ 1.56 hr, then added A's 2-hour head start: 1.56 + 6 ≈ 8. Combined two different errors.
**mistake_e:** Found the correct catch-up time (7 hours after B departs) but then added A's 2-hour head start: 7 + 2 = 9, answering "total travel time from when A departed" instead of "how many hours after B departs." Re-read: the question asks for time after B's departure, which is 7 hr.
**common_trap:** Using sum of speeds (160) instead of difference (20) for catch-up, or adding A's head-start time to the correct answer.
**takeaway:** Catch-up (same direction) → closing rate = *difference*. Approach (opposite) → *sum*. After computing T, check exactly what the question asked — "after B departs" excludes A's head start time.

**mistake_a:** Slip → 3.
**mistake_c:** Slip → 5.
**mistake_d:** Bubbled A's solo time (6).
**mistake_e:** Averaged times: (6+12)/2 = 9.
**common_trap:** Averaging solo times instead of adding rates.
**takeaway:** For combined work: rates add, not times. T = ab/(a+b).

## Q7
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Mixture

A chemist has 10 liters of a 30% acid solution. How many liters of pure acid must be added to create a 50% acid solution?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5
- E) 6

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** Acid = 3 L. (3+x)/(10+x) = 0.5 → x = 4.
**explanation:** Initial acid = 0.30 × 10 = 3 L. After adding x L pure acid: (3+x)/(10+x) = 0.5 → 3 + x = 5 + 0.5x → 0.5x = 2 → x = 4.
**mistake_a:** Added x only to the numerator, treating the total volume as fixed: (3+x)/10 = 0.5 → 3 + x = 5 → x = 2. The denominator must also grow by x because every liter of pure acid added increases both the acid content *and* the total solution volume.
**mistake_b:** Computed the required total acid (50% × 10 L = 5 L) minus existing acid (30% × 10 L = 3 L) = 2 L, then confused this with some other quantity or made an arithmetic slip to reach 3.
**mistake_d:** Computed "required total acid = 50% of original 10 L = 5 L" and reported 5 as the amount to add, without subtracting the 3 L of acid already present. The amount to *add* is (required) − (existing) = 5 − 3 = 2 L... but solving correctly with the growing denominator gives x = 4 L.
**mistake_e:** Arithmetic slip in the equation: 3 + x = 5 + 0.5x → 0.5x = 2 → x = 4. A student who writes 0.5x = 3 (carried the 3 to the wrong side) gets x = 6.
**common_trap:** Adding x to numerator only (forgetting denominator also grows). Every liter of pure acid added increases the total solution by 1 liter.
**takeaway:** When adding pure substance, both numerator and denominator gain x. Set new fraction = target.

## Q8
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Profit/Loss

A retailer marks up the cost of goods by 40% and then offers a seasonal discount of 10% on the marked price. What is the retailer's profit as a percentage of cost?

- A) 20%
- B) 24%
- C) 26%
- D) 30%
- E) 36%

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** 1.40 × 0.90 = 1.26 → 26% profit on cost.
**explanation:** Set cost = $100. Marked = 100 × 1.40 = $140. After 10% off: 140 × 0.90 = $126. Profit = $26 → 26%.
**mistake_a:** Treated the percentage changes as additive with additional subtractions: 40 − 10 − 10 = 20%. Each percentage change must be applied multiplicatively to the *current* amount, not added or subtracted as raw numbers.
**mistake_b:** Applied the 10% discount to cost rather than to the marked price: marked price = $140, discount = 10% of $100 (cost) = $10, sell price = $130. Profit = $30 = 30% of cost. Then divided that by 1.25 or made a subsequent arithmetic slip landing on 24%. The discount applies to the *marked price* ($140), not to the original cost.
**mistake_d:** Added and subtracted percentages directly: 40% markup − 10% discount = 30% net profit. This ignores that the 10% discount is applied to the *marked price* ($140), not to the cost ($100). 10% of $140 = $14, not $10.
**mistake_e:** Decimal placement error in the chained multiplication: 1.40 × 0.90 = 1.26 (correct), but student writes 1.36 — computing 14 × 9 = 126 correctly and then misplacing the decimal by one position to get 1.36. Profit = 36% of cost.
**common_trap:** Treating successive percent changes additively (40 − 10 = 30%) — but the 10% is taken off the *marked* price, not cost.
**takeaway:** Multipliers chain: net = (1 + markup)(1 − discount). Subtract 1 for net profit %.

## Q9
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Rate/Time/Distance

A cyclist travels from Town A to Town B at 15 mph and returns by the same route at 10 mph. What is the cyclist's average speed, in miles per hour, for the entire round trip?

- A) 11
- B) 12
- C) 12.5
- D) 13
- E) 13.5

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** Equal distances → harmonic mean: 2(15)(10)/(15+10) = 300/25 = 12.
**explanation:** Equal-distance round trip → harmonic mean: 2ab/(a+b) = 2·15·10/25 = 12 mph.
**mistake_a:** Arithmetic slip when applying the harmonic mean formula: correctly set up 2×15×10/(15+10) = 300/25 = 12, but miscomputed 15+10 = 28 instead of 25, giving 300/28 ≈ 10.7 ≈ 11.
**mistake_c:** Took arithmetic mean: (15+10)/2 = 12.5. Equal-distance trips weight slower legs more heavily (slower legs take more time), pulling the true average *below* the arithmetic mean.
**mistake_d:** Used a "speed-weighted" average: (15² + 10²)/(15+10) = (225+100)/25 = 325/25 = 13. This formula has no basis in the rate-distance relationship; it squares the speeds, overweighting the faster leg.
**mistake_e:** Averaged the arithmetic mean (12.5) and the correct harmonic mean (12), landing at (12.5+12)/2 = 12.25 ≈ 12.5... or alternatively split the difference between 12 and 15 by one-third: 12 + (15−12)/2 = 13.5. Neither is a principled calculation; this distractor catches rounding or "splitting the difference" behavior.
**common_trap:** Averaging the two speeds (12.5) — equal-distance trips weight slower legs more, pulling avg below the midpoint.
**takeaway:** Equal distance → harmonic mean. Equal time → arithmetic mean. Always check what's equal.

## Q10
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Work Rate

Pipe A can fill a tank in 4 hours, and Pipe B can fill the same tank in 6 hours. Pipe C can drain the full tank in 12 hours. If all three pipes are open simultaneously, how many hours will it take to fill the empty tank?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5
- E) 6

**answer:** B
**fastest_path:** Net rate = 1/4 + 1/6 − 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3 → T = 3.
**explanation:** Net rate = 1/4 + 1/6 − 1/12 = 3/12 + 2/12 − 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3 → T = 3 hr.
**mistake_a:** Treated Pipe C as another fill pipe instead of a drain: net rate = 1/4 + 1/6 + 1/12 = 3/12 + 2/12 + 1/12 = 6/12 = 1/2 → T = 2 hr. The problem says C can *drain* the tank — its rate is subtracted, not added.
**mistake_c:** Used only Pipe A's individual rate, ignoring both B and C: rate = 1/4 → T = 4 hr (A's solo fill time). Both B and C contribute to the net rate.
**mistake_d:** Arithmetic error when summing fractions: computed 1/4 + 1/6 − 1/12 = 3/12 + 2/12 − 1/12 = 4/12 but then inverted: T = 12/4 = 3, then divided by something to get 5. Or: computed 3/12+2/12 = 5/12 (omitted the subtraction), then T = 12/5 ≈ 2.4, misread as 5.
**mistake_e:** Forgot to subtract drain rate: 1/4 + 1/6 = 5/12 → T = 12/5 = 2.4, and rounded to 6 (perhaps expecting a whole number and rounding up awkwardly).
**common_trap:** Forgetting the *negative* sign on the drain rate (treats C as another fill pipe).
**takeaway:** Drains have *negative* rates. Net = Σ fill rates − Σ drain rates.

## Q11
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Mixture

A grocer mixes Brand A coffee at $12 per pound with Brand B coffee at $8 per pound to create a blend that sells at $9.50 per pound. If the grocer makes 40 pounds of the blend, how many pounds of Brand A coffee are used?

- A) 10
- B) 12
- C) 15
- D) 18
- E) 20

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** Alligation: $9.50 is 1.50 from $8 and 2.50 from $12. So A:B = 1.5:2.5 = 3:5. A = (3/8)·40 = 15.
**explanation:** Let a + b = 40 and 12a + 8b = 380. Substitute b = 40 − a: 12a + 320 − 8a = 380 → 4a = 60 → a = 15.
**mistake_a:** Treated the decimal blend price incorrectly as a whole number: used $9.50 × 40 = $360 (computing $9 × 40) rather than the correct $380. The blend equation then gives 12a + 320 − 8a = 360 → 4a = 40 → a = 10 pounds instead of 15.
**mistake_b:** Arithmetic error mid-computation, e.g., computing 8 × (40 − a) as 8 × 30 = 240 instead of 8 × 40 = 320, giving 12a + 240 − 8a = 380 → 4a = 140 → a = 35, then subtracting from 40 or making a subsequent sign error to reach 12.
**mistake_d:** Set up the blend equation with Brand B's numerator as the unknown rather than Brand A's: using alligation distances but applying B's distance to find A's quantity. With A:B = 1.5:2.5, student writes A = 2.5/4 × 40 = 25 then subtracts from 40 to get "B" = 15, but then confuses which label is which and reports Brand A = 40 − 22 = 18.
**mistake_e:** Assumed equal quantities of both brands (50/50 split): 40/2 = 20 pounds of each. This would only produce a blend priced at ($12 + $8)/2 = $10 per pound, not the target $9.50. The blend price $9.50 is closer to $8 than to $12, indicating Brand B (the cheaper one) must be the larger portion.
**common_trap:** Inverting the alligation ratio — assigning the distance from Brand A's price to the proportion of Brand A, rather than the proportion of Brand B.
**takeaway:** Alligation: distance ratio inverted = quantity ratio. Closer to A's price → less of A.

## Q12
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Age Problems

Five years ago, James was 4 times as old as his nephew. In 3 years, James will be twice as old as his nephew. How old is James now?

- A) 21
- B) 25
- C) 29
- D) 33
- E) 37

**answer:** A
**fastest_path:** J = 4N − 15; J = 2N + 3 → 2N = 18 → N = 9 → J = 21.
**explanation:** J − 5 = 4(N − 5) → J = 4N − 15. J + 3 = 2(N + 3) → J = 2N + 3. Equate: 2N = 18 → N = 9 → J = 21. Verify: 5 yrs ago 16 = 4·4 ✓; in 3 yrs 24 = 2·12 ✓.
**mistake_b:** Arithmetic error when solving 2N = 18: computed N = 10 instead of N = 9. Then J = 4(10) − 15 = 25. Double-check: 2N = 18 → N = 9, not 10.
**mistake_c:** Computed N = 11 (e.g., from 2N = 22 via an earlier sign error), then J = 4(11) − 15 = 29.
**mistake_d:** Computed N = 12, then J = 4(12) − 15 = 33. Often arises from writing J − 5 = 4N (omitting the nephew's time shift): J = 4N − 5, J = 2N + 3 → 2N = 8 → N = 4? No — if J = 4N − 5 and J = 2N + 3: 2N = 8 → N = 4 → J = 11. Not 33. More likely: pure arithmetic slip on 2N = 18.
**mistake_e:** Computed N = 13, then J = 4(13) − 15 = 37. All B–E arise from arithmetic errors in solving 2N = 18 rather than conceptual setup errors.
**common_trap:** Forgetting to apply the time shift to *both* people (e.g., writing J − 5 = 4N instead of 4(N − 5)). This shifts the system and produces a very different (wrong) answer.
**takeaway:** Time shifts apply to *every* person's age. "5 years ago" → both ages are 5 less.

## Q13
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Consecutive Integers

The sum of three consecutive integers is 72. What is the largest of the three integers?

- A) 22
- B) 23
- C) 24
- D) 25
- E) 26

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** Middle = 72/3 = 24. Largest = 24 + 1 = 25.
**explanation:** For 3 consecutive integers, middle = sum/count = 24. Integers: 23, 24, 25 → largest = 25.
**mistake_a:** Treated consecutive integers as consecutive *even* integers (step of 2): smallest = 72/3 − 2 = 22. But the problem says "consecutive integers" — they differ by 1. Integers: 23, 24, 25.
**mistake_b:** Bubbled the smallest integer (23) instead of the largest (25).
**mistake_c:** Bubbled the middle integer (24), which equals the mean. The middle is a tempting answer because 72/3 = 24 is the only explicit calculation needed — but the question asks for the *largest*, not the *middle*.
**mistake_e:** Treated consecutive integers as consecutive *even* integers: smallest = 22, middle = 24, largest = 26. Consecutive integers step by 1 (23, 24, 25), not by 2.
**common_trap:** Bubbling the middle (24, which equals the mean) instead of the largest.
**takeaway:** For consecutives: middle = mean. Largest = middle + (count−1)/2. Always confirm what's asked.

## Q14
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Averages

The average (arithmetic mean) of 5 numbers is 18. If a sixth number is added and the new average is 20, what is the sixth number?

- A) 22
- B) 24
- C) 28
- D) 30
- E) 32

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** New sum 6·20 = 120. Old sum 5·18 = 90. 6th = 30.
**explanation:** Old sum = 90; new sum = 120. 6th = 120 − 90 = 30. Shortcut: new value = new mean + n × Δmean = 20 + 5 × 2 = 30.
**mistake_a:** Applied only the mean difference: new value = new mean + Δmean = 20 + 2 = 22. This formula ignores the 5 original numbers that each need to "grow" by 2 to raise the mean; the added value must compensate for all of them.
**mistake_b:** Added the mean difference twice: 20 + 2 + 2 = 24. Partial application of the compensation logic.
**mistake_c:** Added n − 1 × Δmean instead of n × Δmean: 20 + 4 × 2 = 28 (used count 4 instead of 5).
**mistake_e:** Added n + 1 × Δmean: 20 + 6 × 2 = 32 (used the *new* count 6 rather than the original count 5).
**common_trap:** Computing mean differences (20 − 18 = 2) instead of sum differences. The 6th number must compensate for n × Δmean (5 × 2 = 10) extra units spread across all n original numbers, so new_value = new_mean + n × Δmean.
**takeaway:** Mean problems → work in *sums* (mean × count). Always.

## Q15
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Age Problems

Ten years ago, Sara was twice as old as her brother Tom. Today, the sum of their ages is 62. How old is Sara now?

- A) 32
- B) 36
- C) 38
- D) 40
- E) 42

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** S − 10 = 2(T − 10) → S = 2T − 10. With S + T = 62 → T = 24, S = 38.
**explanation:** S − 10 = 2(T − 10) → S = 2T − 10. Substitute into S + T = 62: 3T − 10 = 62 → T = 24 → S = 38.
**mistake_a:** Arithmetic error early in solving: after correctly reaching 3T = 72, divided by the wrong factor or made a subtraction error in the back-substitution. T = 24 gives S = 2(24) − 10 = 38. If T = 21 (three below), S = 32.
**mistake_b:** Off-by-one error on T: T = 23 → S = 2(23) − 10 = 36.
**mistake_d:** Off-by-one in the other direction: T = 25 → S = 2(25) − 10 = 40.
**mistake_e:** Off-by-two: T = 26 → S = 2(26) − 10 = 42. All four wrong answers trace back to computing T ≠ 24 from the linear step 3T = 72; the conceptual setup is correct but the arithmetic slips.
**common_trap:** Writing S − 10 = 2T (forgetting Tom's "−10" too), which gives S = 2T + 10, a different equation.
**takeaway:** "10 years ago" applies to *both* people. Translate: now-age − 10 for everyone.

## Q16
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Coin Problems

A piggy bank contains only nickels and dimes. If the bank contains 48 coins worth $3.60 in total, how many dimes are in the bank?

- A) 18
- B) 20
- C) 22
- D) 24
- E) 28

**answer:** D
**fastest_path:** n + d = 48; 5n + 10d = 360 (cents) → d = 24.
**explanation:** n + d = 48 and 5n + 10d = 360. Substitute n = 48 − d: 5(48 − d) + 10d = 360 → 240 + 5d = 360 → d = 24.
**mistake_a:** Divided total value by average coin value: $3.60 / $0.20 = 18, treating the "average" coin as worth 20¢ ((5¢ + 10¢)/2). This shortcut ignores that the 48-coin constraint is needed to distinguish d from n.
**mistake_b:** Worked in dollars and made a decimal slip: wrote the equation as 0.05n + 0.10d = 3.60 but then used 0.05(48) = 0.24 (instead of 2.40), giving 0.24 + 0.10d = 3.60 → d = 33.6 → 20 by rounding; or solved n + d = 48 and 0.05n + 0.10d = 36 (misplaced decimal on $3.60) → 5n + 10d = 3600 → n = 48 − d: 5(48 − d) + 10d = 3600 → 240 + 5d = 3600 → d = 672. Working in cents (5n + 10d = 360) avoids these decimal pitfalls entirely.
**mistake_c:** Made an off-by-two arithmetic error when solving: 5(48 − d) + 10d = 360 → 240 + 5d = 360 → 5d = 120 → d = 24. If the student instead writes 5(48 + d) + 10d = 360 (adding instead of subtracting): 240 + 5d + 10d = 360 → 15d = 120 → d = 8, then misreads to get 22.
**mistake_e:** Switched the variable — solved for n (nickels) instead of d (dimes): n = 48 − 24 = 24 → then misread to get 28. Or computed d correctly but set up n + d = 48 with d on the wrong side: n = 48 + 24 = 72, then bubbled "n among the choices" = 28.
**common_trap:** Mixing decimal dollars and integer coin counts. Always convert to cents: n + d = 48, 5n + 10d = 360.
**takeaway:** Coin problems: always work in cents (smallest unit). Avoids decimal slips.
**related_reading:** reading-quant-05-word-problems

## Q17
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Venn Diagrams

In a class of 80 students, 45 study Spanish, 38 study French, and 12 study neither language. How many students study both Spanish and French?

- A) 9
- B) 12
- C) 15
- D) 18
- E) 23

**answer:** C
**fastest_path:** At least one = 80 − 12 = 68. Both = 45 + 38 − 68 = 15.
**explanation:** Studying ≥1 language: 80 − 12 = 68. Inclusion-exclusion: 68 = 45 + 38 − |both| → |both| = 83 − 68 = 15.
**mistake_a:** Applied the formula without subtracting "neither" first: |Both| = 45 + 38 − 80 = 3, then incorrectly added the "neither" count: 3 + 6 = 9 (or some adjustment). The correct step is |at least one| = 80 − 12 = 68, then |Both| = 45 + 38 − 68 = 15.
**mistake_b:** Bubbled the "neither" count (12) — answered the given information rather than computing the "both" group.
