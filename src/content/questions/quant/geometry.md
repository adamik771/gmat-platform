## Q1
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Triangles

In a right triangle, the two legs have lengths 5 and 12. What is the length of the hypotenuse?

- A) 13
- B) 15
- C) 17
- D) 7
- E) √119

**answer:** A
**explanation:** In a right triangle, the lengths of the three sides are related by the Pythagorean theorem, which states that the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two legs. The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle and is the longest side.

Let a and b denote the lengths of the two legs and let c denote the length of the hypotenuse. We are given that a = 5 and b = 12, and we are asked to find c.

Applying the Pythagorean theorem:

c^2 = a^2 + b^2
c^2 = 5^2 + 12^2
c^2 = 25 + 144
c^2 = 169

Taking the positive square root, since a length must be positive:

c = sqrt(169) = 13

The correct answer is A.

## Q3
**difficulty:** Easy
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Quadrilaterals

The length of a rectangle is 3 times its width. If the perimeter is 48, what is the area?

- A) 36
- B) 72
- C) 108
- D) 144
- E) 192

**answer:** C
**explanation:** The area of a rectangle is the product of its length and width, and its perimeter is twice the sum of its length and width: P = 2(L + W). Because the two dimensions are given in a fixed ratio, we represent both in terms of a single variable.

Let w = the width of the rectangle. Since the length is 3 times the width, the length is 3w.

Translating the perimeter condition into an equation, we have

2(w + 3w) = 48.

Combining like terms inside the parentheses gives

2(4w) = 48,

so that

8w = 48.

Dividing both sides by 8 yields

w = 6.

The width is therefore 6, and the length is 3w = 3(6) = 18. The area is the product of the length and the width:

Area = L × W = 18 × 6 = 108.

The correct answer is C.

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6
- E) 7

**answer:** C

Let the two points be (x1, y1) = (1, 2) and (x2, y2) = (4, 6). The horizontal separation is x2 - x1 = 4 - 1 = 3, and the vertical separation is y2 - y1 = 6 - 2 = 4.

The distance d between the points is given by

d = sqrt((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2).

Substituting the values gives

d = sqrt((4 - 1)^2 + (6 - 2)^2)
  = sqrt(3^2 + 4^2)
  = sqrt(9 + 16)
  = sqrt(25)
  = 5.

The correct answer is C.

Let n denote the number of sides of the polygon. A hexagon has six sides, so n = 6. Substituting this value into the formula gives

(n − 2) × 180° = (6 − 2) × 180°.

We simplify the expression inside the parentheses:

6 − 2 = 4.

We then multiply:

4 × 180° = 720°.

Thus the interior angles of a hexagon sum to 720°.

The correct answer is C.

## Q10

Line L passes through the points (2, 3) and (6, 11). What is the slope of any line perpendicular to L?

- A) -2

- D) 2
- E) -4

**answer:** B
**explanation:** Two nonvertical lines are perpendicular if and only if the product of their slopes equals -1; equivalently, the slope of a line perpendicular to a given line is the negative reciprocal of that line's slope. The method is therefore to determine the slope of line L from the two given points and then take its negative reciprocal.

The slope of a line through two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) is defined as m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1). Let the two given points be (x1, y1) = (2, 3) and (x2, y2) = (6, 11).

Substituting these coordinates gives the slope of L:

m = (11 - 3)/(6 - 2) = 8/4 = 2.

Let m_perp denote the slope of any line perpendicular to L. By the perpendicularity condition, m * m_perp = -1, so

m_perp = -1/m = -1/2.

As a check, the product of the two slopes is 2 * (-1/2) = -1, which confirms that the lines are perpendicular.

The correct answer is B.

## Q15
**difficulty:** Medium
**type:** Data Sufficiency
**topic:** Polygons

How many sides does regular polygon P have?

(1) Each interior angle of P measures 150 degrees.
(2) The sum of the interior angles of P is 1800 degrees.

- A) Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) ALONE is not sufficient.
- B) Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) ALONE is not sufficient.
- C) BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER statement ALONE is sufficient.
- D) EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- E) Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

**answer:** D
**explanation:** This question asks for the number of sides of a regular polygon P, and it is sufficient to determine that number exactly. Two standard relationships govern a regular polygon with n sides: the exterior angles sum to 360 degrees, so each exterior angle measures 360/n degrees and each interior angle measures 180 minus 360/n degrees; and the interior angles sum to (n minus 2) times 180 degrees. We evaluate each statement against these relationships.

Statement (1) gives that each interior angle measures 150 degrees. Since an interior angle and its adjacent exterior angle are supplementary, each exterior angle measures 180 minus 150, which is 30 degrees. Because the exterior angles of any polygon sum to 360 degrees, we have n times 30 equals 360, so n equals 360 divided by 30, which is 12. The number of sides is determined uniquely. Statement (1) alone is sufficient.

Statement (2) gives that the sum of the interior angles is 1800 degrees. Using (n minus 2) times 180 equals 1800, we divide both sides by 180 to obtain n minus 2 equals 10, so n equals 12. The number of sides is again determined uniquely. Statement (2) alone is sufficient.

Because each statement alone determines that P has 12 sides, each statement alone is sufficient.

The correct answer is D.

y = -2x + 4.

## Q19
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Data Sufficiency
**topic:** Triangles

In triangle ABC, is angle A a right angle?

(1) The side lengths satisfy AB² + AC² = BC².
(2) AB = 3 and AC = 4.

- A) Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) ALONE is not sufficient.
- B) Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) ALONE is not sufficient.
- C) BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER statement ALONE is sufficient.
- D) EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- E) Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

**answer:** A
**explanation:** This is a data sufficiency question, so the task is to determine, for each statement, whether the information given settles the question "Is angle A a right angle?" with a definite yes or a definite no. The governing principle is the Pythagorean theorem together with its converse: in a triangle with sides a, b, and c, the angle opposite side c is a right angle if and only if a² + b² = c². In triangle ABC, the angle at vertex A is opposite side BC, while sides AB and AC are the two sides adjacent to A.

Statement (1): The side lengths satisfy AB² + AC² = BC². By the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, the equality of the sum of the squares of two sides with the square of the third side forces the angle opposite that third side to be a right angle. Here the two squared sides are AB and AC, and the squared third side is BC, so the angle opposite BC, which is angle A, must measure 90 degrees. This holds for every triangle whose sides obey the given equation, so the answer to the question is a definite yes. Statement (1) is sufficient.

Statement (2): AB = 3 and AC = 4. This fixes the two sides adjacent to angle A but provides no information about BC or about the measure of angle A itself. Let BC = x. The triangle inequality permits any x with 1 < x < 7, and the angle at A varies continuously with x. If x = 5, then AB² + AC² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5² = BC², so angle A is 90 degrees and the answer is yes. If instead x = 6, then AB² + AC² = 9 + 16 = 25, while BC² = 36, and since 25 is not equal to 36, angle A is not 90 degrees and the answer is no. Because both a yes case and a no case are possible, statement (2) does not settle the question. Statement (2) is not sufficient.

Since statement (1) alone is sufficient and statement (2) alone is not sufficient, the credited response is the choice indicating that statement (1) alone suffices but statement (2) alone does not.

The correct answer is A.

## Q20
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Data Sufficiency
**topic:** Quadrilaterals

What is the area of rectangle ABCD?

(1) The diagonal AC has length 10.
(2) The perimeter of the rectangle is 28.

- A) Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) ALONE is not sufficient.
- B) Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) ALONE is not sufficient.
- C) BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER statement ALONE is sufficient.
- D) EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- E) Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

**answer:** C
**explanation:** The governing method is to recognize that the area of a rectangle is the product of its two side lengths, and that this product can be obtained from a sum and a sum of squares by means of the algebraic identity (L + W)^2 = L^2 + 2LW + W^2. We let L and W denote the length and width of rectangle ABCD, so the area sought is LW. A single linear or quadratic equation in the two unknowns L and W does not by itself determine the value of LW.

Statement (1) states that diagonal AC has length 10. By the Pythagorean theorem applied to the right triangle formed by two adjacent sides and the diagonal, L^2 + W^2 = 10^2 = 100. This is one equation in two unknowns, and infinitely many pairs (L, W) satisfy it while producing different products. For instance, L^2 + W^2 = 100 is consistent with both (6, 8), giving LW = 48, and with (sqrt 50, sqrt 50), giving LW = 50. Statement (1) alone is not sufficient.

Statement (2) states that the perimeter of the rectangle is 28. Since the perimeter equals 2(L + W), we have 2(L + W) = 28, hence L + W = 14. This too is one equation in two unknowns, satisfied by many pairs with different products; for example, (4, 10) gives LW = 40 while (7, 7) gives LW = 49. Statement (2) alone is not sufficient.

Taking the two statements together, we have both L^2 + W^2 = 100 and L + W = 14. Squaring the second equation gives (L + W)^2 = 14^2 = 196. Expanding the left side yields L^2 + 2LW + W^2 = 196. Substituting L^2 + W^2 = 100 produces 100 + 2LW = 196, so 2LW = 96 and therefore LW = 48. The area is uniquely determined, so both statements together are sufficient, although neither alone is sufficient.

The correct answer is C.

In the xy-plane, line ℓ passes through the point (2, 5) and is perpendicular to the line 3x + 4y = 20. What is the y-intercept of line ℓ?

**answer:** A
**explanation:** The slope of a line written in the form ax + by = c can be found by solving for y, and two nonvertical lines are perpendicular exactly when the slope of one is the negative reciprocal of the slope of the other. Once a slope and a point on a line are known, the point-slope form y - y0 = m(x - x0) determines the line, and converting to slope-intercept form y = mx + b reveals the y-intercept b.

First, rewrite the given line 3x + 4y = 20 in slope-intercept form. Subtracting 3x from both sides gives 4y = -3x + 20, and dividing by 4 gives y = -(3/4)x + 5. The slope of the given line is therefore -3/4.

A rectangle has two sides on the legs of a right triangle with legs of length 6 and 8 (right angle at the origin), and one vertex on the hypotenuse. What is the maximum possible area of the rectangle?

- A) 10
- B) 12
- C) 16
- D) 20
- E) 24

**answer:** B
**explanation:** The area of a rectangle is the product of its two adjacent side lengths, so the task is to maximize this product subject to the constraint that one vertex lies on the hypotenuse of the triangle. The standard method is to express one dimension in terms of the other using the equation of the hypotenuse, write the area as a function of a single variable, and then locate the maximum of that function.

Place the right angle at the origin with the legs along the coordinate axes, the leg of length 8 along the x-axis and the leg of length 6 along the y-axis. First cube: 6s² = 96, so s² = 16 and s = 4. Its volume is 4³ = 64. The second cube has edges twice as long (k = 2), so its volume is 2³ = 8 times larger: 8 × 64 = 512. (Equivalently, the second edge is 8, and 8³ = 512.)

The correct answer is D.
**fastest_path:** Get s = 4 from 6s² = 96. Doubling the edge multiplies volume by 2³ = 8, so the answer is 8 × 4³ = 8 × 64 = 512 — or just cube the new edge directly, 8³ = 512.
**common_trap:** Scaling the volume by the linear factor 2 (or the area factor 4) instead of the volume factor 2³ = 8.
**mistake_a:** 64 is the volume of the first cube (4³). It answers for the wrong cube — the scaling step was not applied.
**mistake_b:** 128 multiplies the first cube's volume by the linear factor 2 (64 × 2). Doubling the edge does far more than double the volume.
**mistake_c:** 256 multiplies by the area factor 4 (64 × 4), as if volume scaled like area. Volume scales by the cube of the linear factor, not the square.
**mistake_e:** 4096 mistakes s² = 16 for s = 16, then cubes the doubled edge of 32 — or computes 16³. The error is reading the edge as 16 instead of taking the square root to get 4.
**takeaway:** Scaling lengths by k scales areas by k² and volumes by k³. Doubling a cube's edge multiplies its volume by 8. Also: surface area gives s², so take the square root to get the edge. (Error-log tag: Conceptual.)
**hint_nudge:** Doubling every edge does more than double the volume — by what factor does volume grow?
**hint_strategy:** Find the first edge from 6s² = 96, then scale volume by 2³ when the edge doubles.
**hint_setup:** 6s² = 96 gives s = 4; new volume = 2³ × 4³.

## Q50
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Triangle Inequality
**skill:** triangle-inequality-with-parity-constraint
**trap_type:** ignored-secondary-constraint
**est_time_seconds:** 150
**prerequisite:** reading-quant-07-geometry-and-coordinate-reasoning

Two sides of a triangle have lengths 7 and 11. The third side has an integer length, and the perimeter of the triangle is an even integer. How many different values are possible for the perimeter?

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7
- D) 12
- E) 13

**answer:** B
**explanation:** This is a triangle-inequality counting problem with an extra parity filter layered on top. The triangle inequality fixes the range of the third side; the "even perimeter" condition then removes half of those values.

Let the third side be x. The triangle inequality requires x to lie strictly between the difference and the sum of the other two sides: 11 − 7 < x < 11 + 7, i.e., 4 < x < 18. Since x is an integer, x ranges over 5, 6, 7, …, 17 — that is 13 possible values.

Now apply the parity condition. The perimeter is 7 + 11 + x = 18 + x. Because 18 is even, 18 + x is even exactly when x is even. The even integers in 5 ≤ x ≤ 17 are 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16 — that is 6 values. Each gives a distinct perimeter, so there are 6 possible perimeters.

The correct answer is B.
**fastest_path:** Range 4 < x < 18 gives x = 5…17. Even perimeter ⇔ x even (since 18 is even), and the even values 6,8,…,16 number (16 − 6)/2 + 1 = 6. Count the constrained values directly rather than listing all perimeters.
**common_trap:** Counting all 13 integer values of x and forgetting that the even-perimeter condition keeps only the even ones.
**mistake_a:** 5 undercounts the even values — typically an off-by-one when counting 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, or mistakenly excluding an endpoint.
**mistake_c:** 7 counts the odd values of x instead of the even ones (5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17 is 7 values). The parity that makes the perimeter even is x even, since 18 is even — the parity was flipped.
**mistake_d:** 12 comes from a boundary error in the triangle inequality, such as using 4 ≤ x ≤ 16 or otherwise miscounting the range before applying parity, or halving 13 and rounding.
**mistake_e:** 13 is the count of all integer values of x satisfying only the triangle inequality. The even-perimeter constraint was ignored entirely — the headline trap.
**takeaway:** Read for every constraint. Here the triangle inequality sets the range and a parity condition halves it; missing the second constraint doubles your count. Check the parity of the fixed part (18) to know whether x must be even or odd. (Error-log tag: Misread.)
**hint_nudge:** First bound the third side with the triangle inequality, then ask which of those values make the perimeter even.
**hint_strategy:** Find the integer range for x from 4 < x < 18, then keep only the x that make 18 + x even.
**hint_setup:** x ∈ {5, …, 17}; even perimeter needs x even.

## Q51
**difficulty:** Hard
**type:** Problem Solving
**topic:** Inscribed and Circumscribed Figures
**skill:** chaining-inscribed-square-circle-square
**trap_type:** linear-ratio-reported-for-area
**est_time_seconds:** 165
**prerequisite:** reading-quant-07-geometry-and-coordinate-reasoning

A circle is inscribed in a square, and a second, smaller square is inscribed in that circle. What is the ratio of the area of the larger square to the area of the smaller square?

- A) √2 to 1
- B) 2 to 1
- C) 2√2 to 1
- D) 4 to 1
- E) 1 to 2

**answer:** B
**explanation:** Nothing in this problem requires actual numbers — the figures are linked through the shared circle, and choosing a convenient value for one length makes the relationships transparent. The two key bridges are: (1) a circle inscribed in a square touches all four sides, so the square's side equals the circle's diameter; and (2) a square inscribed in a circle has its corners on the circle, so the square's diagonal equals the circle's diameter.

Let the larger square have side s. The inscribed circle then has diameter s. The smaller square is inscribed in that circle, so its diagonal equals the circle's diameter, s. A square with diagonal d has side d/√2, so the smaller square has side s/√2.

Now compare areas:
- Larger square area = s².
- Smaller square area = (s/√2)² = s²/2.

The ratio is s² : s²/2 = 1 : (1/2) = 2 : 1.

The correct answer is B.
**fastest_path:** Let the big square's side be √2 (so the circle's diameter is √2 and the small square's diagonal is √2, making its side 1). Areas are 2 and 1 — ratio 2 to 1. Picking the side as √2 clears the radical instantly.
**common_trap:** Comparing the side lengths (whose ratio is √2 to 1) and reporting that instead of the area ratio, which is the square of the linear ratio.
**mistake_a:** √2 to 1 is the ratio of the side lengths, not the areas. Since area scales as the square of length, the area ratio is (√2)² = 2 to 1. This is the dominant trap.
**mistake_c:** 2√2 to 1 mixes a linear and an area factor — for example, multiplying the side ratio √2 by the area ratio 2. It double-counts the scaling.
**mistake_d:** 4 to 1 squares the wrong linear ratio, treating the side ratio as 2 to 1 (perhaps confusing the diagonal/side relationship) and then squaring to 4. The side ratio is √2, not 2.
**mistake_e:** 1 to 2 inverts the comparison, giving smaller-to-larger when the question asks larger-to-smaller.
**takeaway:** When figures are nested through a shared circle, translate via "inscribed square's diagonal = diameter = circumscribing square's side," and remember that an area ratio is the square of the corresponding length ratio. Assigning a convenient length avoids algebra. (Error-log tag: Conceptual.)
**hint_nudge:** The circle's diameter equals the big square's side and also the small square's diagonal — that single length links both squares.
**hint_strategy:** Let the big square's side be s. Find the small square's side from its diagonal (= s), then compare areas, not sides.
**hint_setup:** Big side s, small square diagonal s so small side s/√2; compare s² to (s/√2)².
**related_reading:** reading-quant-07-geometry-and-coordinate-reasoning
