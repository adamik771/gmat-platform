# Chapter 3.6 — Two-Part Analysis

## Core idea

Two-Part Analysis (TPA) asks you to *fill in two cells* of a small table from a list of options, where the two cells have a *constraint relationship* — they must satisfy a condition jointly, not independently. The format looks like a single question but is actually two intertwined sub-questions. The skill is *recognizing the constraint that links the two cells* and using it to narrow the candidate combinations before computing.

The mindset shift in this chapter: *the two cells are linked; treat them as a system, not as two separate problems*. Most TPA mistakes come from treating each cell independently and missing the joint constraint.

## Why it matters

Three reasons TPA deserves its own chapter.

*The format is unique to GMAT*. Students arrive at TPA with no analog from school or other tests. The skill is entirely new and entirely learnable. Targeted prep produces large gains.

*The constraint-recognition skill is high-leverage*. Once you've named the constraint, the answer often falls out from logic alone — no computation needed. Students who spot the constraint quickly answer in 60-90 seconds; students who don't grind through computation for 3+ minutes.

*TPA tests transferability of CR-style reading*. Two-Part questions often present a scenario and ask you to identify two roles, two values, or two outcomes that satisfy the scenario's logic. The reading discipline from CR (Chapter 2.3) — pre-thinking what the answer requires — applies directly.

## Mental model

Picture every TPA problem as a *small system of two equations*. The scenario gives you constraints; the two cells are the unknowns; the answer-option columns are the candidate values for each unknown. Your job is to *find the pair of values that jointly satisfies the constraints*.

The system framing carries three reminders. First, *identify the constraints from the scenario*. Second, *the two cells are linked* — picking a value for cell A may rule out values for cell B. Third, *eliminate from the pair side, not from each cell separately*. A value for cell A that satisfies the constraint with one cell-B value may not satisfy with another.

## GMAT recognition signals

Five recurring TPA constraint types.

*Sum or total constraint.* "X and Y together must equal Z." Cell A and cell B values must add (or multiply, or otherwise combine) to a specified total.

*Difference or comparison constraint.* "X must be twice Y," or "X must exceed Y by at least Z." Cell A's value is a function of cell B's, or they must satisfy a comparison.

*Categorical constraint.* "Cell A and cell B must come from different categories." E.g., "the first item is from group X, the second from group Y."

*Sequence or order constraint.* "Cell A comes before cell B in the sequence." Time-based, position-based, or causal ordering.

*Maximum or minimum constraint.* "Cell A is the largest value satisfying X; cell B is the smallest." Selection from a set under joint criteria.

## Method

A four-step Two-Part Analysis protocol.

*Step 1. Read the scenario and the two cell prompts*. Identify what each cell is asking for. Cell A asks for X; cell B asks for Y. Are X and Y the same kind of thing (e.g., both prices) or different (one price, one quantity)?

*Step 2. Identify the constraint that links the two cells.* What relationship must they satisfy jointly? This is the most important step. Without naming the constraint, you can't eliminate.

*Step 3. Test candidate pairs against the constraint.* Run through the answer options. For each potential pair, ask: does this pair satisfy the constraint? Often the constraint eliminates most pairs immediately.

*Step 4. Verify the surviving pair against any additional conditions.* If multiple pairs survive Step 3, look for additional constraints in the scenario that distinguish them.

## Common traps

Six recurring TPA traps.

*Treating the two cells as independent.* Picking the "best" value for cell A separately from cell B, then trying to make cell B work. Misses the joint constraint. Fix: identify the constraint first, test pairs second.

*Missing the constraint entirely.* Reading the scenario without identifying the linking condition. Symptom: you arrive at multiple "valid" pairs because you didn't apply the constraint to filter. Fix: re-read the scenario explicitly looking for the linking condition.

*Eliminating from cell A or cell B separately.* "Value 1 in cell A is too low" — but value 1 in cell A might be exactly right paired with the right value in cell B. Fix: eliminate pairs, not values.

*Computing every pair.* Working through all combinations arithmetically when constraint-based logic would eliminate most pairs without computation. Fix: scan for logical constraints first; compute only on the survivors.

*Confusing the two cells' columns.* Filling in cell A's value in cell B's column or vice versa. Costly. Fix: re-read the column headers before bubbling.

*Overlooking the implicit "and."* The scenario may state the constraint as "X and Y" but the right pair must satisfy *both* X and Y, not either-or. Fix: read constraints carefully.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating constraint-mapping on a TPA problem.

*Scenario.* A small company is hiring two new employees. Their combined annual salaries cannot exceed $150,000. The first employee will be a senior engineer; the second will be a junior engineer. Senior engineers cost at least $90,000; junior engineers cost between $50,000 and $80,000.

*Cell A (Senior Engineer Salary):* Options $80K, $90K, $100K, $110K, $120K, $130K.
*Cell B (Junior Engineer Salary):* Same options ($80K, $90K, $100K, $110K, $120K, $130K).

*Question.* "Select one salary for each role such that the constraints are satisfied and the total annual cost is maximized."

*Step 1 — Read.* Cell A is senior engineer salary. Cell B is junior engineer salary. Constraints: senior ≥ $90K, junior between $50K and $80K (so from the options, junior must be $80K — only option in range), combined ≤ $150K. Goal: maximize the combined.

*Step 2 — Identify constraints.*
1. Senior ≥ $90K (cell A choices: $90K, $100K, $110K, $120K, $130K).
2. Junior between $50K and $80K (cell B choice: only $80K).
3. Combined ≤ $150K (constraint on the pair).
4. Maximize combined.

*Step 3 — Test pairs.* With cell B forced to $80K, cell A must satisfy $80K + cell A ≤ $150K, so cell A ≤ $70K. But cell A also must be ≥ $90K. Contradiction.

Wait — that means there's no valid pair. Let me re-check the constraints. Combined ≤ $150K. If junior is $80K, senior ≤ $70K. But senior ≥ $90K. No valid pair exists.

*Recheck.* The example is poorly constructed (or I've set up an unsolvable problem). In the real format, the constraints would be feasible. Let me adjust: combined ≤ $200K.

With combined ≤ $200K and junior = $80K, senior ≤ $120K. With senior ≥ $90K, senior ∈ {$90K, $100K, $110K, $120K}. To maximize combined, pick senior = $120K. Combined = $200K.

*Answer.* Cell A = $120K, Cell B = $80K.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap is treating the cells independently. A student who picks senior = $130K (highest option satisfying senior ≥ $90K) without checking the combined-cap constraint produces an invalid pair. A student who picks junior = $90K (highest option) misses the cap on junior salary. The discipline of pair-testing — checking that the two cells *jointly satisfy all constraints* — prevents these errors.

The other trap is computing every pair. With six options for each cell, there are 36 pairs. Most are ruled out by the simple junior=$80K-only constraint. Logical elimination dropped the work to four pairs to consider; computing every pair would have wasted time.

*Time spent.* About 2 minutes — most of it in identifying the constraints and recognizing that junior is forced to $80K.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Sum constraint

*Setup.* A travel budget is $400. Cell A asks for the highest-priced flight option that fits the budget; Cell B asks for the highest-priced hotel option that fits, given that the chosen flight has been booked. Flight options: $150, $200, $250, $300, $350. Hotel options: $80, $120, $160, $200, $240. Constraint: A + B ≤ $400.

*Thinking process.* If we pick the highest flight ($350), only $50 remains for the hotel — none of the hotel options fit. If we pick $300 flight, $100 remains — only $80 hotel fits. If we pick $250 flight, $150 remains — $80 or $120 hotel fits. Maximize: pick $250 flight + $120 hotel = $370.

*Solution.* Cell A = $250, Cell B = $120.

*Common mistake.* Picking the highest flight ($350) and then trying to find a hotel that fits — and concluding "no valid pair." The constraint forces a *joint* optimization, not independent maximization.

*Takeaway.* In sum-constrained TPA, each cell can't be maximized independently. The pair must jointly satisfy the constraint, and the optimal pair often isn't (highest, highest).

### Example 2 — Sequence constraint

*Setup.* From a list of months {Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun}, pick two months such that revenue in the second month is exactly twice revenue in the first. Revenue (in $K): Jan=10, Feb=20, Mar=15, Apr=30, May=25, Jun=60.

*Thinking process.* Test pairs: Jan(10) → Feb(20)? Yes. Jan(10) → Mar(15)? No. Jan(10) → Apr(30)? No (30 ≠ 20). Feb(20) → Apr(30)? No. Mar(15) → Apr(30)? Yes. Apr(30) → Jun(60)? Yes.

*Solution.* Multiple valid pairs: (Jan, Feb), (Mar, Apr), (Apr, Jun). The question may specify additional constraints to narrow further.

*Common mistake.* Picking the first pair found and assuming it's unique. Sequence constraints can have multiple solutions; check all candidates.

*Takeaway.* TPA constraint-satisfaction may produce multiple pairs. Read the question stem for additional disambiguating conditions before locking in.

### Example 3 — Categorical constraint

*Setup.* Pick two products from a list: 4 must be from "premium" category, 4 from "budget." Constraint: combined cost ≤ $50, and the two products must be from *different* categories. Premium: $30, $35, $40, $45. Budget: $15, $20, $25, $30.

*Thinking process.* Test combinations. (Premium 30, Budget 15): $45, valid. (Premium 30, Budget 20): $50, valid. (Premium 35, Budget 15): $50, valid. Higher combinations exceed $50.

*Solution.* Several pairs satisfy the constraint. The maximum-cost pair is either ($30, $20) or ($35, $15), both at $50.

*Common mistake.* Picking two from the same category — violates the categorical constraint. Or picking the cheapest from each — loses optimization opportunities.

*Takeaway.* TPA cells with categorical constraints require explicit cross-category pairing. Track the constraint actively as you test pairs.
