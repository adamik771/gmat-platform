# Chapter 3.1 — The DI Mindset

## Core idea

Data Insights is the section *most students underestimate and most students underprepare for*. On the surface, it looks like a hybrid of Quant and Verbal — some math, some reading, some tables, some charts. Underneath, it tests a *distinct skill set*: rapid information triage, the ability to navigate multi-tab data quickly, the discipline to stop reading once you've located what you need, and the math fluency to handle calculations on data you didn't expect to see. None of the other sections train these skills as a single bundle. DI does.

The mindset shift in this chapter: *don't read everything; triage*. DI passages, especially Multi-Source Reasoning, deliberately give you more information than you can process in two minutes. The students who try to read everything fail. The students who locate the question's information requirement and read only what's needed succeed. Triage is the skill.

*DI scores show the largest variance from preparation*. Most students arrive at GMAT prep reasonably comfortable with Quant and reasonably comfortable with Verbal. Few students arrive comfortable with DI's specific skill set, because nothing in school or college trains it. As a result, DI prep produces the largest score gains per hour invested — but only if the prep is targeted at DI's actual skills, not at general Quant or Verbal ability.

*The DI section is roughly 33% of the total score*. Three sections, weighted roughly equally. Underperformance on DI is a one-third drag on your composite score. Yet most students spend less than one-third of their prep time on DI.

*DI rewards habits from Quant and Verbal but punishes their bad habits*. The Quant habit of "compute carefully" fails on DI when computation is unnecessary. The Verbal habit of "read every word" fails on DI when ninety percent of the text is irrelevant to the specific question. Strong DI solvers transfer the *good* habits of each section (method selection from Quant; structural reading from Verbal) and discard the bad ones.

## Mental model

Picture every DI problem as an *emergency-room triage scenario*. A patient arrives. You don't have time to run every possible test; you have to identify the specific symptom that matters and act. Same for DI: the data set is more than you can process; the question identifies the specific information you need; your job is to *find it and use it, ignoring everything else*.

The triage analogy carries three reminders. First, *read the question first*. The question tells you what to look for. Without it, you don't know which data to triage in. Second, *don't process information you don't need*. Resist the urge to "make sense of the whole thing." Third, *commit to your answer*. Triage doctors don't second-guess; they act on the diagnosis. So should you.

## GMAT recognition signals

DI has five question formats, each with its own subtype. Recognize them fast.

*Data Sufficiency.* A traditional Quant-style question with two statements (1) and (2). The task: determine whether the statements alone or together are sufficient to answer the question. Recognition: explicit "(1)" and "(2)" labels and the standard five answer choices about statement sufficiency.

*Table Analysis.* A sortable table with multiple columns. Three or four true/false statements about the data. Recognition: a tabular display with column headers and the ability to sort.

*Graphics Interpretation.* A chart or graph with two or more drop-down questions. Recognition: a visualization (line, bar, scatter, etc.) and pull-down completion-style answers.

*Multi-Source Reasoning.* Multiple tabs of information (text, tables, charts) and a sequence of questions about the combined data. Recognition: a tabbed interface with the question targeting cross-source synthesis.

*Two-Part Analysis.* A scenario with a question asking you to fill in two cells from a list of options, where the cells often have a constraint relationship. Recognition: a two-column answer table where you select one option per column.

Each format rewards different skills. The mindset chapter introduces the meta-skill (triage); the type-specific chapters (3.2 to 3.7) develop the formats individually.

## Method

A four-step protocol for every DI problem.

*Step 1. Read the question first*. Always. The question tells you what to look for; without it, you'll waste time processing data that doesn't matter.

*Step 2. Identify which source / column / tab the question targets*. For Multi-Source Reasoning, this is the most important triage step — the question often specifies a tab implicitly through its vocabulary. For Table Analysis, identify which columns matter. For Graphics, identify which axis or category. For DS, identify the implicit question (yes/no, value, range).

*Step 3. Process only the relevant information*. Read the targeted source carefully. Ignore everything else. If a piece of information looks like it might matter but isn't directly addressed, leave it for now — return only if your first pass doesn't suffice.

*Step 4. Commit and move on*. DI is timed tightly; spending too long on one question costs the next two. Once you've made a defensible decision, commit. Don't second-guess.

## Common traps

Six recurring DI traps.

*Reading everything.* The instinct to "make sense of the whole thing" before answering. On Multi-Source Reasoning, this is fatal — you'll spend three minutes orienting and have ninety seconds for the actual question. Fix: question first, source-targeted reading second.

*Computing when computation isn't needed.* Many DI questions are answered by reading the data, not computing on it. "Which row has the highest value in column X" is a sort-and-look, not an arithmetic problem. Fix: ask "do I actually need to compute, or just to find?"

*Confusing units and denominators across sources.* The chart shows "millions of units"; the table shows "thousands of units"; the question asks about "total units." Mixing the two produces wrong answers by a factor of 1000. Fix: explicitly note the units of every figure you use.

*Mistaking correlation for causation in tabular data.* Tables show data; they don't show why the data is what it is. An answer choice that claims "X causes Y" because the table shows X and Y together is over-inferring. Fix: stick to what the data shows, not why.

*Over-reading the chart.* Reading exact values off a graph that only shows approximate values. The y-axis ticks are at 0, 50, 100, 150 and the bar is between 75 and 100; "the value is 87" is over-precise. Fix: state values to the precision the chart actually supports.

*Spending too long on one question*. DI has hard pacing. If a Multi-Source Reasoning question is taking three minutes, the next two questions in the set will suffer. Fix: have a walk-away threshold (around 2:30 for hard problems) and respect it.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating the triage protocol on a Multi-Source-style question.

*Hypothetical data set.* Three tabs.

*Tab 1 (Description).* Text: "ABC Manufacturing operates three plants — Plant 1, Plant 2, Plant 3. The company produces two products — Product A and Product B. The company is evaluating a proposal to consolidate operations."

*Tab 2 (Production Volumes by Plant).* Table: Plant, Product A units, Product B units, Total units. Plant 1: 5000 / 3000 / 8000. Plant 2: 7000 / 5000 / 12000. Plant 3: 4000 / 6000 / 10000.

*Tab 3 (Cost per Unit by Plant).* Table: Plant, Cost per Product A unit, Cost per Product B unit. Plant 1: $12 / $15. Plant 2: $10 / $14. Plant 3: $11 / $13.

*Question.* "If the company decided to consolidate Product A production at the lowest-cost-per-unit plant for Product A, what is the total cost of producing all of the company's Product A units at that plant?"

*Step 1 — Read the question first*. The question asks for the total cost of consolidating all Product A production at the cheapest Product-A plant. Two pieces of data are needed: (1) the cheapest-Product-A plant, and (2) the total Product A units across all plants.

*Step 2 — Identify the relevant sources.* Tab 3 (Cost per Unit by Plant) gives the cost-per-Product-A figures. Tab 2 (Production Volumes by Plant) gives the units-by-product breakdown. Tab 1 (Description) is background, not data — skip.

*Step 3 — Process only the relevant information.* From Tab 3, Plant 2 has the lowest Product A cost at $10/unit. From Tab 2, total Product A units across plants: 5000 + 7000 + 4000 = 16,000. Total cost: 16,000 × $10 = $160,000.

*Step 4 — Commit and move on.* Answer: $160,000.

*Time spent.* About 90 seconds total — most of it in reading the question carefully and identifying the two relevant data points. Notice: I never read or processed the Product B data. I never read the description text beyond the first orientation. The triage cut the work in half.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap on this kind of problem is to "process the whole data set" — to spend two minutes reading every cell, every cost, every total — before turning to the question. By the time you've done that, you have ninety seconds for the actual question and you've already used your bandwidth. Question-first triage saves the bandwidth.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Question-first triage on MSR

*Setup.* An MSR set has three tabs: company description, monthly revenue table, regional sales chart. First sub-question: "What was the company's total revenue in Q3?"

*Thinking process.* "Total revenue in Q3" → revenue table is the target tab. Description and chart aren't needed for this sub-question.

*Solution.* Open Tab 2 (revenue table), find Q3 totals, sum or read off.

*Common mistake.* Reading all three tabs in sequence before turning to the question. Costs 60+ seconds of orientation that doesn't help with this specific sub-question.

*Takeaway.* The question's vocabulary points to specific data. Process only what the question demands; ignore the rest.

### Example 2 — Compute when computation is necessary, find when it isn't

*Setup.* Table Analysis statement: "The company with the highest revenue in 2024 has more than 1,000 employees."

*Thinking process.* This is a *find* operation, not a compute operation. Sort by revenue, look at the top row, check the employee count. No arithmetic required.

*Solution.* Sort and read.

*Common mistake.* Treating it as a compute problem and trying to calculate something. The statement is binary; verify the row directly.

*Takeaway.* Many DI statements ask whether a *specific row's value* meets a condition. Sort, read, verify — not compute.

### Example 3 — Walk-away threshold

*Setup.* You've spent 2 minutes 30 seconds on an MSR sub-question. You're not yet sure how to combine the data across tabs. Time remaining in section: 18 minutes for 11 more questions.

*Thinking process.* Average remaining time per question: 18/11 ≈ 1:38. You're already over budget on this one. Mark a guess and move on.

*Solution.* Walk away. The lost time is already lost; protect the next 11 questions.

*Common mistake.* Continuing to grind because "I'm so close." Sunk-cost fallacy. The cost of finishing this question (another 90 seconds, say) trades against the cost of rushing the next 11 (worse downstream accuracy).

*Takeaway.* The walk-away threshold is non-negotiable. Section pacing protects total score; one question's correctness is a small fraction of section value.
