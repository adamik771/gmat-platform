# Chapter 3.8 — DI Timing and Workflow

## Core idea

DI has the *tightest pacing pressure of the three sections* relative to the work each question requires. Multi-Source Reasoning sets cost three or four minutes; Two-Part Analysis costs two-plus minutes; even single-question Graphics or Table problems can sink a section if mishandled. The skill in this chapter is *workflow optimization*: knowing which problem types demand reading everything first, which reward jumping straight to the question, and when to walk away from a problem you're not going to solve in budget.

The mindset shift in this chapter: *protect the section, not the question*. Any one question is worth, at most, the average time per question. Letting a single hard MSR set consume eight minutes is a section-wide pacing collapse, even if you ultimately get that one question right.

## Why it matters

Three reasons workflow deserves its own chapter.

*DI has the most uneven question difficulty in the test*. Some DI questions take 30 seconds; some take 3 minutes. Strong solvers identify which is which in the first 30 seconds and adjust accordingly. Weak solvers treat every question the same and bleed time on the hard ones.

*The walk-away discipline is the highest-leverage habit*. Knowing when to abandon a question and move on prevents pacing collapses. Most DI score crashes come from one or two problems eating five-plus minutes; trained walk-away discipline prevents the crash.

*Format-specific workflows compress execution*. Each DI format has its own optimal workflow — read everything for MSR, jump to question for DS, sort first for tables. Memorizing the workflows turns DI from a high-cognitive-load section into a procedural one.

## Mental model

The DI section gives you a *fixed 45-minute budget for 20 questions* — about 2 minutes 15 seconds per question on average. Some questions cost one minute; some MSR sub-questions cost three. The discipline is to *match your time investment to the question's information value*, not to its surface difficulty.

This means a 30-second question is not just an easy question — it's *banked time available for the next hard question*. Use the savings deliberately. Don't burn the savings on the next question by lingering when you could move.

Three reminders follow from the budget framing. First, *track your time per question* as you go, glancing at the clock at logical break points. Second, *bank time on easy questions* and spend it on hard ones — don't redistribute the savings into linger time. Third, *walk away from problems that would exceed your budget* even if you can almost solve them. The walk-away is not a failure; it's a section-protection move.

## GMAT recognition signals

Five workflow choices for DI problems.

*Read everything first* — for MSR sets. The tab structure rewards an initial cataloging walk-through. About 60-90 seconds for the catalog, then per-question targeted reads.

*Read the question first, then the data* — for Table Analysis, Graphics, Two-Part. The question tells you which data to focus on; reading the data first wastes time on irrelevant information.

*Read the question stem alone, ignore the data initially* — for Data Sufficiency. The question's structure (yes/no vs value) determines what counts as sufficient. The statements come second.

*Skim and decide whether to invest* — for any DI problem on first encounter. If the problem looks like it will take more than 3 minutes and you don't have a clear path, consider walking away.

*Walk away* — when 2:30+ has elapsed and you don't have a clear path forward. The remaining 30 seconds are better spent moving to the next question and earning the next minute.

## Method

A four-step DI workflow protocol.

*Step 1. At the start of each question, glance at the format and decide the workflow.* MSR? Catalog the tabs. DS? Read the stem first. Tables? Sort first. Graphics? Axes first. Two-Part? Identify the cells and constraint. The workflow choice takes 5 seconds and compresses the rest of the work.

*Step 2. Track time as you go.* Glance at the timer at logical break points — after the data orientation, after the first question of an MSR set, after each computation. The data tells you whether you're on budget or over.

*Step 3. Walk-away threshold.* If 2:30 has elapsed on a single question and you don't see a clear path, mark a guess and move on. The investment is lost; protect the rest of the section.

*Step 4. Bank time on easy questions for hard ones.* When a question takes 30 seconds, you've earned 90 seconds of buffer. Spend it on the hardest problem in the next set, not by lingering on the next easy one.

## Common traps

Six recurring DI workflow traps.

*Treating every question with the same workflow.* Reading data first on Tables (good for MSR, bad for Tables). Reading the question first on MSR (good for Tables, bad for MSR). Fix: pick the workflow based on format.

*Sunk-cost continuation.* Two minutes into a problem with no progress, you keep going because you've invested two minutes. The investment is gone whether you continue or stop. Fix: respect the walk-away threshold.

*Not banking time.* Running fast on easy questions and then slowing right back down. The savings need to be banked deliberately for the hard problems. Fix: track time and use the buffer consciously.

*Re-reading data unnecessarily.* On MSR sets, returning to a tab you've already cataloged. Wastes 20-30 seconds. Fix: trust your source map.

*Computing past sufficiency.* On DS, computing the full answer when you only needed sufficiency. Wastes 30-60 seconds per problem. Fix: stop computing once sufficiency is established.

*Failing to mark and move.* Spending too long on a problem because you can't decide whether it's solvable. Mark a guess, move, and return at the end if time allows. Fix: practice the mark-and-move habit during drills.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating workflow choice and walk-away discipline.

*Hypothetical scenario.* You're 18 minutes into a 45-minute DI section. You've answered 6 questions in 18 minutes — average 3 minutes per question, well over budget. The next question is a Multi-Source Reasoning set with three tabs and four sub-questions. The time pressure is real.

*Workflow choice.* Catalog the tabs in 60 seconds (tab walk). Then go to the first sub-question. If a sub-question is taking more than 90 seconds, mark a guess and move to the next.

*Execution.* Tab walk: 60 seconds. Identifies that Tab 1 is description, Tab 2 is cost data, Tab 3 is volume data.

Sub-question 1 (cost-related): 75 seconds. Targeted read on Tab 2; quick computation. Done.

Sub-question 2 (cross-source synthesis): 2:00 elapsed, no clear path. The question needs combining Tab 2 and Tab 3 in a way that's not obvious. Decision: spend 30 more seconds; if still stuck, mark and move. At 2:30, no progress. Mark a guess and move on.

Sub-question 3 (volume-related): 60 seconds. Targeted read on Tab 3. Done.

Sub-question 4 (cost-volume comparison): 90 seconds. Doable with the source map already built. Done.

Total time on MSR set: 60 + 75 + 150 + 60 + 90 = 7 minutes 15 seconds. Slightly over budget but manageable. The walk-away on sub-question 2 saved 1-2 minutes that would otherwise have crashed the section.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap is to spend 4 minutes on sub-question 2, get it right, and crash on the next set. Better to mark a guess and protect the section's pacing. The walk-away cost: probably one wrong answer. The walk-away saved: enough time to attempt three more questions confidently.

The other trap is failing to track time. If you don't notice you're at 2:00 on sub-question 2, you don't know you should be considering walking away. The discipline is *check the timer at break points*, not just at the end.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Banking time on easy questions

*Setup.* You finish a Graphics question in 60 seconds (well under the 2:15 average). The next question is a hard MSR sub-question.

*Thinking process.* You've banked 75 seconds. You can spend that on the MSR question if needed, allowing you up to ~3:00 there without falling behind.

*Solution.* Use the bank deliberately — but don't expand to fill it just because the time is available. Aim for 2:30 on the MSR; bank a portion for the next hard question.

*Common mistake.* Filling the bank by lingering on the easy question instead of moving on. The bank is only useful if you actually move.

*Takeaway.* Time saved on easy questions is a budget for hard ones. Move when you've finished, even if you have leftover time. The leftover time is more valuable spent on a hard question than reading the easy one twice.

### Example 2 — Walk-away threshold

*Setup.* You've spent 2:30 on a Two-Part Analysis problem with no clear path. 8 questions remain. 12 minutes left.

*Thinking process.* Average time remaining per question: 12/8 = 1:30. You're already over budget by 60 seconds. Continuing risks bleeding into the next question's budget.

*Solution.* Mark a guess and move. The lost 2:30 is gone whether you continue or stop.

*Common mistake.* Continuing because "I'm 90 seconds away from the answer." That 90 seconds is borrowed from the next question, which will then go under-resourced. Net loss.

*Takeaway.* Sunk cost is irrelevant. Forward time is what matters. Walk away when 30+ seconds over budget on a single question.

### Example 3 — Workflow choice

*Setup.* The first question of the DI section is an MSR set with 4 sub-questions. You have 45 minutes total.

*Thinking process.* MSR rewards a tab walk first. Spend 60-90 seconds cataloging the tabs, then run sub-questions targeted to specific tabs. Average MSR set time: 7-9 minutes (about 1:45-2:15 per sub-question after the catalog).

*Solution.* Catalog first, target second. Don't try to read every tab in detail.

*Common mistake.* Treating MSR like Table Analysis — going question-first, hunting tabs as you go. MSR's information density rewards an upfront map.

*Takeaway.* Each DI format has its own optimal workflow. Match the workflow to the format.

DI workflow connects to every DI format chapter. *Data Sufficiency Logic* (Chapter 3.2), *Table Analysis* (Chapter 3.3), *Graphics Interpretation* (Chapter 3.4), *Multi-Source Reasoning* (Chapter 3.5), *Two-Part Analysis* (Chapter 3.6), and *Business and Data Interpretation* (Chapter 3.7) each describe their own format-specific workflows; this chapter provides the meta-framework for choosing among them.

Cross-section connection: the *walk-away discipline* in DI parallels the thirty-second checkpoint in Quant (Chapter 1.10) and the don't-grind-the-finalists habit in Verbal (Chapter 2.8). All three sections punish students who refuse to abandon a question that is not yielding — the timer doesn't care that you almost solved it. DI is where this habit pays back most because DI's pacing budget is the tightest relative to the information density per question; transferring the habit from DI back to the other sections is one of the highest-yield cross-section spillovers in the curriculum.
