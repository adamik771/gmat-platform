# Chapter 1.2 — Arithmetic Foundations

## Core idea

Arithmetic is the *low-level language* the rest of the Quant section speaks. Every algebra problem, every word problem, every number-property problem ultimately comes back to multiplying, dividing, distributing signs, and converting between fractions, decimals, and percentages. If your arithmetic is not automatic — if you slow down to compute, if you lose negatives during distribution, if you take ten seconds to convert 3/8 to a decimal — then arithmetic is a tax you pay on every problem in the section. That tax compounds. The fix is not to be smarter. It is to make arithmetic *boring* enough that you can do it while thinking about something else.

Most students underestimate this chapter because the content looks easy. The content is easy. The skill of deploying it under time pressure, with no cognitive overhead, on the third hour of a test, is not. One arithmetic slip per twelve-question set caps your Quant score in the mid-tier bands regardless of how strong your strategic skills are.

## Why it matters

Quant gives you about two minutes per problem on average. Inside those two minutes, you have to read the problem, plan the method, execute the arithmetic, sanity-check, and bubble. If arithmetic eats forty seconds of that — because you're computing carefully, double-checking signs, converting fractions slowly — you have eighty seconds left for everything else. That's barely enough to pick a method, much less to recover when your first method stalls. Strong solvers spend ten seconds on arithmetic and ninety seconds on thinking. The ratio is the score.

There is a second, less obvious reason. Arithmetic strain *increases trap susceptibility*. When your working memory is occupied by carrying digits, you lose the bandwidth to notice that the problem said "positive integer" or that the answer choice you picked has the wrong sign. The trap answer choices are positioned to catch students whose arithmetic is consuming the cognitive bandwidth they would otherwise spend on reading carefully. Tighter arithmetic means looser working memory means better trap awareness. The chapters chain.

## Mental model

Think of arithmetic as the *engine of a car*. When the engine works, you don't think about it — you steer, you pick lanes, you decide when to brake. When the engine sputters, you think about nothing else. The skill of driving a car is not the skill of operating the engine. It is the skill of making decisions while the engine runs unattended. Quant is the same. The engine is arithmetic. Get it to the point where it runs unattended, and the *real* test — method selection, recognition, trap awareness — becomes possible.

This is why students who study harder material before automating arithmetic often plateau. They are trying to drive while still consciously operating the throttle.

## GMAT recognition signals

Every Quant problem uses arithmetic, so the question is not "does this problem reward arithmetic" — it is "does this problem reward *aggressive arithmetic shortcuts*." Three signals tell you the answer is yes.

*Spread answer choices.* When the five answer choices are 12, 18, 24, 36, 48 — i.e., far enough apart that an estimation within 10% picks the answer — the problem is rewarding the solver who estimates aggressively rather than computing precisely. Sloppy computation is faster than careful computation when the answer choices are sparse.

*Compound percent or growth language.* When the problem chains percent changes ("increased by 20% then decreased by 15%"), or compounds growth ("doubles every three years for nine years"), the problem is rewarding multiplicative arithmetic and punishing additive shortcuts. The trap answers are positioned at the additive results.

*Fractions, decimals, and percents in the same problem.* When the problem mixes notations, it is rewarding the solver who can fluently switch notations to pick the fastest computation. A "30% of 250" problem is faster as "0.3 × 250" or "1/3 × 250 minus a bit" than as a percent-formula calculation. The notation is a tool; pick it consciously.

## Method

Four sub-fluencies separate strong arithmetic from weak arithmetic. Train them as separate drills, not as side effects of doing problems.

*Estimation by rounding.* Round both operands aggressively, compute the rough product, then refine if needed. 47 × 89 is "about 50 × 90 = 4,500, slightly under because both numbers were rounded up — call it 4,200." That estimate is enough to pick the right answer choice if the answers are spread by more than 10%. If they are not, refine: 47 × 89 = 47 × 90 − 47 = 4,230 − 47 = 4,183.

*Sign tracking under distribution.* When you distribute a negative across a parenthetical, write the negative outside the bracket explicitly and apply it sign by sign. − (3x − 4y + 7) becomes −3x + 4y − 7. Lost negatives are the most common source of "I knew how to do that, I just slipped" errors. Practice them deliberately — five problems a day for a week — and the slip rate drops to near zero.

*Fraction-decimal-percent fluency.* Memorize the conversion table for the most common values. 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%. 1/3 = 0.333… = 33.3%. 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%. 1/6 = 0.166… = 16.6%. 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5%. 1/9 = 0.111… = 11.1%. 2/3, 3/4, 5/8, 7/8, 1/12 — drill the whole grid until each conversion is reflex. Then practice deciding which notation is fastest for the current computation. "30% of 90" is faster as "(3/10) × 90 = 27" than as "0.3 × 90 = 27" — both work, but the fraction form skips the decimal-place tracking step.

*Multiplicative chaining for percent changes.* A 20% increase followed by a 25% decrease is *1.20 × 0.75 = 0.90*, a net 10% decrease. Never add percent changes. Always multiply. Internalize this so deeply that "20% up then 25% down" produces "0.90" before you think about it. The trap answer for this problem is "5% decrease," produced by adding (−25 + 20). It will appear on the test, and it will catch the students who chain additively.

## Common traps

Five arithmetic traps recur constantly enough to memorize.

*Percent-base confusion.* "20% greater than X" is 1.20X. "20% of X" is 0.20X. Students who confuse the two answer the wrong problem. The fix: read the *preposition*. "Greater than" multiplies (1 + percent). "Of" multiplies (percent itself).

*Additive percent chaining.* Treating "20% up then 20% down" as zero net change. The actual answer is 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, a 4% net decrease. The trap answer "0%" is on the page for the additive thinker.

*Cross-multiplying inequalities of unknown sign.* When you cross-multiply 3/x < 4 to get 3 < 4x, you have implicitly assumed x is positive. If x is negative, the inequality flips. The fix: before cross-multiplying any inequality with a variable in the denominator, ask whether the variable could be zero or negative.

*Decimal-place loss.* Multiplying 0.04 × 0.7 and getting 0.28 instead of 0.028. The fix: count decimal places in operands, sum them, place the decimal point in the answer to match. 0.04 has two decimal places, 0.7 has one, so the product has three: 0.028.

*Sign loss in distribution.* As noted above, the most common slip on the section. The fix: explicit sign tracking, drilled until automatic.

Each of these traps has a clean fix. The fix is rarely *understanding* — most students understand each rule when shown it. The fix is *automaticity* — drilling the rule until it requires no working memory.

## Original mini-example

A worked example to show how arithmetic fluency converts to time savings.

*Problem.* A jacket originally cost $80. The store first marked the price up by 25%, then offered a 20% discount on the marked-up price. What is the final price as a percentage of the original cost?

*Method-selection scan.* Answer choices (assume A: 95%, B: 100%, C: 105%, D: 110%, E: 115%) — close together, all within 20% of each other. Direct calculation, not estimation. Multiplicative chaining is the right tool.

*Method.* The final price is 80 × 1.25 × 0.80. Compute as a chain. 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00 (this is a clean cancellation; 1.25 = 5/4 and 0.80 = 4/5, product is 1). So the final price is 80 × 1.00 = 80, which is 100% of the original.

*Time spent.* About fifteen seconds — most of it in the recognition that 1.25 and 0.80 are reciprocals (5/4 × 4/5 = 1), which collapses the whole problem to "the final price equals the original."

*Trap to avoid.* The trap answer is "5% premium" or "5% discount," produced by adding 25 − 20 = 5%. Students who chain additively will produce it confidently. Students whose arithmetic is fluent will not even consider it.

This is the kind of problem where the gap between strong and weak solvers is not skill — it is *automaticity*. The strong solver sees 5/4 × 4/5 and the chain collapses. The weak solver computes 80 × 1.25 = 100, then 100 × 0.80 = 80, which is a longer path that produces the right answer but takes thirty seconds. Across the 21-question Quant section, those thirty-second taxes add up to a full pacing collapse.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Multiplicative percent chaining

*Setup.* A widget originally costs $100. The wholesaler raises the price by 30%, then offers a 20% discount on the raised price. What is the final price?

*Thinking process.* Chain the changes multiplicatively, not additively. Final = 100 × 1.30 × 0.80.

*Solution.* 100 × 1.30 × 0.80 = 100 × 1.04 = $104.

*Common mistake.* Computing 30% − 20% = 10% net increase, giving $110. The additive shortcut fails because the second percentage applies to the *raised* price, not the original.

*Takeaway.* Always multiply percent changes; never add them. The additive trap answer is positioned for students who shortcut without thinking about which base each percentage references.

### Example 2 — Sign tracking under distribution

*Setup.* Simplify: 5x − 3(2x − 4y + 1).

*Thinking process.* Distribute the negative carefully. The −3 multiplies each term inside the parentheses, including the *signs* of those terms.

*Solution.* 5x − 3(2x − 4y + 1) = 5x − 6x + 12y − 3 = −x + 12y − 3.

*Common mistake.* Distributing the −3 across only the first term and forgetting the rest, producing 5x − 6x − 4y + 1 = −x − 4y + 1. The sign on the −4y stays negative because − × − = +.

*Takeaway.* When distributing a negative across a parenthesized expression, write the operation explicitly: each term inside the parentheses gets multiplied by the negative coefficient, including its sign.

### Example 3 — Estimation over precise calculation

*Setup.* What is 47 × 89? Answer choices: 3,800, 4,000, 4,200, 4,400, 4,600.

*Thinking process.* Round both: 47 ≈ 50, 89 ≈ 90. Estimate: 50 × 90 = 4,500. Both numbers were rounded up, so the actual product is somewhat below 4,500. Choices below 4,500 by a moderate amount: 4,200 or 4,400.

*Solution.* 47 × 89 = 4,183. Closest answer: 4,200.

*Common mistake.* Computing the full product longhand — works, but takes 30+ seconds when the answer choices are spread enough that estimation produces the answer in 5.

*Takeaway.* Round-and-adjust beats full computation when the spread between answer choices exceeds your estimation error. The skill is calibrating that error.
