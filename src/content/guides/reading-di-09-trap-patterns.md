# Chapter 3.9 — DI Trap Patterns

## Core idea

DI wrong answers cluster into a *finite trap inventory*, just like CR and RC wrong answers do. The traps differ from Verbal traps because the medium is different — DI is data-rich, so DI traps exploit data-reading errors specifically. But the structure is the same: each wrong answer is *engineered* to catch a specific kind of mistake, and recognizing the trap pattern lets you eliminate fast.

This chapter is the DI trap inventory. Memorize the nine patterns, scan each answer for which trap it represents, and reject. The discipline is the same as in Verbal Chapter 2.7: *named-trap recognition replaces gut-based elimination*.

## Why it matters

Three reasons DI trap recognition matters.

*DI traps are subtle*. A unit confusion off by a factor of 1000 produces an answer that looks numerically plausible. A denominator mistake gives a percentage that's "in the right range." Strong solvers spot the engineered subtlety; weak solvers don't.

*DI trap recognition compresses elimination*. Each named trap eliminates one or more answer choices. The right answer is among the survivors.

*Most DI mistakes come from one of nine patterns*. Memorizing the inventory and re-classifying error-log entries by pattern surfaces your dominant trap, which targets the fix.

## Mental model

The right framing here: each DI wrong answer is *engineered*, not accidental. The test designer placed it specifically to catch a particular kind of misreading — a unit slip, a denominator confusion, a confused source — and the wrong answer's number or wording is calibrated to look plausible to someone making that exact error. Students who don't recognize the engineering see five plausible answers and guess. Students who do recognize it see four engineered counterfeits and one match.

The named-trap inventory in this chapter is the *catalog of the engineering choices*. Once you know which counterfeits the test produces, the work shifts from "evaluate each answer" to "spot which trap each one represents."

## GMAT recognition signals

The nine recurring DI trap patterns.

*Wrong unit.* The data is in millions; the answer treats it as ones (or vice versa). The numerical part of the answer is "right" but the unit is off. Signal: a clean factor of 1,000 (or 100, or 1,000,000) between your computed answer and the trap answer.

*Wrong denominator.* The question asks for "percentage of total revenue"; you computed "percentage of one segment's revenue" (or vice versa). The number is within range but referenced to the wrong base. Signal: an answer that's plausible but referenced to the wrong total.

*Correlation vs causation.* The data shows two variables moving together; the trap answer claims one causes the other. Often there's a confounding variable or reverse causation. Signal: causal language ("causes," "leads to," "results in") in an answer when the data only shows correlation.

*Trend extrapolation.* The data shows a past trend; the trap answer projects the trend forward without justification. The data may not support the projection. Signal: future-tense claims ("will continue to," "is likely to grow") in answers when the question only provides past data.

*Irrelevant data.* The trap answer uses data that wasn't relevant to the question. Often a tab or column that the question didn't actually ask about. Signal: the answer references something you noticed in the data but the question didn't target.

*Hidden condition.* The data has a constraint or condition that the trap answer ignores. The condition might be in the description tab ("only customers over 18 are included") or in a footnote. Signal: an answer that's mathematically valid but violates a stated condition.

*Sorting mistakes.* On Table Analysis, the trap answer is the value at the wrong end of the sort — top instead of bottom, or vice versa. Signal: the right *direction* of the answer is reversed.

*Scale mistakes.* On Graphics, the trap answer treats the chart's visual size as proportional when the chart has a non-zero baseline or log scale. Signal: an answer that exaggerates or compresses the actual data difference.

*Source-mixing.* On MSR, the trap answer combines data from the wrong tabs, or applies a constraint from one tab to the data of another. Signal: an answer that uses figures from incorrect sources.

## Method

A four-step DI trap-elimination protocol.

*Step 1. Identify the question's exact target.* What unit? What denominator? What time period? What scope? Be precise — vagueness is what trap doors exploit.

*Step 2. Compute or look up the value with full attention to units, denominators, and conditions.* Don't shortcut.

*Step 3. Run each answer choice through the trap checklist.* For each answer, ask: is this *wrong unit*? *Wrong denominator*? *Correlation-as-causation*? *Trend extrapolation*? *Irrelevant data*? *Hidden condition*? *Sorting mistake*? *Scale mistake*? *Source mixing*? Most wrong answers fall under one of these.

*Step 4. Verify the survivor against the question.* Once you've eliminated the trap answers, the survivor should match the question's target precisely. If it doesn't, re-check.

## Common traps

Five meta-traps in DI trap elimination.

*Recognizing the trap but second-guessing.* You spot a unit error in an answer and start to wonder if you misread the unit. Trust the recognition. Re-check the data; if your reading is right, eliminate.

*Failing to name the trap explicitly.* You feel an answer is wrong but can't articulate why. The fix is to force the name — wrong unit? wrong denominator? sorting mistake? — and only eliminate when you can name it.

*Over-trusting the visual on Graphics.* Even after reading the chapter on graphics traps, students still default to the visual impression on hard problems. The fix is to *read the axes every time*, not just on flagged questions.

*Mixing sources confidently.* MSR encourages cross-tab synthesis, and students sometimes combine sources without realizing they're using data from the wrong tab. Fix: explicitly note which tab each figure came from.

*Forgetting hidden conditions across questions.* On MSR sets, a condition stated in tab 1 (e.g., "only customers over 18 are included") applies across all sub-questions. Forgetting it on the third sub-question after applying it correctly on the first is common. Fix: keep the conditions visible in your scratch work for the duration of the set.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating named-trap elimination.

*Hypothetical scenario.* A table shows revenue and customer counts for four product lines. Revenue column is in $ millions. Customer counts are in thousands.

| Product | Revenue ($ millions) | Customers (thousands) |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------|
| A | 12 | 80 |
| B | 18 | 60 |
| C | 9 | 30 |
| D | 21 | 70 |

*Question.* "What is the average revenue per customer for Product B (in dollars)?"

*Sample answer choices.*

(A) $0.30 — Trap: *wrong unit*. This is what you'd get if you treated revenue as units (12) and customers as units (80), giving 12/80 = $0.15 — but in fact: $18M revenue / 60,000 customers = $300 per customer. Off by a factor of 1000.

(B) $300 — IN. Correct: $18,000,000 revenue / 60,000 customers = $300/customer.

(C) $30 — Trap: *wrong unit*. This treats revenue as thousands (18) divided by customers as thousands (60), or some similar mismatch.

(D) $1.20 — Trap: *wrong denominator* or *wrong product*. This might be the result of using Product A's customer count (80) with Product B's revenue, or some other mix.

(E) $0.15 — Trap: *wrong unit*. Treating revenue and customers both as raw counts (12/80 in some base).

*Answer.* (B).

*Trap to avoid.* The trap is wrong unit, primarily. The student who doesn't read column headers carefully treats $18M as 18 and 60K as 60, computing 18/60 = $0.30 — answer (A). This is the most common DI mistake on questions involving units. The fix: read every column header, note units explicitly on scratch, and convert as needed.

The other trap is wrong denominator, where the student divides Product B's revenue by Product A's customer count (or some other mismatch). The fix: when computing per-customer or per-unit metrics, double-check that the numerator and denominator come from the *same row*.

*Time spent.* About 60 seconds — most of it in carefully reading the units and the row.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Wrong unit

*Setup.* Table column header: "Annual revenue ($ millions)." Cell value: 8. Question: "Express Company X's annual revenue in dollars."

*Thinking process.* The unit is millions, so 8 in the table = 8,000,000 in actual dollars.

*Solution.* $8,000,000 (or $8M).

*Common mistake.* Bubbling $8 (treating cell as raw dollars) or $8,000 (treating cell as thousands). Off by a factor of 1,000,000 or 1,000.

*Takeaway.* Always read the column header before extracting any numerical value. Wrong-unit traps are positioned for students who skip this step.

### Example 2 — Wrong denominator

*Setup.* A pie chart shows revenue by segment: Segment A = 40%, B = 30%, C = 20%, D = 10%. A separate text says total revenue = $200M. Question: "What percentage of Segment A's revenue comes from Product X (which produces $32M of revenue)?"

*Thinking process.* The denominator is *Segment A's revenue*, not total revenue. Segment A = 40% of $200M = $80M. Product X within Segment A = $32M. So $32M / $80M = 40%.

*Solution.* 40%.

*Common mistake.* Computing $32M / $200M = 16% (using total revenue as denominator). Or computing $32M / $80M = 40% but bubbling 16% by reflex.

*Takeaway.* "What percentage of X" requires X as the denominator. Identify the denominator from the question's wording before computing.

### Example 3 — Source-mixing

*Setup.* MSR set: Tab 2 has cost data; Tab 3 has volume data. Question requires per-unit cost — i.e., total cost from Tab 2 divided by total volume from Tab 3.

*Thinking process.* Pull total cost from Tab 2; pull total volume from Tab 3; divide.

*Solution.* Cost / Volume from the appropriate tabs.

*Common mistake.* Pulling cost from Tab 2 and accidentally pulling volume from a different metric on Tab 2 (e.g., from a "year" or "category" column). Or pulling both from Tab 3 because you're already there.

*Takeaway.* Track which tab each figure came from. Source-mixing produces wrong results that look numerically plausible — the most insidious DI trap.

## Connection to other skills

DI trap recognition complements *Verbal Trap Answer Patterns* (Chapter 2.7), which catalogs the analogous patterns for CR and RC. The same discipline applies in both: name the trap, eliminate. The DI patterns are data-specific (units, denominators, sources); the Verbal patterns are language-specific (extreme, reversed, unsupported); but the elimination protocol is identical.

Cross-section connection: trap recognition compounds across sections. A student who has automated *named-trap elimination* on Verbal will pick it up faster on DI, and vice versa. Train one and the other gets easier.
