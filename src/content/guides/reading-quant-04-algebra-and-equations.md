# Chapter 1.4 — Algebra and Equations

## Core idea

Algebra is a *precision tool*, not a default. The single most expensive habit on Quant is reaching for algebra on every problem because it's the tool you trust most. The students who do this are not wrong about algebra working — algebra works on most problems eventually. They are wrong about which problems algebra wins on. On many problems, backsolving, estimation, or structural insight reaches the answer in a third of the time, and the algebraic path costs you the pacing budget you needed for the next three problems.

This chapter is about two things: when algebra is the right tool, and how to execute algebra cleanly when it is. The second is content. The first is method selection — and method selection wins more points than content does at every level above the 70th percentile.

## Why it matters

Three reasons algebra deserves a chapter of its own.

*It is the most overused tool on the section.* Watch a middle scorer work the 21-question Quant set and you will see equations set up on roughly seventeen or eighteen of the questions, when maybe eight or nine of them actually reward equations as the fastest path. The remaining ten or so reward backsolving, estimation, structural insight, or test-cases — and the time bled to algebra on those problems is the difference between finishing the section and not.

*The algebra rules students forget are concentrated in five places.* Exponents (especially fractional and negative exponents), absolute value (especially equations with absolute values on both sides), inequalities (sign flips when multiplying or dividing by negatives), quadratic factoring (recognition of common patterns), and systems of equations (when to substitute, when to eliminate). Most "I forgot how to do algebra" failures come from one of these five. Drill them and the gap closes.

*Algebraic shortcuts collapse multi-step problems into one line.* Recognizing that x² − y² = (x + y)(x − y), or that the sum of three consecutive integers is three times the middle one, or that a + b + c can be solved without finding each variable separately — these aren't tricks. They are the *structural insights* that elite scorers see in five seconds and average scorers grind through in two minutes.

## Mental model

Picture algebra as a *scalpel*. It is the right tool for clean, surgical work — taking apart a precise relationship, isolating a variable, solving an equation that has been set up cleanly. It is the wrong tool for blunt-force work — answering a question where five candidate values are listed and you can just check them, or estimating a quantity where rough work is enough.

A scalpel-user looks at the problem and asks: *can this be solved without algebra?* If yes, use the cheaper tool. If no, then algebra is the right call — and at that point, execute precisely. The scalpel image carries two reminders: don't reach for the tool reflexively, and when you do reach for it, use it with care.

## GMAT recognition signals

Algebra is the right tool when you see these signals.

*Clean variable counts with enough equations to solve directly.* Two variables, two equations, no constraints — set up and solve. One variable with a quadratic relationship — set up and factor. The variable count and equation count line up and the relationships are explicit.

*Expressions to manipulate, not values to find.* "Simplify the following," or "which of the following is equivalent to," or "find an expression for." These problems are *not* about finding a number. They are about restructuring an expression. Algebra is the only tool that works.

*Quadratic patterns in the problem.* x² − y², x² + 2xy + y², x² − 5x + 6, or any expression that you recognize as factorable. The factored form is almost always more useful than the expanded form.

*Exponent identities staring at you.* 2^(x+1) = 8 × 2^(x−1) is a problem about rewriting both sides with the same base. There is no way to solve it except algebraically — but the algebra is fast once you recognize the pattern.

Algebra is the *wrong* tool when you see these signals: numeric answer choices that you can plug in (use backsolving), problems where a quick estimate distinguishes the answer (use estimation), problems with three or more variables and only two equations (the problem is asking for a relationship, not a value, and brute-force algebra will fail), or problems where the structure is symmetric or recurring (use the structural insight).

## Method

A four-step protocol for problems where algebra is the right tool.

*Step 1. Translate cleanly.* Define each variable with one letter, write what each represents on scratch ("x = number of widgets," "p = price per widget"), and translate the problem into equations slowly. Speed at this step backfires — a wrong translation produces an algebra-correct, problem-wrong answer.

*Step 2. Pause and look for shortcuts before solving.* Once your equations are set up, *do not solve immediately*. Scan the equations and the question for shortcuts. Is the question asking for a + b when you have separate equations for a and b? You may not need to find them individually — adding the equations might give a + b directly. Is the question asking for x²? You might be able to answer it without finding x. Is the system separable in a way that skips a variable? Look first.

*Step 3. Execute precisely.* Track signs. Distribute carefully. When you square both sides, remember that you may introduce an extraneous solution and need to check. When you multiply both sides of an inequality by a variable expression, remember the sign-flip rule.

*Step 4. Sanity-check against the question.* Did you solve for the right thing? Common failure: the problem asks for x + y, you solved for x and y separately, and you bubbled x. The fix: re-read the question after solving, before bubbling.

## Common traps

Six traps catch students at this skill more than any others.

*Algebra by default.* Reaching for equations when backsolving or estimation would have been faster. Symptom: you have set up two equations, simplified them, and noticed that you could have plugged in answer choice C in fifteen seconds.

*Solving past sufficiency.* The question asks for the value of x + y. You have a + b + c = 10 and you can see that x + y = 10 − c. You don't need x and y separately. Solving for them anyway costs ninety seconds you didn't have.

*Squaring without checking.* If x² = 9, then x = ±3, not just x = 3. Squaring loses sign information. Whenever you square an expression to solve, your answer set is potentially larger than the true answer set; check each candidate against the original problem.

*Sign-flip on inequalities.* Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number flips the inequality. Dividing by a *variable expression of unknown sign* is a subtler version of the same trap — you don't know whether to flip. Solution: split into cases based on the sign of the variable, or restructure to avoid dividing by it.

*Forgetting absolute value cases.* |x − 3| = 5 has two solutions: x = 8 and x = −2. Students who write x − 3 = 5 and stop have missed half the solution set. Always set up |A| = B as the disjunction A = B or A = −B.

*Quadratic factoring failures.* Trying to use the quadratic formula on x² − 5x + 6 = 0 instead of factoring directly to (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0. The formula works but is slower. For coefficients in the single digits, look for clean factors first.

## Original mini-example

A worked example showing the difference between algebra-by-default and algebra-with-shortcut-recognition.

*Problem.* If $a + b = 7$ and $a^2 − b^2 = 21$, what is the value of $a − b$?

*Method-selection scan.* Two equations, two unknowns. Looks like an algebra problem. The question asks for a − b, not for a and b individually — that's the recognition signal.

*Method (the slow path, for contrast).* Set up: a + b = 7, a² − b² = 21. Substitute: from equation 1, a = 7 − b. Plug into equation 2: (7 − b)² − b² = 21. Expand: 49 − 14b + b² − b² = 21. Simplify: 49 − 14b = 21. Solve: 14b = 28, b = 2. Then a = 5. Then a − b = 3. Time: about ninety seconds.

*Method (the shortcut).* Recognize that a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). So 21 = 7 × (a − b), which means a − b = 3. Time: about ten seconds.

*Trap to avoid.* The slow path produces the right answer; this isn't about getting it wrong, it's about pacing. Students who default to substitution miss the structural insight that (a + b)(a − b) is staring at them in the second equation. Across the 21-question Quant section, missing five shortcuts of this size is a full minute and a half of accumulated time bled to slower paths than necessary. That minute and a half is two extra problems you could have answered confidently.

The lesson is the *pause-for-shortcut* habit at Step 2 of the protocol. The structure was visible. The recognition was the score.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Difference-of-squares shortcut

*Setup.* If a + b = 9 and a² − b² = 27, what is a − b?

*Thinking process.* Recognize the structural insight: a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). So 27 = 9 × (a − b), giving a − b = 3.

*Solution.* a − b = 3.

*Common mistake.* Setting up substitution: solve a + b = 9 for a, then plug into a² − b² = 27, expand, simplify — produces the same answer in 90+ seconds when the structural shortcut takes 10.

*Takeaway.* When a problem gives you a + b and a² − b² (or any factored form pair), the answer often falls out in one step if you recognize the factorization. Pause for shortcuts before substituting.

### Example 2 — Quadratic factoring vs. formula

*Setup.* Solve x² − 7x + 12 = 0.

*Thinking process.* Look for two integers that multiply to 12 and sum to 7. 3 and 4 work. Factor: (x − 3)(x − 4) = 0. Solutions: x = 3 or x = 4.

*Solution.* x ∈ {3, 4}.

*Common mistake.* Reaching for the quadratic formula immediately. Works, but takes 60+ seconds for arithmetic that should take 10. The formula is a fallback; clean factoring is the default for small-coefficient quadratics.

*Takeaway.* For quadratics with small integer coefficients, scan for clean factor pairs first. The formula is the right tool only when factoring fails.

### Example 3 — Inequality sign-flip

*Setup.* Solve −3x > 12.

*Thinking process.* Divide both sides by −3, *flipping* the inequality direction because we're dividing by a negative.

*Solution.* x < −4.

*Common mistake.* Dividing without flipping, producing x > −4. The sign-flip rule is the most common inequality slip.

*Takeaway.* Whenever you multiply or divide an inequality by a negative number, the inequality flips. Whenever you do this with a *variable* of unknown sign, you have to split into cases — or restructure to avoid the division entirely.

The skill of going from messy prose to clean formal structure is one skill. Algebra is the most familiar instance of it.
