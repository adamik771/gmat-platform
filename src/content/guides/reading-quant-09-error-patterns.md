# Chapter 1.9 — Quant Error Patterns

## Core idea

Quant mistakes are not random. They cluster into a *small number of recurring failure modes*, and each student tends to have one or two dominant patterns that account for most of their wrong answers. The mistake at problem 7 of a practice set probably has the same root cause as the mistake at problem 23 — they look like different problems, but the failure mode is the same. Once you can name your dominant pattern, you can target the fix specifically. Without that diagnosis, students re-drill problems hoping for general improvement, which produces general improvement at one-third the rate of targeted improvement.

This chapter catalogs the seven failure modes. Read it once to understand the taxonomy, then return to it after every two-week drill block to re-diagnose. The dominant pattern often shifts as you fix the previous one — which is itself a sign the diagnosis-and-fix loop is working.

## Why it matters

Three reasons error analysis deserves its own chapter.

*The error log is the most underused tool in GMAT prep*. Most students log mistakes, redo them once, and move on. That's a tiny fraction of the value the log can provide. The full value comes from *patterns across mistakes* — the realization that three of last week's wrong answers all came from the same trap, even though the surface problems were different. The chapter teaches you what to look for.

*Misdiagnosis is the largest hidden tax in prep*. Students under-tag *Strategy* errors and over-tag *Careless* errors, because Strategy means admitting they picked the wrong approach and Careless means it was just a slip. The diagnosis matters because it routes the fix. A careless error gets a "slow down" remedy. A strategy error gets a re-read of the method-selection chapter. Routing the wrong fix wastes the prep budget.

*Different patterns require different fixes*. There is no general "study harder" remedy. Knowledge gaps need content review. Reasoning gaps need worked-solution study. Pacing gaps need timed drills with skip discipline. Careless gaps need arithmetic drilling. The chapter teaches which fix matches which pattern.

## Mental model

Every Quant mistake breaks at exactly one point in a five-step process: *read* the problem, *plan* the method, *translate* into mathematical form, *compute* the result, *check* against the question. The break point is the step where things went wrong. Naming the break point gives you the fix. A read break is fixed by reading more carefully. A plan break is fixed by training method selection. A translate break is fixed by drilling word-problem translation. A compute break is fixed by drilling arithmetic. A check break is fixed by adding the sanity-check step to your reflexes.

Most students don't separate the steps when they review mistakes. They look at the whole problem as "the one I got wrong" and feel bad. The fix starts with separation: which step broke?

## GMAT recognition signals

Each error pattern has its own signature. Recognize yours.

*Careless errors.* You knew the skill. You knew the method. You slipped on arithmetic, lost a sign, dropped a digit, or misread a number. Symptom: when you re-attempt the problem cold, you get it right immediately. Signal: the gap between "what you did" and "the right answer" is one mechanical step.

*Hidden constraints.* You produced a mathematically valid answer that violated an explicit constraint of the problem ("positive integer," "distinct," "greater than zero"). Symptom: your answer choice was a trap answer specifically positioned for students who missed the constraint. Signal: the right answer is in the same vicinity as yours but adjusted for the constraint.

*Misread units.* You computed in the wrong units. Hours when minutes were asked. Dollars when cents. The whole problem is right except the unit conversion. Signal: your answer is off by a clean factor (60, 100, 1,000) from the right answer.

*Solving for the wrong question.* The problem asked for *x + y*. You solved for *x*. Signal: the right answer is what your variable represented but combined with another variable in a way you skipped over.

*Overcomplication.* You used a heavy method (algebra, full enumeration) when a light method (estimation, structural insight, complement) would have been faster and cleaner. The heavy method introduced an arithmetic error along the way. Signal: when you re-solve with the lighter method, the work is half a page shorter.

*Premature calculation.* You started computing before fully reading the problem or scanning the answer choices. The early work was on the wrong setup, and you discovered it midway through. Signal: scratch paper has multiple crossed-out attempts, and the final right approach is at the bottom.

*Sanity-check failure.* Your answer was implausible — wrong order of magnitude, negative when it should be positive, non-integer when integers were required — and you bubbled anyway. Signal: a five-second pre-bubble check would have caught it.

## Method

A four-step error-review protocol. Run it on every wrong answer.

*Step 1. Identify the break point.* Of the five process steps (read, plan, translate, compute, check), which one broke? Be specific. "I read the problem fine, but I planned to use algebra when backsolving was faster" is a plan break, not a careless break.

*Step 2. Tag the error in the error log* using the platform's six-tag taxonomy: Conceptual, Careless, Time pressure, Misread, Strategy, Other. The tag should match the break point. Plan breaks → Strategy. Compute breaks → Careless (if the skill was known) or Conceptual (if not). Read breaks → Misread. Check breaks → don't have a tag of their own; usually they show up alongside another tag, but note the missed sanity-check in the notes field.

*Step 3. Classify by frequency.* After every twenty mistakes, look across the error log for the *dominant pattern*. Most students have one tag that accounts for thirty to fifty percent of their errors. That's the one to target.

*Step 4. Apply the matching fix.* Conceptual gaps → re-read the relevant chapter. Careless errors → drill arithmetic precision. Time-pressure errors → drill skip discipline and pacing. Misreads → slow down on translation, drill the two-pass reading habit. Strategy errors → re-read the *Method Selection* chapter (1.8) and drill method-naming on twenty problems. Other → review individually; this tag should be rare.

## Common traps

Five recurring error-review traps.

*Over-tagging Careless.* Treating every wrong answer as a "slip" because the alternative — admitting you picked the wrong method or didn't know the skill — feels worse. The cost: you never address the real failure mode. Fix: be honest. If the same kind of slip happens twice, it's not a slip.

*Under-tagging Strategy.* Refusing to tag a problem as a method-selection error because you "could have done it the other way." The point is not whether you could have; it's whether you *did* and whether the choice cost you time. If algebra cost you ninety seconds when backsolving would have taken twenty, that's a Strategy error even though the algebra was correct.

*Re-doing problems without re-reading the chapter.* The redo is the smallest part of the value. The bigger value is the chapter re-read that teaches you to recognize the trap on a *fresh* problem. Without the re-read, the redo is rote.

*Treating the error log as a checklist.* Logging the mistake, marking it reviewed, and moving on. The log isn't a checklist; it's a *diagnostic tool*. It earns its keep when it surfaces patterns you act on.

*Reviewing mistakes alone instead of with the chapter.* The mistake without the framing is just a random data point. With the chapter open, the same mistake is a specific case of a known trap, which makes the lesson generalizable.

## Original mini-example

A worked example showing how a single mistake can be diagnosed across the seven patterns.

*Problem.* If $n$ is a positive integer such that $3n$ is a multiple of 12, what is the smallest possible value of $n$?

*The wrong answer.* A student bubbles n = 4.

*Surface diagnosis.* Wrong answer; the right answer is n = 4. Wait, that's not wrong. Let me re-cast.

*The actual wrong answer.* A student bubbles n = 1.

*The student's reasoning.* "If 3n is a multiple of 12, then 3n = 12, so n = 4. But the smallest positive integer is 1, so the answer is 1."

*Diagnosis.* This is a *misread* error. The student translated "the smallest possible value of n such that 3n is a multiple of 12" as "the smallest n," ignoring the constraint that 3n must actually be a multiple of 12. The constraint was right there in the problem.

*Right answer.* 3n must be divisible by 12. So n must be divisible by 4 (since 3 contributes a factor of 3, but not 4, to the product). Smallest positive integer n divisible by 4 is 4.

*Tag.* Misread. The fix routes to "slow down on translation" and "always re-read the constraint before producing a candidate."

*Trap to avoid.* The trap answer 1 is positioned exactly for students who translate the problem hastily. The lesson: when the problem says "smallest positive integer such that X," the constraint X is *the entire problem* — not a side condition. Read X before producing a candidate.

This example shows the pattern. A wrong answer is not just "wrong." It is a specific pattern — Misread, in this case — and the fix is specific: slow down on the constraint clause. Generic remedies ("study harder," "be more careful") would not address this. The taxonomy does.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Misread vs. conceptual

*Setup.* Problem: "If x is a positive integer and 2x + 5 < 17, what is the largest possible value of x?" A student answers 6.

*Thinking process.* The correct approach: 2x < 12, so x < 6. The largest positive integer less than 6 is 5.

*Solution to the problem.* x = 5.

*Common mistake.* The student answered 6, missing the *strict* inequality. If the inequality were ≤, the answer would be 6; with <, it must be strictly less.

*Takeaway.* This is a *misread* error, not a conceptual error. The student knew the procedure but misread "<" as "≤." The fix routes to careful re-reading, not to re-learning inequalities.

### Example 2 — Strategy mistagged as careless

*Setup.* A student spent 2 minutes 30 seconds solving a probability problem by enumerating all favorable outcomes individually, got the right answer, but ran out of time on the next two problems. She tags the wasted-time problem "Careless" and moves on.

*Thinking process.* The mistake here isn't carelessness — the student got the answer right. The mistake is *strategy*: choosing direct enumeration over the complement-counting shortcut, which would have produced the answer in 30 seconds.

*Solution to the diagnosis.* Tag this as Strategy, not Careless. The fix routes to *Quant Method Selection* (Chapter 1.8), specifically the complement-counting habit.

*Common mistake.* Over-tagging Careless because the original problem was answered correctly. The score loss came from the *next two problems*, which the student rushed because of the time bled here. That cascading time cost is the strategy failure.

*Takeaway.* Pacing-induced wrong answers downstream are usually traceable to a Strategy error upstream. Tag honestly — the time you spent matters as much as the answer you got.

### Example 3 — Sanity-check failure

*Setup.* A student solves a probability problem and gets 1.4 as the answer.

*Thinking process.* Probabilities are bounded between 0 and 1. Any answer outside this range is an arithmetic error somewhere — likely a misplaced decimal, a missed fraction, or a flipped numerator/denominator.

*Solution to the diagnosis.* The student's answer is invalid by definition. They should have caught this at the sanity-check step.

*Common mistake.* Bubbling 1.4 because the arithmetic came out that way and not running the *plausibility check*. A 5-second sanity check would have surfaced the error immediately.

*Takeaway.* Every answer needs a final plausibility check against the question's natural constraints (probabilities ≤ 1, counts as non-negative integers, percent ≤ 100% in most contexts, etc.). The check is the cheapest insurance available.
