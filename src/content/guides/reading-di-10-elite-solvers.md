# Chapter 3.10 — How Elite DI Solvers Think

## Core idea

This is the synthesis chapter for Data Insights. The previous nine chapters laid out the section's mindset, format-specific frameworks, timing discipline, and trap inventory. This chapter assembles them into a picture of *what elite DI scoring looks like in real time*. The picture is not faster reading or stronger math — it's a small set of *information-management habits* that produce 90th-plus performance on the section that most students find hardest.

DI is the section where the gap between average preparation and elite preparation is widest. Most students don't train DI as a distinct skill set; they assume their Quant and Verbal habits will transfer. They partially do — but DI rewards specific habits (triage, source mapping, walk-away discipline) that the other sections don't train as explicitly. This chapter names them.

## Why it matters

Three reasons the synthesis chapter matters.

*DI is the highest-leverage prep target*. Most students can move their DI score 50-80 points with focused prep, more than either Quant or Verbal at the same time investment. The reason: the specific habits that decide DI aren't trained anywhere else, so targeted training pays back fast.

*DI synthesis transfers downward*. Strong DI habits — triage, structural reading, walk-away discipline — improve Quant and Verbal as side effects. Students who train DI seriously often see their Quant and Verbal scores improve too, even without specific work on those sections.

*The DI section's pacing pressure exposes habits brutally*. A 45-minute section with 20+ questions including MSR sets is unforgiving. Habits that work in untimed practice fall apart under pressure. Elite solvers have automated their habits to the point of immunity; weak solvers revert to gut-based decisions when the timer presses.

## Mental model

Picture an elite DI solver as a *senior analyst handling a fast-moving deal*. The data is more than they can fully process. The deadline is real. Decisions need to be made on incomplete information. The analyst's skill: *organize, triage, decide, move*. Not perfect comprehension. Not exhaustive analysis. Sufficient understanding, applied to the right decision, in the right time.

A middle DI scorer is an analyst trying to fully understand every piece of data before committing to any decision. By the time they've read everything, the deadline has passed.

The senior-analyst frame carries three reminders. First, *not all data is relevant to any single question* — figure out what is, ignore what isn't. Second, *make a decision on incomplete information* if necessary — don't grind for completeness. Third, *learn from every decision* — the review loop is what makes the next deal go better.

## GMAT recognition signals

Five habits separate elite DI performance from very good DI performance.

*Organizing information without memorizing it*. Elite solvers build a *source map* on first encounter — knowing which tab has which kind of data — without trying to retain the data itself. Middle scorers either try to memorize (and overload working memory) or fail to build the map (and re-orient on every question). The map is the first investment; it pays back across all the questions in a set.

*Choosing what not to read*. The data set is more than the question requires. Elite solvers identify the relevant subset in 5-10 seconds and process only that. Middle scorers process everything to be safe, then run out of time.

*Reasoning under ambiguity*. Some DI questions have less-than-perfect data — projections, estimates, ranges. Elite solvers commit to defensible answers based on what the data actually shows; middle scorers freeze when the data isn't crystalline.

*Transferring section habits efficiently*. Elite solvers deploy Quant habits (method selection, arithmetic precision) on quantitative DI problems and Verbal habits (structural reading, pre-thinking) on text-heavy DI problems — without confusion about which to use when. Middle scorers either compartmentalize (treating DI as a third section with its own untrained habits) or over-rotate to one set of habits regardless of fit.

*Reviewing DI mistakes by pattern*. Elite solvers tag DI mistakes by trap pattern (Chapter 3.9) and identify dominant patterns. Middle scorers redo wrong problems without analyzing the underlying error mode.

## Method

A four-step daily DI protocol.

*Step 1. Mix formats during practice.* Don't drill MSR for an hour, then Tables for an hour. Mix formats the way the actual section presents them. Interleaving builds the cross-format flexibility that the section rewards.

*Step 2. Track time per question, not just per session.* Note which formats and which question types eat your time. Banking time is a deliberate skill; it doesn't develop automatically.

*Step 3. End every drill block with pattern review.* After every twenty DI questions, re-classify wrong answers by trap pattern. Identify the dominant pattern and target the next session at it.

*Step 4. Practice walk-away on every drill set.* Set a 2:30 timer per single-question problem and a 4:00 timer per MSR sub-question. If the timer fires and you don't have a path, mark a guess and move. Train the walk-away muscle so it works under exam pressure.

## Common traps

Five meta-traps in DI training.

*Treating DI as "Quant + Verbal."* Assuming your Quant and Verbal prep covers DI. It partially does, but DI's specific habits (triage, source mapping, format-specific workflows) need targeted training. Fix: dedicate at least 30% of prep time to DI specifically.

*Avoiding MSR practice.* MSR is the hardest DI format and the one most students avoid because it's painful. Avoidance is exactly the wrong response — MSR rewards practice more than any other DI format. Fix: drill MSR until you no longer dread it.

*Practicing untimed.* DI's pacing pressure is essential. Untimed accuracy doesn't predict timed accuracy. Train under timed conditions from week one.

*Not reviewing wrong answers deeply enough.* "I missed it because I didn't read carefully" is not a useful diagnosis. "I missed it because I confused units between tabs" is. Force precision in the diagnosis.

*Skipping the trap inventory.* The named-trap discipline (Chapter 3.9) is the highest-leverage trap-recognition tool, and most students skip it because it feels mechanical. Fix: use it on every wrong answer until it becomes automatic.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating elite DI thinking on a fast MSR sub-question.

*Hypothetical scenario.* You're at minute 30 of a 45-minute DI section. Two MSR sets remain (each with 4 sub-questions) and three Two-Part questions. About 15 minutes for 11 questions = 82 seconds per question average. Tight.

*The MSR set in front of you.* Three tabs: a description, a cost-data table, and a chart of monthly orders.

*Sub-question.* "Based on the data, in which month did per-order revenue exceed $100?"

*The triage scan (10 seconds).* The question asks about per-order revenue (revenue divided by orders). The cost-data table has revenue figures by month. The chart has orders by month. Both tabs are needed.

*The targeted reads (40 seconds).* From the cost-data table, monthly revenues. From the chart, monthly order counts. Compute revenue/orders for each month. The threshold is $100.

*The analysis (15 seconds).* Looking at the data, Month 5 has revenue ~$120K and orders ~1,000 — so per-order = $120. Month 3 has revenue ~$80K and orders ~1,200 — per-order = $67. Month 8 has revenue ~$200K and orders ~1,500 — per-order = $133. Months 5 and 8 exceed $100.

*Sub-question phrasing.* "In which month" — singular. So the answer is one month, not multiple. Re-read for which one. Probably it's the only one above $100, or there's a constraint (e.g., "the most recent month") I missed.

*Re-read.* Yeah, I need to be more careful. Looking again, the question says "Based on the data, in which month did per-order revenue *first* exceed $100?" — i.e., the earliest month. Month 5 is earlier than Month 8. Answer: Month 5.

*Sanity check.* Month 5 per-order = $120 > $100. Earlier months don't exceed. Confirmed.

*Time spent.* Total: about 75 seconds. Under the 82-second budget.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap is to skip the careful re-read of the question phrasing and pick "Month 8" because it has the highest per-order revenue. The question asked for the *first* month, not the *highest*. Reading carefully matters.

The other trap is to grind on whether the chart values are precisely 1,000 or 1,200 (estimation works fine here — the answer doesn't depend on that precision). Don't over-precise on Graphics; reason at the precision the chart supports.

This is what elite DI looks like under pressure. Triage, targeted read, careful re-read of question wording, sanity check, commit. About 75 seconds for a problem that less-trained solvers might spend 3 minutes on. The compounding across the section is the score.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Organizing without memorizing

*Setup.* You start an MSR set with three tabs. You spend 90 seconds walking each tab, noting what kind of resource is on each. You don't try to remember specific values.

*Thinking process.* For each tab, ask: "What kind of question would this tab anchor?" Tab 1 (description) → context questions. Tab 2 (cost data) → cost questions. Tab 3 (chart) → trend questions.

*Solution.* You build a *mental index* of the data without trying to memorize it. When sub-questions arrive, you go directly to the right tab.

*Common mistake.* Trying to memorize specific numbers from the data on first pass. By question 3, you've forgotten most of them anyway, and you'll have to re-read.

*Takeaway.* The first tab walk is a cataloging exercise, not a reading exercise. Catalog → return-on-demand is faster than read-and-retain.

### Example 2 — Choosing what not to read

*Setup.* A Table Analysis problem has 30 rows and 7 columns. The first statement asks about a relationship between two specific columns.

*Thinking process.* Only those two columns matter for this statement. The other 5 columns are irrelevant. Sort by one of the relevant columns; ignore everything else.

*Solution.* Focus on the relevant subset. Process only what the statement requires.

*Common mistake.* Reading all 7 columns to "fully understand the data" before answering. Costs 60+ seconds with no information value for the specific statement.

*Takeaway.* Comprehensiveness is a trap when the question is narrow. Strong solvers read selectively.

### Example 3 — Reasoning under ambiguity

*Setup.* An MSR sub-question asks about a future projection based on past data. The data shows 3 years of growth; the question asks about year 4.

*Thinking process.* The answer requires extrapolation, which is inherently uncertain. Pick the answer that *most defensibly* follows from the trend, not the answer that requires zero uncertainty.

*Solution.* Commit to the most-defensible answer. Move on.

*Common mistake.* Freezing because the data doesn't give a definite answer. The answer is rarely going to be definite on projection questions; the right answer is "most likely given the trend," not "certain."

*Takeaway.* Real DI questions sometimes have inherent ambiguity. The discipline is to commit to the most-defensible answer based on the available data, not to refuse to commit.
