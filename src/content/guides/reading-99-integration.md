# Final Integration — From Knowledge to Score

## Core idea

The previous twenty-eight chapters covered three sections in detail. This chapter closes the curriculum by *integrating* them — showing how the three sections share underlying habits, how interleaved study compounds those habits faster than blocked study, how the mistake-review loop converts errors into score points, and how to make the final transition from "I understand this material" to "I execute this under exam conditions." Knowledge is necessary. Execution is what scores.

The mindset shift in this chapter: *the test is one test, not three sections in a row*. The habits that decide your Quant score also decide your Verbal score and your DI score. Train them as one set of habits across one integrated practice cadence, and the gains compound. Train them as three separate skill silos, and the gains stay siloed.

## Why integration matters

Three reasons the integration chapter matters more than any single section chapter.

*Cross-section transfer is the highest-leverage compounding effect in prep*. A student who improves their *pre-think before evaluating* habit by 20% on Verbal will see 5-10% gains on DI Two-Part Analysis as a side effect, because Two-Part rewards the same habit. Section-specific drilling produces section-specific gains; integrated drilling produces section-spanning gains.

*The exam tests endurance, not just skill*. Three sections in a row — about 2 hours and 15 minutes total — exhausts working memory. A solver who has automated the right habits arrives at section 3 with bandwidth to think. A solver who is consciously deploying habits in section 1 has nothing left by section 3. The integration cadence is what builds the endurance.

*Knowledge-to-execution is a distinct phase*. After you've read every chapter and drilled the content, there's still a gap to close: turning the knowledge into reflexive habit under exam conditions. This chapter is about closing that gap.

## The cross-section habit map

Three habits decide scores across all three sections. Train them as one habit each, not as three section-specific versions.

*Pre-think before executing*. In Quant, this is the method-selection scan before you start computing (Chapter 1.1). In Verbal, this is the right-answer-shape pre-think before evaluating choices (Chapter 2.1). In DI, this is the question-first-then-data triage (Chapter 3.1). The terrains differ; the habit is one. *Stop. Plan. Then execute.*

*Eliminate against named patterns*. In Quant, this is identifying which method dominates and discarding the others (Chapter 1.8). In Verbal, this is recognizing trap patterns and rejecting them (Chapter 2.7). In DI, this is the named-trap inventory (Chapter 3.9). Each section has its own pattern vocabulary. The discipline is the same: *name the pattern; eliminate accordingly*.

*Walk away from the wrong fights*. In Quant, this is the thirty-second checkpoint and the willingness to switch methods (Chapter 1.10). In Verbal, this is committing to your best answer instead of grinding the comparison (Chapter 2.8). In DI, this is the 2:30 walk-away threshold (Chapter 3.8). Each section has its own pacing pressure. The discipline is one: *protect the section, not the question*.

The cross-section habits are the meta-skills the test rewards. Section-specific content is necessary; cross-section habits are what produce 90th-plus performance.

## How to interleave study sessions

Interleaved practice — mixing problem types across sections within a single session — produces stronger transfer than blocked practice (drilling one type for an hour, then switching). The reason: under exam conditions, you have to switch every two minutes. Training under blocked conditions doesn't prepare you for that.

### A concrete weekly template

Use this for the final six to eight weeks before the exam. Adjust block lengths to your available hours, but preserve the *structure* — the cadence is what builds the habits.

*Monday — Mixed timed drilling (60 minutes).*
- 5 min: Glance at error log to set the day's trap-watch ("today I'm watching for percent-base confusion and reverse-direction CR traps").
- 50 min: Run 25-30 mixed-section questions under tight time. Use the platform's randomized-mix mode if available; otherwise rotate sections every 5 questions to force interleaving.
- 5 min: Tag every wrong answer with both an error-log tag (Conceptual / Careless / Time pressure / Misread / Strategy / Other) and a trap-pattern tag from the relevant chapter (Quant Ch 1.9, Verbal Ch 2.7, DI Ch 3.9).

*Tuesday — Targeted single-pattern drill (45 minutes).*
- Pick the one trap pattern your error log shows as dominant from the past week (or, if early in prep, from your last mock).
- Drill 15-20 problems specifically on that pattern. Don't shuffle; the point of this session is *concentrated* exposure to one trap.
- Review *all* of them, right or wrong, using the trap-recognition vocabulary from the relevant chapter.

*Wednesday — Mixed timed drilling (60 minutes).* Same structure as Monday.

*Thursday — Targeted single-pattern drill OR concept re-read (45 minutes).*
- If your dominant pattern from Monday/Wednesday's drilling is still surfacing errors, drill it again.
- If you've made progress on the pattern, switch to *re-reading* the relevant chapter and writing your own active-recall questions on it. The reading is itself a study activity in this curriculum's design.

*Friday — Mixed timed drilling (60 minutes).* Same structure as Monday/Wednesday. By end of week, you should have ~80 timed problems across the week.

*Saturday — Full-length section or full mock (90-150 minutes plus review).*
- Every other Saturday: run a full three-section mock (135 minutes plus break). Other Saturdays: run one full section (45 minutes) under exam conditions.
- *Same time of day as your actual exam.* The brain's performance varies by time of day; train at the time you'll test at.
- Review the mock the same day if possible (Saturday afternoon), using the mock-review protocol below.

*Sunday — Review and metacognition day (60-90 minutes, no new drilling).*
- Re-tag the week's error-log entries by trap pattern. Look across them: is one pattern dominating?
- Re-read one chapter surfaced by the week's mistakes — the chapter most relevant to the dominant pattern.
- Set the *single dominant target* for next week. Write it down. Next Monday's session will scan for it.

This cadence builds endurance through the Saturday mocks, builds cross-section transfer through the Mon/Wed/Fri mixed drilling, builds targeted improvement through the Tue/Thu single-pattern work, and builds metacognition through the Sunday review.

### A 12-week roadmap

For a student moving from a baseline diagnostic to an exam date 12 weeks out, here is a default structure. Adjust to your starting score and target.

*Weeks 1-2 — Diagnostic and curriculum first read.* Take a baseline mock in week 1 to establish your starting score and dominant patterns. Read the curriculum end to end across these two weeks, in chapter order, without drilling problems yet. The point is to load the mental model first.

*Weeks 3-5 — Targeted weakness drilling.* Identify the largest gap from the diagnostic (often a content gap in one Quant area, or a CR question type, or DI Multi-Source Reasoning). Drill it for three weeks under the targeted-single-pattern format. Do not move on until your error log shows the pattern's frequency dropping.

*Week 6 — Mid-prep mock + re-diagnosis.* Take a second full mock. Compare to the baseline. Re-diagnose the dominant pattern; it has usually shifted by this point.

*Weeks 7-10 — Mixed drilling with targeted micro-fixes.* Switch to the weekly cadence above. Mon/Wed/Fri mixed; Tue/Thu targeted on the new dominant pattern; Saturday section or mock; Sunday review. Each week, re-diagnose the dominant pattern.

*Week 11 — Final polish + exam-condition mocks.* Two mocks this week, both at the exam's time of day, both reviewed thoroughly. Identify any remaining habit-execution failures and run targeted drills.

*Week 12 — Light maintenance + rest.* Drop drill volume sharply. Re-read the *Mindset* chapters (1.1, 2.1, 3.1) and the integration chapter (this one). Take one final mock four to five days before the exam to confirm pacing. The two days before the exam: no drilling, no mocks. Rest is part of the protocol.

## How to review mistakes

Every chapter introduced its own review protocol; this section pulls them together into a single integrated mistake-review loop.

*Step 1. Tag the mistake correctly.* Use the six-tag taxonomy (Conceptual, Careless, Time pressure, Misread, Strategy, Other) plus the section-specific trap pattern (Quant error patterns from Chapter 1.9, Verbal traps from Chapter 2.7, DI traps from Chapter 3.9). The tag drives the fix.

*Step 2. Identify the break point.* Use the five process steps (read, plan, translate, compute, check). Which step broke? Be specific.

*Step 3. Apply the matching fix.*
- Conceptual gap → re-read the relevant chapter.
- Careless error → drill arithmetic precision.
- Time-pressure error → drill skip discipline.
- Misread → drill two-pass reading.
- Strategy error → re-read the method-selection chapter for that section.
- Other → analyze individually.

*Step 4. Spaced retrieval on the mistake.* Three days later, ask yourself what was wrong with your original approach and why the right approach was right. Seven days later, ask yourself how you'd recognize this trap on a fresh problem. Fourteen days later, do a similar problem cold. The mistake earns its keep when it has paid back five problems' worth of improvement on similar future problems.

This loop is what turns each mistake into a multiplier. A mistake that produces zero improvement on future problems was wasted. A mistake that produces five problems' worth of improvement is the highest-leverage study activity available.

## The mock-review protocol

Mocks are the single most valuable assessment tool in prep. They are also the most often wasted — students take a mock, glance at the score, redo a few wrong problems, and move on. The full value of a mock comes from *systematic review*. Use this protocol on every mock.

*Within 24 hours of finishing the mock:*

*Step 1. Capture timing data per question (10-15 minutes).* For each section, note which questions you spent over 2:30 on (Quant), over 2:00 on (Verbal), or over 2:30 on (DI). These are your pacing leaks. Some will be questions you got right but slowly — those are still pacing leaks because the time bled to them cost you on later questions.

*Step 2. Tag every wrong answer (30-45 minutes).* Use the full taxonomy: error-log tag (Conceptual / Careless / Time pressure / Misread / Strategy / Other) plus the relevant chapter's trap pattern (Quant Ch 1.9, Verbal Ch 2.7, DI Ch 3.9). Be honest. Do not over-tag Careless to avoid admitting Strategy.

*Step 3. Identify the dominant pattern across the mock (5-10 minutes).* What tag appears most? What trap pattern recurs? What section had the most pacing leaks? The dominant pattern is your next target.

*Step 4. Re-read the chapter that addresses the dominant pattern (20-30 minutes).* Don't redo problems yet. The chapter is the lesson; the redo is the practice. Read first.

*Within the next 3-5 days:*

*Step 5. Targeted drill on the dominant pattern (3-5 sessions).* Drill 30-50 fresh problems specifically on the pattern. Tag them with the same taxonomy. Watch whether the pattern's frequency drops.

*Step 6. Cold redo of the wrong mock problems (1 session).* On a date 7+ days after the mock, attempt the wrong problems again, cold. Your accuracy on the redo measures whether the targeted drilling actually closed the gap — not just whether you can re-do the problem with the answer fresh in mind.

*Step 7. Compare your dominant pattern to the previous mock's (10 minutes).* Has the pattern shifted? Did you close the prior dominant pattern? The shift is the diagnostic for whether your targeted work is producing transfer or not.

This protocol takes about 4-6 hours of focused work per mock, spread across a week. Most students spend 30 minutes reviewing a mock and wonder why their next mock score didn't move. The review *is* the score-improvement work.

## Building the score improvement loop

The full improvement loop has six stages, each compounding into the next.

*Stage 1 — Diagnostic.* Take a baseline mock. Identify your starting score and your dominant patterns by section.

*Stage 2 — Read the curriculum.* Run through the twenty-nine chapters in the order they're written. Don't drill yet; build the mental model.

*Stage 3 — Targeted drilling.* Drill the dominant weakness identified in the diagnostic. Two to three weeks of focused work on the single largest gap.

*Stage 4 — Mid-prep mock.* Take another full-length mock. Re-diagnose. The dominant pattern has usually shifted by this point.

*Stage 5 — Mixed drilling + targeted micro-fixes.* Combine interleaved practice with targeted work on the new dominant pattern. The interleaving builds endurance and transfer; the targeting closes specific gaps.

*Stage 6 — Final mocks + final review.* In the last two to three weeks before the exam, take two to three full-length mocks under realistic conditions (same time of day, same break structure, same chair). Review each mock carefully. The final phase is about *consolidation*, not new content.

This loop typically takes ten to fifteen weeks for a student moving from a 600-level baseline to a 700-plus target. Less time and less score gain go together; more time produces diminishing returns past about twenty weeks.

## From knowledge to execution

The hardest phase of prep is the last one: turning everything you've learned into reflexive habit under exam pressure. This phase requires *deliberate practice with metacognition*, not just more drilling.

### Five execution-phase habits

*Measure habit-execution separately from accuracy.* On every drill problem, score yourself on whether you ran the protocol — pre-thought, named the trap, walked away on time, sanity-checked — independent of whether you got the answer right. Habit-execution is the *leading indicator*; accuracy is the lagging indicator. If your habit-execution rate is rising while your accuracy is flat, give it another two weeks; accuracy will follow. If both are flat, the habits aren't yet engaging — diagnose which step you're skipping.

*Practice under fatigue.* The exam runs for 2 hours and 15 minutes. Train under similar fatigue conditions occasionally — drill at the end of a long day, or during the exam's actual time of day if you can arrange it. Habits that work fresh sometimes break down tired; better to discover which ones in practice than on test day.

*Visualize the section before you take it.* Before sitting down for a Quant section, mentally run the protocol: scan answer choices, pre-think method, run thirty-second checkpoint, walk away on hard problems. Two minutes of mental rehearsal primes the habits and reduces cognitive load when the section starts. The visualization is a recall checkpoint — you're retrieving the protocol from memory, which strengthens it.

*Trust the protocol on hard problems.* The temptation when a problem feels hard is to abandon the protocol and "just figure it out." This is exactly backwards. Hard problems need *more* protocol, not less. Slow down deliberately and run the full procedure with extra care. The students who break under hard problems are the ones who default to gut-based solving when the protocol becomes uncomfortable.

*Commit and move.* Once you've made a decision, commit. Don't second-guess mid-section. Doubt is more expensive than wrong answers because doubt costs you the next problem too.

### Self-questioning prompts for metacognition

The following prompts, asked at specific moments during practice, build the metacognitive habits that distinguish top-decile execution. Use them deliberately during drill sessions.

*Before each problem* (5 seconds):
- *What kind of problem is this?* (Quant subtype, CR question type, RC question type, DI format.)
- *Which method will I use, and why?* (Be explicit about the method choice.)
- *What's the trap I'm watching for?* (Specific to the type identified.)

*During each problem* (silent self-check at ~30 seconds in):
- *Am I making progress?* (If no, switch methods.)
- *Did I check the constraints?* (Number-property constraints, RC-attribution markers, DI units.)

*After each problem* (5 seconds):
- *Did the answer match what I pre-thought?* (If not, what surprised me?)
- *What habit, if any, did I skip?* (Pre-think? Trap-naming? Sanity check?)

*At end of session* (5 minutes):
- *What was my dominant trap pattern across these problems?*
- *Which habit had the lowest execution rate?*
- *What's the single thing I want to do differently next session?*

This level of metacognition feels onerous at first. Within two weeks of practice it compresses to seconds per question and becomes invisible. The compression is the goal — at the end, you're running the metacognition reflexively while solving.

### The execution gap

These execution-phase habits are what produce the gap between students who score what they're capable of and students who score below their potential. Knowledge is roughly equal across serious test-takers in the same prep tier; *execution is what distinguishes the top decile*. The students who get the score they expected to get all run something like the above protocols. The students who underperform their practice scores are usually the ones whose habits collapse under exam-day pressure — for predictable reasons: they didn't train fatigue, didn't measure habit-execution, didn't visualize, broke protocol on hard problems, or second-guessed mid-section.

Train against each of those failure modes deliberately, and execution-day performance tends to match practice-day performance.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Cross-section habit transfer

*Setup.* You spent the past two weeks drilling Quant method selection — explicitly naming the method before each problem. Your Quant accuracy and pacing both improved noticeably. This week you start drilling DI Multi-Source Reasoning sets.

*Thinking process.* The method-selection habit transfers directly. Before reading any tab on an MSR set, you scan the question stem and identify which tab to consult. The vocabulary differs ("method scan" → "tab walk") but the discipline is identical: plan before executing.

*Solution.* MSR scores rise faster than they would without the Quant habit base. The cross-section transfer is real and measurable.

*Common mistake.* Treating each section's habits as separate skills, requiring fresh drilling. Many habits transfer; recognizing the transfer accelerates progress.

*Takeaway.* When you train a meta-habit (plan before execute) in one section, it pays dividends in others. Notice when you're applying a Quant-trained habit to a Verbal or DI problem; the noticing reinforces the transfer.

### Example 2 — Mock review that compounds

*Setup.* You take a full mock and score 660. You spent 30 minutes reviewing — looked at wrong answers, redid a couple, moved on. Your next mock is in two weeks.

*Thinking process.* The 30-minute review extracted minimal value. Compare to: spend 4-6 hours over the next week on systematic review (tag every wrong answer by error pattern, identify the dominant pattern, drill that pattern with 30+ fresh problems, redo the wrong mock problems cold a week later). Same mock, different review intensity.

*Solution.* The 4-6 hour review produces meaningful score movement on the next mock; the 30-minute review produces little.

*Common mistake.* Treating mocks as score-checks rather than diagnostic tools. The score is one number; the *diagnostic* is dozens of data points about your dominant patterns.

*Takeaway.* Mocks earn their keep through the review process, not through the score. A 30-minute review wastes most of the mock's value.

### Example 3 — Knowledge-execution gap

*Setup.* You can solve 90% of Quant problems correctly when given unlimited time. Under timed conditions, your accuracy drops to 70%.

*Thinking process.* The gap is *not* a content gap. You know the math. The gap is in *execution under pressure* — habits don't deploy reflexively, method selection slows, sanity checks get skipped. The fix isn't more content study; it's habit-execution practice under timed conditions.

*Solution.* Switch from untimed drilling to timed sets where you score yourself on habit-execution separately from accuracy. Habit-execution is the leading indicator; accuracy follows.

*Common mistake.* Treating the timed-vs-untimed gap as a knowledge gap and re-studying content. Content study doesn't close execution gaps.

*Takeaway.* Diagnose the gap honestly. Knowledge gaps need content review; execution gaps need timed-condition practice. The right diagnosis routes to the right remedy.
