# Chapter 3.4 — Graphics Interpretation

## Core idea

Graphics Interpretation tests *visual literacy under time pressure*. The format gives you a chart or graph — line, bar, scatter, pie, sometimes more exotic — and asks one or two drop-down completion questions about the visual. The skill is *reading the visual carefully*: noting axes, scales, units, trends, and the precision the visual actually supports. Many graphics include subtle visual misleads — non-zero baselines, irregular axis spacing, doubled-up units — that the test deliberately deploys to catch students who eyeball the chart instead of reading it carefully.

The mindset shift in this chapter: *axes before content*. Before you reason about what the chart shows, read what the axes are measuring, what units they use, and what scale they're on. Skipping this step is the single most expensive Graphics error.

## Why it matters

Three reasons Graphics deserves dedicated treatment.

*Visual misleads are common*. A non-zero y-axis can make a 5% difference look like a 200% difference. A logarithmic scale can compress huge differences to look small. A bar chart with broken axes looks dramatic but represents tiny changes. Strong solvers spot these in the first few seconds; weak solvers reason from the visual impression and miss.

*The drop-down completion format compresses elimination*. Each drop-down has typically 4-6 options. Many of the options are clearly wrong if you've read the axes. Eliminating fast leaves a small set of plausible answers; the right one is usually obvious among them.

*Graphics questions reward precision*. The chart shows a value that looks like 47, but the y-axis ticks are at 0, 25, 50. The chart actually shows about 47, give or take 3. A student who reports "47.3" is over-precise; the chart didn't support that. A student who reports "between 45 and 50" is appropriately precise.

## Mental model

Picture every chart as a *measurement device with a specified resolution*. The axes tell you the resolution — what intervals the chart can distinguish. The data tells you the values to that resolution. Don't claim more precision than the chart supports. Don't ignore the axis structure.

The mental model carries three reminders. First, *read the labels and units before reading the data*. Second, *check the scale for irregularities* — non-zero baselines, log scales, broken axes. Third, *report values to the precision the chart supports*, no more.

## GMAT recognition signals

Five recurring chart types in Graphics Interpretation.

*Line chart.* Time series or continuous data. Reads: trends, slopes, peaks, intersections between lines, rate of change. Watch for: the y-axis baseline (zero or non-zero?), the time interval (consistent or irregular?).

*Bar chart.* Categorical data. Reads: comparisons across categories, totals, ratios. Watch for: stacked bars (showing parts within wholes), grouped bars (comparing categories side by side), and the y-axis scale.

*Scatter plot.* Two-variable data. Reads: correlation strength, outliers, clusters, slope of the trend. Watch for: the axes (which variable is X, which is Y?), the scale (linear or log?), and outliers.

*Pie chart.* Parts of a whole. Reads: each segment's share, comparisons of sizes. Watch for: whether the segments sum to 100% (sometimes they don't), and the labels (percentages or absolute numbers?).

*Stacked area or stacked bar.* Components over time or across categories. Reads: components, totals, changes in component shares. Watch for: which component is on top vs bottom, and how the stack reads (additive or relative?).

## Method

A four-step Graphics Interpretation protocol.

*Step 1. Read the title, axes, and units.* What is the chart measuring? What are the units on each axis? What's the time period or category breakdown? Five to ten seconds.

*Step 2. Note any visual irregularities.* Non-zero baselines? Log scales? Broken axes? Different scales on dual y-axes? These can dramatically change interpretation.

*Step 3. Read the question and identify the relevant data point or trend.* The question usually targets one specific feature of the chart: a value at a point, a slope between two points, a comparison between categories, a peak or trough.

*Step 4. Read the value or trend to appropriate precision.* If the y-axis ticks are at 0, 25, 50, you can read to about ±3. Don't claim ±0.5 precision the chart doesn't support.

## Common traps

Six recurring Graphics traps.

*Eyeballing without reading the axes.* Reading the visual impression rather than the actual values. A bar chart with a non-zero baseline can make small differences look enormous. Fix: read the y-axis baseline first.

*Confusing percent and percentage points.* "Increased from 30% to 40%" is a 10 *percentage point* increase, not a 10% increase. The 10% increase would be 30% to 33%. Different. Fix: distinguish the two carefully.

*Misreading log scales.* Logarithmic axes compress large values. Two bars that look "twice as tall" might represent values that are ten times apart. Fix: spot log scales early (the axis ticks are typically 1, 10, 100, 1000) and reason multiplicatively, not additively.

*Reading dual-axis charts as if both lines use the same scale.* Some charts have two y-axes — one on the left, one on the right — for two different metrics. Treating both lines as on the same scale produces wrong comparisons. Fix: check whether each line is referenced to the left or right axis.

*Overprecise reading.* Stating values to a precision the chart doesn't support. Fix: report to the precision of the axis ticks.

*Misreading the time interval.* Bar charts often show categories that look like time but aren't (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 — not necessarily 4 equal time periods if they're labeled differently). Fix: read the labels carefully.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating the axes-first protocol.

*Hypothetical chart.* A bar chart showing quarterly revenue for a company over four quarters of 2024. Title: "Quarterly Revenue 2024." Y-axis: "Revenue ($ millions)" with ticks at 95, 100, 105, 110, 115, 120. X-axis: "Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4." Bars at heights: Q1 = 100, Q2 = 105, Q3 = 110, Q4 = 115.

*Question.* "From Q1 to Q4 of 2024, the company's revenue increased by approximately what percentage?"

*Step 1 — Read axes.* Y-axis is revenue in millions, with a *non-zero baseline at 95*. The visual difference between the Q1 bar and the Q4 bar looks dramatic, but the actual difference is from 100 to 115.

*Step 2 — Note irregularity.* Non-zero baseline. The bars look like Q4 is roughly 3-4x as tall as Q1, but that's because the baseline is at 95. Don't be fooled.

*Step 3 — Identify the relevant values.* Q1 = 100, Q4 = 115.

*Step 4 — Compute and report.* Percent increase = (115 - 100) / 100 = 15%. Answer: approximately 15%.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap answer would be something like "300%" — what students who eyeball the chart and see "Q4 bar is roughly 3x the size of Q1 bar" might pick. The bar is taller because of the visual scaling, not because the value is 3x. Reading the axes carefully prevents this.

The other trap is confusing absolute increase with percent increase. The absolute increase is $15M; the percent increase is 15%. They happen to coincide here because the base was $100M, but in general they're different. Trap answers might say "increased by approximately 15 million" (true but not what was asked) or "increased by approximately 15 percentage points" (technically a different concept and not quite right either).

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Non-zero baseline trap

*Setup.* A bar chart shows quarterly revenue: Q1 = 100, Q2 = 105, Q3 = 110, Q4 = 115. The y-axis baseline starts at 95 (not 0).

*Thinking process.* Visually, the Q4 bar appears about twice as tall as the Q1 bar — but the *actual* values differ by only 15%. The non-zero baseline exaggerates the visual difference.

*Solution.* Q4 is 15% greater than Q1 (115/100 = 1.15), not 2x or more.

*Common mistake.* Reading the visual ratio (Q4 looks ~2x Q1) and answering accordingly. The non-zero baseline is engineered to produce this trap.

*Takeaway.* Always check the y-axis baseline before reading a bar chart. Visual size is not value when the baseline isn't zero.

### Example 2 — Log-scale recognition

*Setup.* A line chart shows population growth over a century. The y-axis ticks are at 1M, 10M, 100M, 1B (each tick a 10x increase).

*Thinking process.* The y-axis is logarithmic. A line that visually rises at a constant slope on this chart represents *exponential* growth (multiplicative), not linear (additive).

*Solution.* Read the values multiplicatively. A doubling visual difference might represent 10x or 100x change.

*Common mistake.* Reading the log-scale chart linearly. A line going from "1M tick" to "100M tick" might look like "1M to 2M" if you don't notice the log structure.

*Takeaway.* Logarithmic axes are easy to spot once you know to look — equally-spaced ticks at 1, 10, 100, 1000. Pause and check whenever values span orders of magnitude.

### Example 3 — Dual-axis trap

*Setup.* A chart shows two lines: "Sales (left axis, $ thousands)" rising steeply, and "Customer count (right axis, in thousands)" rising gradually. Right-axis ticks: 50, 60, 70. Left-axis ticks: 100, 200, 300, 400, 500.

*Thinking process.* The two lines use different scales. The "steeper" sales line and the "gentler" customers line aren't directly comparable visually — they have different units and different ranges.

*Solution.* Read each line against its *own* axis. Don't compare the slopes visually.

*Common mistake.* Concluding sales grew "faster" than customers because the line is steeper. The steepness is partly a function of the axis range, not just the data.

*Takeaway.* Dual-axis charts can deceive. Always check which axis each line references, and compute proportional changes against the line's own scale.
