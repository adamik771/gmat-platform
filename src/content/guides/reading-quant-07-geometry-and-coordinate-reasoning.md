# Chapter 1.7 — Geometry and Coordinate Reasoning

## Core idea

Geometry on the GMAT is a *small content surface with a high pattern-recognition reward*. The rules are short — area formulas, the Pythagorean theorem, special-triangle ratios, basic circle properties, line-and-slope relationships in the coordinate plane. The reasoning is mostly visual. The skill is *seeing the special triangle inside a complicated figure*, *recognizing when a circle problem is really a chord-and-radius problem*, *knowing when to convert a geometry problem to algebra by setting up coordinates*. This is the most pattern-driven content area in Quant, which means it's the one where drilling pays back the fastest.

The lever in this chapter is a habit: *always draw, but don't trust the diagram*. The GMAT often provides figures that are not drawn to scale, and figures that distract from the underlying structure. Strong solvers redraw with the constraints respected; weak solvers eyeball the given figure and miss the angle that's actually a right angle but doesn't look like one.

## Why it matters

Three reasons geometry decides scores.

*The content is finite and learnable*. A weekend of focused study covers the entire content surface — area and perimeter formulas, the major special triangles (30-60-90, 45-45-90, 3-4-5), basic circle properties, slope formulas, distance and midpoint formulas. After that, every additional rep is pure pattern recognition.

*The traps are visual, not algebraic*. Most wrong answers on geometry problems come from misreading the figure — assuming a triangle is a right triangle when it isn't, assuming two lines are parallel when the problem doesn't say so, assuming a tangent line touches the circle when it actually crosses. The fix is *never trust the diagram*; always re-derive the geometric relationships from the given conditions, not from how the figure looks.

*Coordinate geometry is often faster than synthetic geometry*. When a geometry problem has more than two specific points or specific dimensions, set up coordinates and convert the problem to algebra. The algebra is usually cleaner than the geometric reasoning, and the coordinate-plane mental model lets you handle slope, distance, and intersection problems uniformly.

## Mental model

Carry two images. *Always draw, but don't trust the diagram* is the first. The second is *every geometry problem is either a triangle problem, a circle problem, a line problem, or a coordinate problem in disguise*. Triangle problems usually reduce to "find the missing side" via Pythagorean theorem or special-triangle ratios. Circle problems usually involve radius, chord, tangent, or the central-angle / inscribed-angle relationship. Line problems usually involve slope, parallel/perpendicular relationships, or distance. Coordinate problems are any of the above, written in (x, y) form.

Practice categorizing every geometry problem you see into one of these four buckets within five seconds of reading it. The categorization tells you which set of rules applies.

## GMAT recognition signals

Geometry problems have explicit visual vocabulary. Recognize the subtype fast.

*Triangle vocabulary.* "Right triangle," "isoceles," "equilateral," "similar triangles," "30-60-90," "45-45-90." If a problem mentions any of these, the special-triangle ratios are in play. 30-60-90: sides 1 : √3 : 2. 45-45-90: sides 1 : 1 : √2. 3-4-5 (and multiples like 6-8-10): one of the most common right triangles on the test.

*Circle vocabulary.* "Radius," "diameter," "chord," "tangent," "arc," "inscribed angle," "central angle." Radius and diameter are basic. The relationships to memorize: chord-radius forms a triangle (the radius bisects a perpendicular chord), tangent is perpendicular to radius at the point of tangency, inscribed angle is half the central angle subtending the same arc.

*Line and slope vocabulary.* "Parallel," "perpendicular," "slope," "y-intercept," "passes through." Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular slopes multiply to −1. The slope of a line through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁).

*Coordinate-plane vocabulary.* "In the xy-plane," "on the coordinate plane," "graph of," "(x, y)." This is your signal to use coordinate methods rather than synthetic geometry. Distance: √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]. Midpoint: average of the coordinates. Equation of a line: y = mx + b.

*Volume and surface area vocabulary.* "Cube," "cylinder," "rectangular solid," "volume," "surface area." These are formula-driven; memorize the half-dozen formulas the GMAT uses (volume of a rectangular solid is l × w × h, volume of a cylinder is πr²h, etc.).

## Method

The four-step universal protocol — draw, mark conditions, identify subtype, apply relationship — is the wrapper. The substance is the *subtype-specific rule set* you apply at Step 4. Each of the five buckets has its own toolkit.

### Triangles

The Pythagorean theorem is your floor. *a² + b² = c²* for any right triangle. Memorize the special triangles so the Pythagorean computation usually isn't needed.

*Special triangles.*
- *45-45-90.* Sides 1 : 1 : √2. The two legs are equal; the hypotenuse is leg × √2.
- *30-60-90.* Sides 1 : √3 : 2. The side opposite 30° is the shortest; the side opposite 90° (hypotenuse) is twice that; the side opposite 60° is shortest × √3.
- *3-4-5 family.* Pythagorean triples whose ratios are integers: 3-4-5, 6-8-10, 9-12-15, 5-12-13, 8-15-17. If you see two sides matching one of these patterns, the third is forced.

*Similar triangles.* Two triangles are *similar* when their angles match (and consequently their side ratios are proportional). Recognition: triangles with shared angles, parallel sides, or shared vertices often produce similar pairs. The proportionality relation: if triangle ABC ~ triangle DEF, then AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF. Most "find the missing length" problems on similar-triangle setups reduce to a single proportion equation.

*Triangle-inequality.* The sum of any two sides exceeds the third. Useful for ruling out impossible triangle dimensions and for "could the third side be X?" questions.

*Triangle area.* Standard: (1/2) × base × height. Heron's formula exists but is rarely the fastest path on the GMAT — drop a perpendicular and use the standard formula instead.

### Circles

The relationships to memorize:

*Radius and chord.* A radius drawn perpendicular to a chord *bisects* the chord. This creates a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the radius, one leg is half the chord, and the other leg is the perpendicular distance from center to chord. Most chord-length problems reduce to a Pythagorean computation in this triangle.

*Tangent and radius.* A tangent line is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency. This produces right angles available for Pythagorean reasoning whenever a tangent is involved.

*Inscribed and central angles.* An inscribed angle is *half* the central angle that subtends the same arc. The central angle's measure equals its arc's measure (in degrees). An inscribed angle subtending a diameter is always 90° — a useful right-angle generator.

*Circle area and circumference.* Area = πr². Circumference = 2πr. Arc length = (θ/360) × 2πr where θ is the central angle in degrees. Sector area = (θ/360) × πr².

*Inscribed shapes.* When a polygon is inscribed in a circle, the polygon's vertices lie on the circle. For a square inscribed in a circle, the diagonal of the square equals the diameter. For an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, side length and radius relate by side = r√3.

### Lines and angles (with parallel lines)

*Slope.* For a line through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂): slope = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁). Vertical lines have undefined slope; horizontal lines have slope 0.

*Parallel and perpendicular.* Parallel lines have *equal* slopes. Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is −1 (each is the negative reciprocal of the other).

*Line equations.* Slope-intercept form: y = mx + b. Point-slope form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁). Standard form: ax + by = c, with slope = −a/b.

*Transversals across parallel lines.* When two parallel lines are crossed by a third line (the transversal), eight angles are formed, and they fall into four pairs of equal angles plus four pairs of supplementary angles. The relationships:
- *Corresponding angles* (same position relative to each parallel line) are equal.
- *Alternate interior angles* (between the two parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal) are equal.
- *Alternate exterior angles* (outside both parallel lines, on opposite sides) are equal.
- *Same-side interior angles* (between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal) are supplementary (sum to 180°).

These rules unlock most parallel-line angle problems. Without them, the problems look unsolvable.

### Coordinate-plane geometry

When a problem places a figure in the coordinate plane, you have algebra at your disposal in addition to synthetic geometry. The conversion is usually worth it.

*Distance between points.* d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]. Pythagorean theorem applied to coordinate differences.

*Midpoint of a segment.* The midpoint of (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2) — averages of the coordinates.

*Equation of a circle.* (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r², where (a, b) is the center and r is the radius.

*Reflection.* Reflecting (x, y) across the y-axis gives (−x, y). Across the x-axis: (x, −y). Across the line y = x: (y, x).

### Volume and surface area (3D solids)

Three solids appear regularly: rectangular solid, cube, cylinder. Two more are rarer but possible: cone and sphere.

*Rectangular solid.* Volume = l × w × h. Surface area = 2(lw + lh + wh). The longest interior diagonal (corner to opposite corner through the interior) has length √(l² + w² + h²), useful for "longest pencil that fits in a box" type questions.

*Cube.* All sides equal s. Volume = s³. Surface area = 6s². Interior diagonal = s√3.

*Cylinder.* Volume = πr²h. Surface area = 2πr² (two circular ends) + 2πrh (curved side) = 2πr(r + h). The curved-side surface area corresponds to "unrolling" the cylinder into a rectangle of dimensions h × 2πr.

*Cone.* Volume = (1/3)πr²h. Surface area is more complex; rarely tested.

*Sphere.* Volume = (4/3)πr³. Surface area = 4πr². Worth memorizing but rarely tested.

*Cross-section reasoning.* When a problem asks about a 3D figure cut by a plane, the cross-section is typically a 2D shape (rectangle, circle, ellipse). Identify the cross-section's dimensions in terms of the original solid's dimensions; then apply 2D area or perimeter formulas.

### Quick check

*Answers.* (1) Corresponding (equal); alternate interior (equal); alternate exterior (equal); same-side interior (supplementary, summing to 180°). (2) πr²h. (3) √(l² + w² + h²). (4) AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF — the ratios of corresponding sides are all equal. (5) (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r².

## Common traps

Six recurring geometry traps.

*Trusting the diagram.* The figure shows what looks like a right angle. The problem doesn't say it is. Your answer assumes it is and is wrong. Fix: only use angle and parallel relationships explicitly stated or explicitly derived.

*Missing the special triangle.* The problem describes a triangle with sides 6, 8, and an unknown third side opposite a right angle. The unknown is 10 — a 3-4-5 multiple. Students who don't recognize the pattern grind through Pythagorean. Fix: memorize the special triangles and scan for them.

*Confusing chord with diameter.* A chord is any segment with both endpoints on the circle. A diameter is the longest chord — through the center. Not every chord is a diameter. Some problems use "chord" when "diameter" would be a stronger constraint; read carefully.

*Forgetting the parallel-line angle relationships.* When two parallel lines are crossed by a transversal, alternate interior angles are equal, corresponding angles are equal, same-side interior angles are supplementary (add to 180°). These rules unlock many problems. Without them, the problem looks unsolvable.

*Misusing the perpendicular-slope rule.* Perpendicular slopes multiply to −1, not to +1 or to 0. The slopes are *negative reciprocals* of each other. A line with slope 2 is perpendicular to a line with slope −1/2.

*Computing area when the question asks for perimeter (or vice versa).* The most embarrassing geometry mistake. Fix: re-read the question after solving.

## Original mini-example

A worked example to demonstrate the coordinate-conversion mindset.

*Problem.* In the xy-plane, line $L$ passes through points $(2, 5)$ and $(6, −3)$. What is the slope of any line perpendicular to $L$?

*Method-selection scan.* "Slope of perpendicular line" — coordinate problem, perpendicular-slope rule applies. Need to find slope of L first.

*Method.* Slope of L = (−3 − 5) / (6 − 2) = −8 / 4 = −2. Perpendicular slope is the negative reciprocal: −1 / (−2) = 1/2.

*Sanity check.* Slope 1/2 is the negative reciprocal of −2. Check: 1/2 × (−2) = −1, satisfying the perpendicular-product rule.

*Trap to avoid.* The two common wrong answers are 2 (forgot the negative) and −1/2 (forgot the reciprocal). The trap is in remembering the rule precisely: *negative reciprocal*, both transformations applied. Fix: write the rule explicitly on scratch — "perpendicular slope = −1 / m" — before computing.

*Time spent.* About twenty seconds. The slope formula is one step; the reciprocal is another. Fast if the rule is memorized.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Special-triangle recognition

*Setup.* A right triangle has legs of length 5 and 12. What is the length of the hypotenuse?

*Thinking process.* 5-12-? is a Pythagorean triple — the third value is 13. (Confirm: 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13².)

*Solution.* Hypotenuse = 13.

*Common mistake.* Computing 5² + 12² and then taking a square root via guessing or longhand. Works, but slower than recognizing the 5-12-13 triple.

*Takeaway.* Memorize the common Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25) and their multiples. Recognition saves the Pythagorean-arithmetic step entirely.

### Example 2 — Inscribed-angle relationship

*Setup.* In a circle, points A and B lie on the circle, and the central angle for arc AB measures 80°. What is the measure of the inscribed angle subtending the same arc?

*Thinking process.* The inscribed-angle theorem: an inscribed angle is *half* the central angle subtending the same arc.

*Solution.* The inscribed angle is 40°.

*Common mistake.* Treating the inscribed angle as equal to the central angle (giving 80°) or as some other relationship (assuming half + 90° = 130° from confusing rules).

*Takeaway.* Inscribed = half of central, when both subtend the same arc. The relationship is one of the most-tested circle properties; memorize it.

### Example 3 — Coordinate distance

*Setup.* In the xy-plane, what is the distance between the points (1, 2) and (4, 6)?

*Thinking process.* Distance formula: d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] = √[(4 − 1)² + (6 − 2)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5.

*Solution.* Distance = 5.

*Common mistake.* Adding the differences instead of squaring them: |4 − 1| + |6 − 2| = 3 + 4 = 7. This is the *taxicab* distance, not the Euclidean distance.

*Takeaway.* Distance in the xy-plane is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the coordinate differences. Memorize the formula — it's just Pythagoras applied to coordinate gaps.
