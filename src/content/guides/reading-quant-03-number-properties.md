# Chapter 1.3 — Number Properties

## Core idea

Number properties is the *highest-leverage content area on the Quant section*. The rules are short — divisibility, primes, factors, multiples, parity (odd/even), sign behavior, and a small set of constraints around integers — and you can write them on a single index card. The reasoning the GMAT builds on top of those rules generates an enormous variety of hard problems. Students who think of number properties as "the easy stuff" often score lowest on it, because the difficulty isn't in the content. It's in the *structural reading* the content rewards.

Treat this chapter as the most important content area in Quant after method selection itself. You will see number-property questions on every Quant section, and they cluster at the difficulty levels where the score table swings hardest — the 600-to-700-point band specifically.

## Why it matters

Three reasons number properties decides scores.

*The content is encyclopedic but small*. You can master it in a focused weekend. The whole rule-set fits on one index card. So once you've put in the content time, every additional reasoning rep on number-property problems compounds at high efficiency. There is no "I just don't know enough rules" plateau here — the rule set is finite and closes fast.

*The traps are concentrated*. Half a dozen recurring traps — divisor confusion, the "2 is the only even prime" gap, divisibility-of-products misreads, missing the integer constraint — generate most of the wrong answers on number-property problems. Memorizing the traps closes most of the gap.

*The reasoning transfers to other Quant areas*. Algebra problems with hidden integer constraints are number-property problems in disguise. Probability problems often hinge on counting integer outcomes that satisfy a divisibility condition. Word problems involving "how many people" or "how many widgets" are integer-constrained problems. Strong number-property reasoning quietly improves your score across the section.

## Mental model

Picture every integer as its *prime factorization*: a product of primes raised to powers. 60 = 2² × 3 × 5. 72 = 2³ × 3². 100 = 2² × 5². Once a number is in this form, almost every divisibility, factor, multiple, and remainder question reduces to a *rule about exponents*. Is 60 divisible by 12? 12 = 2² × 3. 60 has 2² × 3 (and a 5 to spare). Yes. Is 72 divisible by 16? 16 = 2⁴. 72 has only 2³. No. The prime-factorization mental model converts number-property questions from puzzles into mechanical exponent comparisons.

The other half of the mental model is *constraint awareness*. The GMAT loves to drop hidden constraints — "n is a positive integer," "x and y are distinct primes," "k is greater than 1" — that narrow the solution set in ways students forget by the third paragraph of the problem. Whenever you see "integer" in a problem, circle it on scratch. Whenever you see "positive," circle it. Whenever you see "distinct," circle it. The constraint is half the answer.

## GMAT recognition signals

Number-property problems announce themselves with specific vocabulary. Recognize these signals fast and switch into number-property mode immediately.

*"Divisible by," "factor of," "multiple of," "remainder when divided by"* — these are the core vocabulary. If you see any of them, prime-factorize the relevant numbers and reason from the factorization.

*"Prime," "composite," "consecutive integers," "consecutive primes"* — these constrain the solution space sharply. Consecutive integers alternate parity (odd/even/odd…) and contain at least one multiple of every small integer up to their length. Consecutive primes have almost no useful general structure beyond their specific values.

*"Even," "odd," "positive," "negative," "non-negative," "integer"* — sign and parity constraints. These are the most-missed signals because they look like decoration. They are not. Whenever you see "the sum of two even integers," remind yourself: sum is even. Whenever you see "the product of two odds," remind yourself: product is odd. Hardcode the parity rules.

*"Units digit," "last digit," "tens digit"* — usually means a cycle-of-powers question. Powers of small primes cycle in their units digits. 2: 2, 4, 8, 6, 2, 4, 8, 6… (cycle length 4). 3: 3, 9, 7, 1, 3, 9, 7, 1… (cycle length 4). Memorize the cycles for 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9. The cycles for 0, 1, 5, 6 are trivial (always end in the same digit).

So n = 2^(2a) × 5^(2b) × (other even-powered primes), with 2a ≥ 2 and 2b ≥ 2 (i.e., a ≥ 1, b ≥ 1). Smallest such n = 100. *T.* Just listing multiples of 100 and checking squares. *L.* Combining "divisible by X" with "perfect square" gives strong structure on prime exponents.

## Method

A four-step protocol that solves most number-property problems.

*Step 1. Translate the constraint*. Before computing, restate the problem in your own words and circle every constraint. "n is a positive integer" → write "n ≥ 1, n integer" on scratch. "x and y are distinct primes" → write "x ≠ y, both prime."

*Step 2. Prime-factorize*. Whatever number is in play, write its prime factorization. 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5. 24 = 2³ × 3. From here, divisibility and factor questions are exponent comparisons.

*Step 3. Test small cases when reasoning is unclear*. Pick the smallest values that satisfy the constraints. If the problem says "n is a positive integer greater than 1," start with n = 2, then n = 3, then n = 4. Watch for patterns. The smallest cases often reveal the structure that the general statement obscures.

*Step 4. Check the constraint at the end*. Before bubbling, return to the constraints you circled. Did your answer respect them? "n must be a positive integer" — is your answer 4.5? Reject it. The most expensive number-property errors come from solutions that are mathematically valid but constraint-invalid.

## Common traps

Six recurring traps. Memorize them.

*Confusing "divisor of n" with "divides into n".* A divisor of 12 is a number like 4 that 12 is divisible by. 12 does not divide into 4 — 4 divides into 12. The directionality matters and the GMAT exploits the confusion. Read the problem twice on this exact phrasing.

*Forgetting that 1 is not prime, and that 2 is the only even prime.* "How many primes are less than 10" is 2, 3, 5, 7 — four primes. Students who include 1 produce five and miss. Students who treat all primes as odd produce wrong parity arguments.

*Assuming a number divisible by m and n is automatically divisible by mn.* This is true *only when m and n are coprime* (share no factors). 12 is divisible by both 4 and 6, but not by 24 — because 4 and 6 share a factor of 2. The fix: when combining divisibility, prime-factorize and take the *maximum* exponent of each prime.

*Missing the integer constraint.* The problem says "a positive integer." You produce a fractional answer. The fractional answer was technically valid in the math but invalid in the problem. The fix: re-check constraints before bubbling.

*Treating consecutive integers as having no useful structure.* They do. The product of any two consecutive integers is even (one of them must be even). The product of any three consecutive integers is divisible by 6 (one is even, one is a multiple of 3). The sum of n consecutive integers starting at k is n times the average. Memorize.

*Assuming the units digit pattern works for all bases.* It works for small primes after you memorize the cycles. It does not work for arbitrary numbers without first reducing to a small base. 47^100 is hard; 7^100 has units digit 1 (cycle 7-9-3-1, position 100 = 4×25, so cycle ends at 1).

*Easy.*

**1.** Is 1 prime? *A.* No — primes have exactly two distinct positive divisors; 1 has only one. *T.* Including 1 in prime counts. *L.* Memorize: 1 is *not* prime.

**2.** What is the only even prime? *A.* 2. *T.* Treating all primes as odd, or excluding 2 from primes. *L.* 2 is special — every other even is a multiple of 2.

**3.** "Divisor of 12" vs. "12 is a divisor of n" — are these the same? *A.* No. Divisors of 12 = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}. "12 is a divisor of n" means 12 | n. Different directions. *T.* Conflating the two phrasings. *L.* Read divisibility statements for direction.

*Medium.*

**4.** If n is divisible by 4 and 6, is n necessarily divisible by 24? *A.* No. LCM(4, 6) = 12, not 24. Smallest n satisfying both = 12. *T.* Multiplying 4 × 6 = 24. *L.* Coprime-only multiplication works; non-coprime requires LCM.

**5.** A student includes 1 as a prime in counting "primes less than 10." Result? *A.* They get 5 primes (1, 2, 3, 5, 7) instead of 4. *T.* Treating 1 as prime. *L.* The off-by-one error here is the trap; remember: 1 is not prime.

**6.** A student claims "if n is divisible by 6, then n is divisible by 12 because 6 × 2 = 12." Correct? *A.* No. 6 itself is divisible by 6 but not by 12. The reasoning conflates "n divisible by 6" with "2n divisible by 12." *T.* Multiplying both sides of a divisibility claim. *L.* Divisibility doesn't compose by simple multiplication; LCM is the right operation.

*Hard.*

**7.** If n, n+1, and n+2 are three consecutive integers, what can you conclude about their product n(n+1)(n+2)? *A.* Always divisible by 6. Three consecutive integers contain at least one multiple of 2 and at least one multiple of 3 — so the product has 2 and 3 as factors, totaling 6. *T.* Concluding only "divisible by 6" without seeing it's *always* (i.e., for any n). *L.* k consecutive integers always contain a multiple of every integer up to k.

**8.** What is the units digit of 47^48? *A.* Units digit of 47 = 7. Cycle of 7^n: 7, 9, 3, 1 (length 4). 48 mod 4 = 0 → last position in cycle = 1. *T.* Computing 47^48 (impossible) or assuming the cycle goes 7, 9, 3, 1, 0... (no, it has length 4). *L.* Units-digit cycles for small primes: memorize them.

## Original mini-example

A worked example to show how the prime-factorization model collapses a hard-looking problem.

*Problem.* If $n$ is a positive integer and $n!$ ends in exactly seven trailing zeros, what is the greatest possible value of $n$?

*Method-selection scan.* Answer choices (assume A: 30, B: 32, C: 34, D: 35, E: 39) — small integers, close together. The shape is "trailing zeros of a factorial," which means the count of factors of 5 in n! (since trailing zeros come from 10s, and 10 = 2 × 5, with 2s always more abundant than 5s in factorials).

*Method.* The number of trailing zeros in n! equals ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + … For values around 30, n/125 is 0, so we only need ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋. For n = 30: ⌊30/5⌋ + ⌊30/25⌋ = 6 + 1 = 7. For n = 31: 6 + 1 = 7. For n = 32: 6 + 1 = 7. For n = 33: 6 + 1 = 7. For n = 34: 6 + 1 = 7. For n = 35: ⌊35/5⌋ = 7, ⌊35/25⌋ = 1, total = 8. The count jumps from 7 to 8 between 34 and 35. So the greatest n with exactly 7 trailing zeros is 34.

*Trap to avoid.* The trap answer is 35, picked by students who locate the *first* n with 7 trailing zeros instead of the *last*. The problem says "the greatest possible value of n" — that means the last n before the count jumps to 8, not the first n where the count reaches 7. This is a misread trap, not a content trap. The fix is reading the question carefully.

*Time spent.* About forty seconds — most of it in setting up the formula correctly and walking n up by ones from a sensible starting point.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Prime factorization for divisibility

*Setup.* Is 360 divisible by 24?

*Thinking process.* Prime-factorize both. 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5. 24 = 2³ × 3. For 360 to be divisible by 24, 360 must contain at least 2³ and at least 3¹. It contains 2³ (yes) and 3² (more than enough).

*Solution.* Yes — 360/24 = 15.

*Common mistake.* Trying to divide 360 by 24 directly via long division. Works but slower than factor comparison, and prone to arithmetic slips.

*Takeaway.* Once both numbers are in prime-factor form, divisibility reduces to checking that each prime's exponent in the divisor is at most its exponent in the dividend.

### Example 2 — The coprime divisibility chain

*Setup.* If n is a positive integer divisible by both 4 and 6, must n be divisible by 24?

*Thinking process.* 4 = 2² and 6 = 2 × 3 share a factor of 2. So 24 = 4 × 6 / shared factor = 4 × 6 / 2 = 12. The smallest positive integer divisible by both 4 and 6 is 12, not 24.

*Solution.* No. n is divisible by 12, but not necessarily by 24. (Example: n = 12 is divisible by 4 and 6 but not 24.)

*Common mistake.* Multiplying 4 × 6 = 24 and concluding n must be divisible by 24. Works only when the two divisors are *coprime* (share no factors). 4 and 6 share a 2, so the rule fails.

*Takeaway.* When combining divisibility constraints, take the *least common multiple*, not the product. The LCM equals the product divided by the GCD of the two divisors.

### Example 3 — Units-digit cycles

*Setup.* What is the units digit of 7^48?

*Thinking process.* The units digits of powers of 7 cycle: 7, 9, 3, 1, 7, 9, 3, 1, ... (cycle length 4). The position in the cycle is determined by 48 mod 4 = 0, which corresponds to the *last* position in the cycle.

*Solution.* The units digit of 7^48 is 1.

*Common mistake.* Computing 7^48 directly — impossibly large. Or treating the cycle as off by one (concluding the units digit is 7 instead of 1).

*Takeaway.* Units-digit problems on large powers reduce to mod-arithmetic on the cycle length. Memorize the cycles for 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9 — they're the only ones that matter on the test.

## Connection to other skills

Number-property reasoning shows up everywhere. *Algebra and Equations* (Chapter 1.4) frequently asks integer-constraint questions disguised as algebra problems. *Word Problems* (Chapter 1.5) often hides number-property logic inside scenarios — "how many full crates can be loaded" is a divisibility question. *Statistics, Probability, and Combinatorics* (Chapter 1.6) uses divisibility to count favorable outcomes. *Quant Method Selection* (Chapter 1.8) treats prime factorization as a core method to switch to when direct algebra stalls.

The cross-section connection is subtler. Number-property reasoning is about *constraint awareness* — the discipline of noticing what the problem narrows down before computing. That discipline transfers directly to *Critical Reasoning* (Chapter 2.3), where the question is "what must be true given these conditions," and to *Data Sufficiency Logic* (Chapter 3.2), where the question is "do these conditions narrow the answer to a single value." The terrain is different. The skill of reading-for-constraint is the same.
