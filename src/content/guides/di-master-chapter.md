# GMAT Data Insights Master Chapter

## Introduction

Data Insights is the section most students underprepare for, and consequently the section where preparation pays the biggest marginal dividends. Students who spent three months on Quant and Verbal and only a couple of weeks on DI are common. Students who score 90th-plus on DI are not. That gap is almost entirely a preparation gap — and it is the highest-ROI single lever on the test.

I score 735s on this test. My DI score sits at the very top of the distribution, and it got there because I studied DI with the same seriousness I studied Quant and Verbal, not as an afterthought. The formats reward specific habits: sufficiency-style thinking on DS, skim-first-then-question workflow on MSR, axis-before-data reading on Graphics Interpretation, equations-first setup on Two-Part Analysis, sort-based reasoning on Table Analysis. Each habit is learnable. This chapter teaches all of them.

*Read it carefully.* This is the third of the three section-specific master chapters, and it is the one where the payoff per hour of study is typically largest. Twenty-three minutes invested in this chapter, applied over the next 4–6 weeks of targeted DI drilling, is likely to move your DI score more than an equivalent number of minutes spent on Quant or Verbal.

*Use it twice.* Read it end to end once to load the whole model. Come back to specific formats as you drill. The DS section in particular should be re-read before any timed DS practice set during your first four weeks.

Part 1 establishes what DI actually measures and the 30-second scan. Parts 2 through 6 cover the five formats in depth, each with concept, method, traps, worked examples, and recognition cues. Parts 7 through 9 cover pacing, calculator discipline, and the trap compendium. Parts 10 through 12 cover study methodology, harder problems fully worked, and test-day execution.

*Every worked example is my own writing*, invented specifically to teach a point. Nothing is paraphrased from any prep book, official guide, or third-party source. Everything is original.

One foundational note. GMAT Focus Edition's Data Insights section is 20 questions in 45 minutes, with a mix of all five formats scrambled. The on-screen calculator is available but must be used sparingly. There is no partial credit — sub-statements and multi-question sets are all-or-nothing per question. All of these constraints shape the section's strategic approach, and each one is addressed below.

## What Data Insights actually measures

On the surface, DI tests quantitative reasoning, data interpretation, and logical analysis. Underneath, it measures five specific skills, in rough order of score impact above the 75th percentile.

*Sufficiency-style thinking.* The question on DS is never "what is the answer?" — it is "do I have enough information?" This is a different cognitive mode than computation. Students who can't switch into this mode bleed points on DS, which is the largest single format slice of the section. The skill is asking "what would I need to know to answer, and do the statements give me that?" before ever starting to compute.

*Scan-then-extract workflow.* MSR, Table Analysis, and Graphics Interpretation all reward a two-phase workflow: scan the structure first (what tabs, what columns, what axes), then extract specific data points as questions demand. Students who try to read every data point first drown. Students who scan the structure and then jump to questions save two to three minutes per problem.

*Cross-tab integration.* MSR sets have three tabs of information. The questions often require pulling data from two or three tabs simultaneously. The skill is knowing which tab contains which type of information, then cross-referencing efficiently. This is the hardest MSR-specific skill.

*Precise reading of dense stems.* DI question stems are often longer and more specific than Quant or Verbal stems. A Two-Part Analysis stem may define the task in a paragraph. A Graphics Interpretation stem may define a specific calculation. Misreading the stem is the single largest source of DI error.

*Calculator discipline.* The calculator is a scalpel, not a hammer. Reaching for it on every arithmetic step burns 30–60 seconds per problem. Reaching for it only when the numbers genuinely require it preserves the budget.

Notice what is not on the list. Advanced math. Advanced reading. Obscure chart types. Complex probability. The content floor is essentially the same as Quant's — if you have Quant content knowledge, you have DI content knowledge. The gap is in the section-specific reasoning habits.

This has a strategic implication. *DI preparation transfers from Quant preparation, but not the reverse.* A student who masters DS sufficiency-style thinking will find parts of Quant easier (the conceptual-insight method benefits). But a student who masters Quant without touching DI will still be lost on MSR, TA, GI, and TPA, because those formats have nothing parallel on Quant.

Plan your time accordingly. If you have scored 80th percentile on Quant in practice, you are nowhere near 80th on DI unless you have specifically drilled the five formats.

## The 30-second DI scan

Before you compute anything, spend 15–30 seconds reading the stem, identifying the format, and picking your method. DI has format-specific scans; a wrong scan wastes a full problem.

*What format is this?* Data Sufficiency, Multi-Source Reasoning, Table Analysis, Graphics Interpretation, or Two-Part Analysis. The visual layout tells you immediately. DS has two numbered statements. MSR has three tabs. TA has a sortable table with sub-statements. GI has a chart or graph. TPA has a two-column task with 5–7 row options.

*What is the question actually asking?* DS is always asking "is the given information sufficient?" — not "what is the answer?" Read the stem until you know exactly what you are being asked to determine. TA questions often have multiple Yes/No sub-statements that are separately evaluated; you must get all of them right to earn the point. TPA questions ask you to select exactly one row for each of two columns; both selections must be right.

*What data do I need?* For MSR, identify which tab likely has the relevant data before you start searching. For TA, decide whether sorting by a specific column would help before you start reading rows. For GI, identify the axes and the units before you try to read a specific point.

*What is the time expectation?* A simple DS problem should be 90–120 seconds. A hard DS problem could be 2:30. An MSR set's first question often takes 3+ minutes because you're also reading the tabs; the second and third questions off the same set should be 60–90 seconds each. A TA problem with three sub-statements might be 2:30. A GI is typically 90 seconds. A TPA is typically 2:00–2:30.

Run this scan on every problem. The DI format mixing means you cannot run the same method on question after question — each type demands a format-specific approach, and the scan is where you select it.

## Part 1: Data Sufficiency — the logic deep dive

Data Sufficiency is the highest-impact DI format because it has the largest slice of the section (roughly 25–30% of DI questions) and because it is the most learnable. The answer choices are the same on every DS question. The reasoning style is consistent. Master DS, and you've unlocked a third of the section.

### The five answer choices, memorized cold

Every DS question has the same five choices:

- **(A)** Statement (1) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (2) alone is not sufficient.
- **(B)** Statement (2) ALONE is sufficient, but statement (1) alone is not sufficient.
- **(C)** BOTH statements TOGETHER are sufficient, but NEITHER statement ALONE is sufficient.
- **(D)** EACH statement ALONE is sufficient.
- **(E)** Statements (1) and (2) TOGETHER are NOT sufficient.

You should be able to recite these in your sleep. On test day, you should not be reading them. If you are, you are losing 15–20 seconds per DS question.

### What "sufficient" really means

Sufficient means the information determines exactly one answer to the question being asked. For a "value" question ("what is x?"), sufficient means the information forces a unique value of x. For a "yes/no" question ("is x > 0?"), sufficient means the information forces either a definite yes or a definite no — not "maybe yes, maybe no."

This definition has a specific implication that students miss. A statement that produces two possible values for a value question is *not* sufficient, even if one of the values is the "right" one in some intuitive sense. If x could be 3 or −3, that statement alone is not sufficient for "what is x?"

A statement that sometimes makes the answer yes and sometimes makes it no for a yes/no question is *not* sufficient, even if the yes case is more common. The statement must force a single answer across all cases the statement allows.

### The five-step DS process

1. *Read the question stem carefully.* Classify it: value or yes/no? What specifically is being asked? If the stem involves variables, list the variables and constraints explicitly.

2. *Evaluate Statement (1) alone.* Ignoring Statement (2), does Statement (1) provide enough information to answer the specific question? Try to construct multiple scenarios consistent with Statement (1) — if they all yield the same answer, sufficient. If any two yield different answers, not sufficient.

3. *Evaluate Statement (2) alone.* Same process, ignoring Statement (1).

4. *If needed, evaluate both together.* If neither is alone sufficient, combine them and evaluate.

5. *Select the answer choice* that matches your finding.

The answer path follows from the two independent evaluations:

- Only (1) works → (A)
- Only (2) works → (B)
- Neither alone, together works → (C)
- Both alone work → (D)
- Neither alone, together doesn't work → (E)

### Value DS vs yes/no DS — the critical distinction

*Value DS* asks for a specific numeric value. "What is the value of x?" "How many students are in the class?" "What is Alan's age?" Sufficient means the statements force a unique value.

*Yes/no DS* asks whether a condition holds. "Is x > 0?" "Is the total less than \$100?" "Is Alan older than Bob?" Sufficient means the statements force a definite yes or definite no.

The distinction matters because the bar for sufficiency differs. For value, you need one answer. For yes/no, you need a consistent answer — and "sometimes yes, sometimes no" is insufficient.

### The C trap

The C trap is the most frequent DS trap. You find that the two statements together seem to answer the question, so you pick (C). But you didn't check whether one of the statements alone was also sufficient — either because you assumed it wasn't or because you rushed.

*How to avoid.* Always evaluate (1) alone and (2) alone fully before considering them together. If both are alone sufficient, the answer is (D), not (C). If one is alone sufficient, the answer is (A) or (B), not (C).

### The E trap

The E trap is picking (E) when the answer is actually (A), (B), (C), or (D). This usually happens when you miss a clever way the statements work together or when you fail to recognize that a single statement provides enough despite looking sparse.

*How to avoid.* Don't pick (E) until you have genuinely tried every combination. If both statements together don't obviously work, revisit — is there a hidden algebraic manipulation or conceptual insight that makes them work?

### The D trap

The D trap is picking (D) when the correct answer is actually (A) or (B). This happens when you assume both statements are sufficient without rigorously checking each.

*How to avoid.* For each statement alone, try to construct a counterexample. If you can construct a scenario where the statement holds but the question has multiple possible answers, the statement is not sufficient.

### The trap checklist for DS

Before selecting your answer, run this checklist:

*1. Did I carefully classify the question as value or yes/no?*
*2. Did I evaluate Statement (1) alone (without peeking at Statement (2))?*
*3. Did I evaluate Statement (2) alone (without letting Statement (1) leak in)?*
*4. If I concluded (C), did I genuinely verify that neither statement alone suffices?*
*5. If I concluded (D), did I verify both alone suffice with counterexample-attempts?*
*6. If I concluded (E), did I verify that combining them still doesn't suffice?*

### DS common traps, expanded

*Hidden constraint in the stem.* The stem might say "x is a positive integer," which restricts the range of values you can use to test. Missing this constraint is a common error.

*Implicit "not necessarily."* A statement like "x = y or x = 2y" does not uniquely determine x unless y is specified. Students sometimes assume "x = y" from such a statement.

*Yes/no where you assumed yes.* The question asks "is x even?" Statement gives you partial info. If some values make x even and others don't, the statement is insufficient. Many students pick sufficient because "x could be even."

*Arithmetic coincidence.* Two specific numbers might happen to satisfy both statements. You must check general cases, not just one example.

*Statement (2) reading Statement (1)'s variables without it.* Sometimes Statement (2) looks sufficient because you mentally carried information from Statement (1). Check Statement (2) in isolation.

### DS worked example 1 — value, integer constraint

*Stem.* If x is a positive integer, what is the value of x?

*(1)* x² = 25
*(2)* x is prime.

Evaluate (1) alone: x² = 25 gives x = 5 or x = −5. But the stem says x is a positive integer, so x = 5. *Sufficient.*

Evaluate (2) alone: x is prime. This allows x = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, ... Multiple values. *Not sufficient.*

Since (1) alone is sufficient and (2) alone is not, the answer is **(A)**.

*Trap to avoid.* If you missed the "positive integer" constraint and treated x² = 25 as giving x = ±5, you might have called (1) insufficient and picked (C). The constraint matters.

### DS worked example 2 — yes/no, careful definitions

*Stem.* Is x > y?

*(1)* x = 2y
*(2)* y > 0.

Evaluate (1) alone: x = 2y. If y = 3, x = 6, so x > y. If y = −3, x = −6, so x > y? No — −6 is less than −3. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

Evaluate (2) alone: y > 0. This tells us nothing about x relative to y. If x = 5 and y = 1, x > y. If x = −5 and y = 1, x < y. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

Together: y > 0 and x = 2y. So x = 2y where y > 0. Thus x = 2y > y (since y > 0 means y < 2y). *Sufficient.*

Answer: **(C)**.

*Trap to avoid.* Skipping the "y = −3" case in Statement (1) — assuming y > 0 without a reason.

### DS worked example 3 — the C trap

*Stem.* What is the value of x + y?

*(1)* x + 2y = 10
*(2)* 2x + y = 8

Evaluate (1) alone: one equation in two unknowns has infinite solutions. *Not sufficient.*

Evaluate (2) alone: same — one equation in two unknowns. *Not sufficient.*

Together: two equations in two unknowns. Solve. Add them: 3x + 3y = 18. So x + y = 6. *Sufficient.*

Answer: **(C)**.

This one is straightforward — the classical two-equations-in-two-unknowns pattern.

*Trap to avoid.* Over-thinking by testing whether an insight (the elimination shortcut) makes one of the statements alone sufficient. It doesn't here. But sometimes the stem has been engineered so that one specific combination gives the target, not the full solution — in those cases, one statement alone could suffice via a clever read. For this problem, straightforward (C).

### DS worked example 4 — the D trap

*Stem.* Is x² > 9?

*(1)* x > 3
*(2)* x < −3

Evaluate (1) alone: x > 3. If x > 3, then x² > 9. *Sufficient.*

Evaluate (2) alone: x < −3. If x < −3, then x² > 9 (for example, x = −4 gives x² = 16 > 9). *Sufficient.*

Both alone are sufficient. Answer: **(D)**.

*Trap to avoid.* Missing Statement (2)'s sufficiency. If x < −3, then x is more negative than −3, so x² > 9. Many students pick (A) because they don't carefully work through the negative case.

### Advanced DS: the algebraic vs conceptual choice

For complex DS questions, you often have two paths: algebraic manipulation or conceptual reasoning.

*Algebraic path.* Set up equations from each statement. Solve or try to solve. Conclude based on whether the system is solvable.

*Conceptual path.* Use number properties, geometric intuition, or structural observation. "Is xy > 0?" is really "do x and y have the same sign?" — a much simpler reformulation.

Elite DS solvers use the conceptual path whenever possible. The algebraic path is backup. Practice recognizing when conceptual reformulation applies.

### DS recognition cues

- *Stem has "value of x"* → value DS. Statements must force a unique value.
- *Stem has "is x..." or "does x..."* → yes/no DS. Statements must force a definite answer.
- *Two statements that each look like single equations* → likely requires both together for two unknowns. But check each alone first.
- *One statement gives a range, the other gives an equation* → usually the equation is sufficient, the range alone is not.
- *Statement has "x > 0" or "x is positive"* → this is a sign constraint that might make the previously-ambiguous clear.

## Part 2: Multi-Source Reasoning

MSR is the format with the longest read time per question. A set has three tabs (often labeled by topic, like "Project Plan," "Budget," and "Personnel") and three linked questions. The tabs contain text, tables, and/or figures. The questions require you to extract and integrate data across tabs.

### The skim-first principle

Do not read every word of all three tabs before looking at the questions. You'll spend four minutes and won't retain it anyway. Instead:

1. Take 30–45 seconds to skim each tab. Identify what type of information each tab contains (calendar, budget, personnel, etc.). Note any headers, column names, and standout values.

2. Move to the first question. Read the question. Identify which tab(s) likely have the answer. Re-read only those tabs carefully for the specific data points needed.

3. Answer the first question, then move to the second. Don't re-skim all three tabs from scratch.

4. Continue to the third question with the same approach.

This workflow saves 2–3 minutes per MSR set compared to trying to memorize all three tabs up front.

### The question-driven workflow

Every MSR question is essentially: "given the data in the tabs, what is true about [some specific question]?" The answer is in the tabs — you just need to find it.

*Step 1.* Parse the question stem. Identify exactly what is being asked. The stem is often long and specific — read it twice.

*Step 2.* Predict which tab(s) contain the relevant data.

*Step 3.* Go to the predicted tab(s) and extract the data.

*Step 4.* If needed, cross-reference with a second tab.

*Step 5.* Answer the question.

### MSR question types

*Data retrieval.* The question asks for a specific piece of data stated explicitly in one tab. Easy — find and report. Example: "According to the budget tab, what is the allocated amount for marketing?"

*Cross-tab integration.* The question requires combining data from two tabs. Moderate. Example: "How many team members from the personnel tab are working on the marketing project from the budget tab?"

*Inference.* The question asks what can be inferred from the data across tabs. Like RC inference — the answer must be supported by what the tabs say, without over-extension.

*Calculation.* The question requires computing a value from the data — a percent change, a total, a ratio. Straightforward if you can find the right data points.

*Yes/no / true-false multi-part.* The question has three sub-statements and asks which are true. Each is evaluated independently.

### MSR trap patterns

*Over-inferring.* The question asks what can be inferred; you extend beyond what the tabs support. Common trap — answers that sound reasonable but aren't explicitly supported.

*Tab confusion.* You look at the wrong tab. The data in Budget tab might contradict the data in Personnel tab on a related point — make sure you're looking at the tab the question references.

*Misreading a column header.* The table might have a column labeled "expected completion date" vs "actual completion date" — different things. Missing this distinction misreads the data.

*Stale data.* One tab might describe the original plan; another might describe the actual results. If the question asks about "current status," use the actual-results tab, not the plan tab.

*Unit confusion.* One tab might be in thousands, another in millions. Check units on every number.

### MSR pacing

A typical MSR set (3 questions off 3 tabs) should take 6–8 minutes total. Roughly: 2 minutes for the initial skim, then 90 seconds to 2 minutes per question.

Do not spend 4 minutes on the initial skim. You don't need to understand everything — just the structure.

### MSR worked example concept

I am not going to lay out a full three-tab MSR here (the visual complexity doesn't render cleanly in markdown), but I will describe the workflow on a hypothetical MSR:

*Setup.* Tab 1 is a project timeline with phases and dates. Tab 2 is a budget with phase-level allocations. Tab 3 is a personnel list with team members assigned to phases.

*Question 1.* "Which phase has the highest budget-per-person ratio?"

Workflow: Go to Budget tab, note budget per phase. Go to Personnel tab, count people per phase. Compute ratios. Report the phase with highest ratio.

*Question 2.* "Is the marketing phase scheduled to begin before or after the design phase is completed?"

Workflow: Go to Timeline tab. Find start date of marketing phase, end date of design phase. Compare.

*Question 3.* "If the personnel assigned to the testing phase were reassigned to the design phase, which of the following would be true?"

Workflow: Go to Personnel tab to count testing-phase people. Go to Budget tab to get design-phase budget. Evaluate the hypothetical.

Each question targets a specific tab or pair of tabs. The skim-first-then-question-driven workflow keeps you efficient.

### MSR advanced: the multi-hop inference

The hardest MSR questions require two or three hops of inference across tabs. For example: "The project manager wants to accelerate the schedule. Given the personnel allocation and the budget constraints, which phase is most likely to be delayed?"

This requires: checking personnel allocation (Tab 3), checking budget (Tab 2), and checking timeline (Tab 1) — then reasoning about which combination implies the delay. These are the hardest MSR questions. They take 2:30+. Budget accordingly.

## Part 3: Table Analysis

Table Analysis (TA) presents a sortable table of data with column headers. Each question asks about relationships among the data, usually via 3 Yes/No sub-statements. You must get all sub-statements right to earn the point (no partial credit).

### The sort-first principle

The table is sortable by any column. Use this. Sorting by the relevant column is often the fastest way to answer a question.

If the question asks "for how many companies is revenue greater than expenses," sort by revenue (or expenses), identify the rows where revenue > expenses, count.

If the question asks "what is the median profit margin," sort by profit margin, find the middle value.

Sorting is often faster than reading rows unsorted.

### The sub-statement workflow

Each TA question has 2–3 sub-statements, each Yes/No. Evaluate them independently.

*Step 1.* Read the full question stem and all sub-statements before starting.

*Step 2.* Identify which column (or columns) is relevant to each sub-statement. If multiple sub-statements use the same column, sort once and evaluate all of them.

*Step 3.* For each sub-statement, determine the answer carefully. All sub-statements must be correct for the point.

### TA trap patterns

*Mistaking percentage for absolute.* Column labeled "percent change" shows percents; don't treat as dollars.

*Off-by-one in sorting.* After sorting, the third row from the top is the third-highest; the third row from the bottom is the third-lowest. Don't confuse.

*Misreading column combined logic.* "Revenue > expenses AND profit margin > 10%" requires both conditions. Don't evaluate only one.

*Stale sort.* If the table auto-sorts or defaults to alphabetical, remember which column you're currently sorted by. Re-sort if needed.

### TA worked example concept

*Table.* 12 rows of companies with columns for revenue, expenses, profit, profit margin, and industry.

*Question.* For each of the following sub-statements, determine whether it is true based on the table.

*Sub-statement 1.* More than 30% of the companies have a profit margin above 15%.

Workflow: Sort by profit margin. Count rows with profit margin > 15%. If more than 30% of 12 (i.e., more than 3.6, so at least 4 rows), true.

*Sub-statement 2.* The company with the highest revenue is in the technology industry.

Workflow: Sort by revenue descending. Check the industry of the top row.

*Sub-statement 3.* The average profit margin of the companies in the manufacturing industry is above 10%.

Workflow: Identify manufacturing companies. Compute average profit margin for just those. Compare to 10%.

Each sub-statement is evaluated separately. Error on any one costs the entire point.

### TA calculator strategy

Use the calculator for: averages across 5+ numbers, multi-step percent calculations, and large number arithmetic. Don't use it for: single multiplication of small numbers, simple fractions, or anything you can do mentally.

The calculator costs 5–10 seconds per use — invoke it strategically.

### TA pacing

A typical TA question (with 3 sub-statements) should take 2:00–2:30. The sort step is 15 seconds. Each sub-statement evaluation is 30–45 seconds. Integration of all three and marking is 15 seconds.

## Part 4: Graphics Interpretation

Graphics Interpretation (GI) presents a chart — bar, line, scatter, pie, dual-axis, sometimes more exotic. You read the chart and answer one or two questions, often in a fill-in-the-blank format with a dropdown menu.

### The axis-first principle

Before you look at any data point, read both axes. Know what the x-axis is (units, range) and what the y-axis is (units, range). Know any legend or color-coding.

Students who skip this step misread the chart. A scatter plot with "percent change in revenue" on the y-axis and "percent change in customer count" on the x-axis is different from one with raw revenue and raw customer count. The labels matter.

### Zooming for specific points

Once you understand the chart, find the specific point(s) the question asks about. If the question is "what was the revenue in 2020?", find the 2020 data point and read its value off the y-axis.

For bar charts: the height of the bar is the value.

For line charts: the y-value at the specific x-value is the data point.

For scatter plots: the specific marker's (x, y) position is the data pair.

For pie charts: the angle of the slice (or its label) gives the proportion.

### CAGR, growth rates, and trend questions

Many GI questions involve growth rates. Know these formulas.

*Percent change.* (new − old) / old × 100.

*Compound annual growth rate (CAGR).* (end / start)^(1/years) − 1. For a 4-year period where value went from 100 to 146.4, CAGR is (1.464)^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 0.10 or 10% per year.

*Simple average growth rate.* Total percent change divided by number of years. Not the same as CAGR but close for small numbers.

For GI, the test often gives you a multi-year chart and asks for CAGR. If exact calculation is needed, use the calculator. Estimate first, then compute.

### Chart type cheat sheet

*Bar chart.* Discrete values across categories. Read height to get value.

*Line chart.* Continuous trend over time or ordered x-axis. Read y at specific x.

*Stacked bar / stacked line.* Shows total plus composition. Read top of stack for total, individual segments for components.

*Pie chart.* Proportions. Read angle or label for each slice.

*Scatter plot.* Each point is an (x, y) pair. Look for correlation direction and strength.

*Dual-axis chart.* Left and right y-axes have different scales. Match the line or bar to its axis by color or style.

*Box plot.* Shows distribution — median (center line), quartiles (box), extremes (whiskers).

*Histogram.* Distribution of values — bar height is count in a range.

### GI trap patterns

*Wrong axis.* Reading the x-axis when you need the y, or vice versa.

*Scale confusion.* The y-axis might be labeled in thousands or millions. Missing the scale means your answer is off by 10x or 1000x.

*Legend misread.* Multiple lines on a chart are distinguished by color or dash pattern — misidentifying which is which.

*Unit swap.* The question asks about percentage; the chart shows absolute values. Or vice versa.

*Truncated axis.* The y-axis starts at 50 instead of 0, exaggerating visual differences. Read the numbers, not the visual proportions.

### GI pacing

A typical GI question (one or two fill-in-the-blanks) should take 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Axis-reading is 15 seconds. Data extraction is 30 seconds. Computation is the rest.

### GI worked example

*Chart.* A line graph showing a company's annual revenue from 2018 to 2023. 2018: 100. 2019: 110. 2020: 125. 2021: 135. 2022: 150. 2023: 160.

*Question.* The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of revenue from 2018 to 2023 is closest to:
(A) 8%  (B) 10%  (C) 12%  (D) 15%  (E) 20%

*Workflow.* 2018 to 2023 is 5 years of growth. Revenue went from 100 to 160. CAGR = (160/100)^(1/5) − 1 = 1.6^(0.2) − 1.

To estimate: 1.1^5 ≈ 1.61. So (160/100)^(1/5) is approximately 1.1, giving CAGR ≈ 10%.

Answer: (B).

*Trap.* Computing simple average growth rate instead. 60% total growth over 5 years = 12% per year simple average, pointing to (C). But CAGR ≠ simple average growth. The right answer is (B).

## Part 5: Two-Part Analysis

Two-Part Analysis (TPA) asks you to select one row for each of two columns, where the rows are a list of options. Both selections must be correct for the point.

### Equations-first, rows-second

A typical TPA stem sets up a relationship — often an algebraic or financial one. Before reading the rows, set up the equation or relationship the stem implies.

Example: "A store sells product A at price p and product B at price q, where p and q are positive integers. The store sold a total of 40 items for \$170. Select the price p that the store charged for product A and the price q that the store charged for product B."

Equations: p × (number of A) + q × (number of B) = 170. Total items = 40.

Without knowing how many A or how many B, you have two unknowns (p, q) plus two more unknowns (number of A, number of B). That's underdetermined — unless the rows give you additional constraints.

### The two canonical TPA structures

*Structure 1: Two linked quantities.* You're given a relationship involving two quantities; you pick the value of each that satisfies the relationship. Often the rows contain specific numeric values; you try combinations.

*Structure 2: Two separate but related computations.* You compute quantity A based on the stem, then compute quantity B based on the stem. They're separate, but both use the same input data.

### TPA worked example

*Stem.* A manager is planning a trip. The trip requires booking 4 hotel nights at rate r per night and 2 flights at cost c each, where r and c are positive integers. The total trip cost is \$800. The hotel rate is less than the flight cost. Select the hotel rate r and the flight cost c from the options below.

*Options:* 80, 100, 120, 150, 180, 200.

*Equations.* 4r + 2c = 800, so 2r + c = 400. Constraint: r < c. Both positive integers.

*Try options.* If r = 80, then c = 400 − 160 = 240. But 240 isn't in the options. No match.

If r = 100, then c = 400 − 200 = 200. Both in options? Yes. r < c? 100 < 200 yes. Match.

If r = 120, then c = 400 − 240 = 160. Not in options.

If r = 150, then c = 400 − 300 = 100. But 150 < 100? No. Fails constraint.

Answer: r = 100, c = 200.

*Trap.* Forgetting the constraint r < c. Picking r = 150 and c = 100 because it also satisfies 4(150) + 2(100) = 800 = target, without checking the ordering.

### TPA verification discipline

Every TPA answer should be verified against every constraint in the stem. This is non-negotiable — TPA is all-or-nothing, and a single constraint violation loses the point.

Before locking in your answer, re-read the stem and confirm that your selections satisfy all stated conditions.

### TPA trap patterns

*Ignoring a constraint.* The stem has a key constraint (like "r < c") buried in the middle of the text. Students skip it and choose an answer that satisfies the primary equation but fails the constraint.

*Reversed column selection.* The question asks for "value of A" and "value of B" in a specific order. Students occasionally swap, picking an answer that would be right for the other column.

*Arithmetic slip in verification.* Students compute the answer correctly but then misverify the multiplication, concluding the wrong answer is right.

*Over-computing when a shortcut exists.* The rows often constrain the problem enough that one column uniquely identifies the other. If after computing the first column, the second is forced, you don't need to enumerate all row combinations.

### TPA pacing

A typical TPA question should take 2:00–2:30. The stem reading and equation setup is 30–45 seconds. The row enumeration and verification is 60–90 seconds.

## Part 6: The Business Interpretation Mindset

Many DI questions are framed in a business context — a company, a market, a project, a product launch. The "right" answer often requires thinking about the business logic, not just the numerical data.

*Example.* A GI question about revenue and customer count asks: "what does the data suggest about the company's strategy?" A purely numerical read says "revenue grew and customer count stayed flat, so revenue per customer grew." A business read adds: "this suggests price increases, higher-value product mix, or cross-sells — the company is growing through existing customer depth, not new customer acquisition."

The business frame is not always necessary, but it is often helpful — especially for MSR and TPA questions with long business-narrative stems. Train the business-reading instinct alongside the numerical reading.

## Part 7: DI Pacing

20 questions in 45 minutes = 2:15 per question on average. But the distribution is uneven.

*Rough time allocation.*
- DS questions: 90 seconds to 2 minutes each. 6 questions × average 1:45 = 10:30.
- MSR sets: 6–8 minutes per set (3 questions). Typically 1 MSR set × 7 minutes = 7:00.
- TA questions: 2:00–2:30 each. 2–3 questions × 2:15 = 5:00–7:00.
- GI questions: 90 seconds to 2 minutes each. 3–4 questions × 1:45 = 5:15–7:00.
- TPA questions: 2:00–2:30 each. 2–3 questions × 2:15 = 4:30–7:00.

This totals roughly 32–38 minutes, which leaves 7–13 minutes of buffer for harder questions or ones that demand more thought.

### The format-switching cost

Because DI mixes formats, every question transition forces a brief cognitive switch. This switch has a cost — 5–10 seconds to re-orient. Over 20 questions, that's 100–200 seconds of overhead. Plan for it.

*Mitigation.* Practice with mixed-format sets, not blocked-format sets. Your mock conditions must match the real test's format mixing.

### The 2:30 walk-away rule

Same as Quant and Verbal: at 2:30 on any DI question, mark a guess and move. The three downstream questions you rush because you stayed on one problem will cost more than one hard-solved question saves.

Exception: MSR. If you're in the middle of a 3-question MSR set and one question is stalling, don't abandon the whole set — answer the remaining questions first (they may be easier), then return to the stalled one if time permits.

### The 30-second sanity check

At 30 seconds into each question, ask: do I understand what's being asked? Do I know which method or format-specific approach I'm using? Am I making visible progress?

If yes to all three, continue. If no, either pivot or skip and come back.

### The clock waypoints

- 10 minutes in: you should have completed 4–5 questions.
- 22 minutes in: you should have completed 10 questions (halfway).
- 33 minutes in: you should have completed 15 questions.
- 45 minutes: all done.

Check at these waypoints. If behind, adjust by budgeting less on subsequent questions.

## Part 8: Calculator Discipline

The on-screen calculator is your friend, not your crutch. Use it wisely.

### When to use the calculator

*Messy multiplication or division.* 437 × 68 takes 30 seconds mental, 5 seconds on calculator. Use the calculator.

*Multi-step percent calculations.* 15% of 640, then multiply by 0.85, then subtract 40. Calculator is faster and more accurate.

*Decimals with many places.* 3.14159 × 7.2. Calculator.

*Large number arithmetic.* 1.4 million × 12% at a specific scale. Calculator.

*Powers, roots, and compound interest.* Calculator is essential.

### When NOT to use the calculator

*Simple mental math.* 12 × 5. Never touch the calculator.

*Estimation.* If you're estimating, mental math is the whole point.

*Multiple-choice with obvious spread.* If answer choices are clearly spread (10, 40, 100, 400), estimation + elimination is faster than calculator + precision.

*Fractions with clean cancellations.* 18 × 5/9 should be mental (10), not calculator (0.555... × 18 = 10).

### The calculator pause

Every calculator invocation costs 5–10 seconds — click, type, read, click back. Budget this. If your problem requires three calculator uses, that's 15–30 seconds of overhead.

Elite DI solvers invoke the calculator 1–3 times per problem maximum. If you're invoking it 5+ times, you're either facing a genuinely calculator-heavy problem or using it for things you should do mentally.

### Calculator accuracy checks

After every calculator computation, sanity-check with a rough mental estimate. If your calculator says 3.47 million and your mental estimate says "about 3 million," they agree. If calculator says 3.47 billion, you probably typed wrong. This 3-second check catches 80% of calculator errors.

## Part 9: The DI Trap Compendium

Fifty traps, organized by format. Review before any timed DI practice session.

### DS traps

1. *C trap.* Picking C without checking whether one statement alone suffices.
2. *E trap.* Picking E when the statements together do actually suffice.
3. *D trap.* Picking D when only one statement suffices.
4. *Value-DS with multiple possible values.* Treating "x = 3 or x = −3" as sufficient for "what is x?".
5. *Yes/no-DS with mixed outcomes.* Treating "sometimes yes, sometimes no" as sufficient.
6. *Missing stem constraint.* Forgetting "x is a positive integer" and treating x = ±5 as valid.
7. *Leaking Statement (1) into Statement (2).* Evaluating Statement (2) with Statement (1)'s information still loaded.
8. *Coincidence verification.* Verifying with one example instead of checking general cases.
9. *Implicit quantifier.* A statement that says "or" as if it's conclusive.
10. *Sign missing.* Failing to consider negative cases when the stem doesn't restrict to positive.

### MSR traps

11. *Tab confusion.* Reading data from the wrong tab.
12. *Column header misread.* "Expected" vs "actual" or "projected" vs "realized."
13. *Scale mismatch.* One tab in thousands, another in millions.
14. *Over-inferring.* Extending beyond what the tabs explicitly support.
15. *Plan vs actual.* Using plan data when the question asks about actual outcomes.
16. *Cross-tab miscount.* Counting people from Personnel tab but forgetting some are in Project tab.
17. *Excessive up-front reading.* Reading all three tabs in full before looking at questions.
18. *Not re-reading on new question.* Forgetting to go back to tabs for a second question.

### TA traps

19. *Misreading sort direction.* Third from top is not third from bottom.
20. *Compound criteria.* "A AND B" when the question uses "AND" — don't settle for just A.
21. *Unit confusion in column.* Percent vs absolute.
22. *Missing a sub-statement.* Evaluating only 2 of 3 sub-statements.
23. *Cumulative vs per-period.* Year-over-year values vs cumulative totals.

### GI traps

24. *Wrong axis.* Reading y when you need x.
25. *Scale missed.* Y-axis in thousands, taken as raw numbers.
26. *Legend misread.* Two lines on the chart — confused which is which.
27. *Truncated axis.* Y-axis starts at 50 — visual impressions exaggerated.
28. *Chart-type confusion.* Reading a line chart as if it were a bar chart.
29. *Unit mismatch.* Question asks percentage, chart shows absolute.
30. *Trend-line misread.* Extrapolating beyond the data range.
31. *CAGR vs simple average.* Using simple average when CAGR is asked.

### TPA traps

32. *Missing a constraint.* Ignoring "r < c" while solving.
33. *Column swap.* Selecting the right answer but for the wrong column.
34. *Arithmetic slip in verification.* Computing right, verifying wrong.
35. *Failed verification.* Not verifying at all.
36. *Over-enumeration.* Trying every combination when a shortcut exists.

### Cross-format traps

37. *Format switch cost not respected.* Jumping straight into a new format without reading the stem carefully.
38. *Calculator over-reliance.* Using calculator for simple mental math.
39. *Calculator under-reliance.* Not using calculator when it would save time.
40. *Over-computing.* Doing the full calculation when the question just needs estimation.
41. *Under-computing.* Estimating when precision matters.
42. *Pace drift on MSR.* Letting one MSR set eat 12+ minutes.
43. *Pace drift on DS.* Grinding a hard DS for 3+ minutes.
44. *Skipping the question stem twice.* Reading the stem, forgetting what was asked, re-reading.
45. *Distractor attraction.* Picking the "first reasonable" answer without checking others.
46. *Business-logic miss.* Purely numerical reads missing the business framing.
47. *Qualifier miss.* "At least" vs "exactly" confusion.
48. *Negation miss.* "Not" vs "not always" confusion.
49. *Time-pressure guessing.* Guessing intelligently on last questions to finish, but forgetting to eliminate obvious wrongs before guessing.
50. *Last-question neglect.* Running out of time on the last 1–2 questions and not guessing at all. Always put down a guess.

## Part 10: How to Study DI

The highest-leverage insight: *DI improvement comes from format-specific practice, not generic quant practice*. A student who does 500 Quant problems a week will not improve DI. A student who does 100 DI problems per week with disciplined error-log review will.

### Week 1–2: Format familiarity

Do a small set of each format — 10 DS, 10 MSR, 10 TA, 10 GI, 10 TPA — at untimed, full-review pace. The goal is familiarity with the format-specific conventions, not speed.

For DS: work every problem using the five-step process. Do not skip steps even if you "see" the answer.

For MSR: practice the skim-first workflow. Deliberately do not read the tabs in full before looking at the first question.

For TA: practice sorting for every problem, even when you could solve without.

For GI: read both axes and any legend before looking at data points. Every problem.

For TPA: set up the equations before reading the row options. Every problem.

### Week 3–4: Pacing introduction

Add timer. Target: 2:30 for any single question; 8 minutes for an MSR set. Log every mistake.

Focus on format-specific error patterns. If you consistently miss TPA because of constraint oversights, add a "re-read constraint" step to your TPA routine.

### Week 5–6: Mixed-format sets

Do 10-question mixed sets under time pressure. Track your error patterns across formats. Identify your weakest format and drill it extra.

### Week 7–8: Full-section simulation

Weekly: one full DI section (45 minutes) under mock conditions. Track pacing at the waypoints.

### Week 9–10: Targeted weakness work

By now you should know your format weaknesses cold. Spend 60% of DI practice time on your two weakest formats. The remaining 40% is mixed to preserve speed.

### Week 11–12: Taper and mock discipline

Reduce practice volume. Continue weekly mocks. Last week: light practice, heavy review.

### The weekly review

Every Sunday, 30 minutes on DI specifically:

- Group week's errors by format.
- Identify top 2 patterns.
- Set a specific goal (e.g., "no C-trap errors this week").
- Re-read the relevant Part of this chapter.

### The DS daily drill

For the first six weeks, do 5 DS problems per day, untimed, fully written out (statement 1 analysis, statement 2 analysis, conclusion). This builds sufficiency-style thinking reliably. After six weeks of this habit, you can transition to timed DS sets.

## Part 11: Six Harder DI Problems, Fully Worked

Part 1 through 5 gave you the format mechanics. Here are six harder problems across the formats with full method commentary.

### Harder problem 1 — a layered DS

*Stem.* A company has two departments, A and B. The ratio of employees in Department A to Department B is 3:5. If 10 employees are transferred from Department B to Department A, the new ratio becomes 1:1. What is the total number of employees in the company?

*(1)* The total number of employees in the company is divisible by 8.
*(2)* The number of employees in Department A before the transfer was more than 10.

*Analysis.* Let A = 3k and B = 5k originally. After transfer: A' = 3k + 10, B' = 5k − 10. New ratio 1:1 means A' = B'. 3k + 10 = 5k − 10, so 2k = 20, k = 10. Total = 8k = 80.

Wait — the stem already gives us enough to determine the answer without either statement. Let me reread. The stem says "if 10 employees are transferred, the new ratio becomes 1:1." That's a condition, not a question. The question asks what the total is.

From the ratio condition (3:5) and the transfer condition (new ratio 1:1 after moving 10), we can solve uniquely: k = 10, total = 80. So the stem alone is sufficient.

But wait — DS doesn't work that way. If the stem alone is sufficient, then both statements are also sufficient (trivially), and the answer would be (D). Let me recheck my setup.

Actually, on rereading — the stem provides an if-then relationship. The "if" might not be saying the transfer actually happened; it might be a hypothetical: "if the transfer were to occur, the ratio would become 1:1." This makes the statement "the new ratio would be 1:1" a conditional, implicit equation.

Given this reading, from 3k + 10 = 5k − 10, k = 10, so total = 80. The stem is sufficient.

Either statement being true just corroborates. The answer is (D).

Actually, since this constructed example lets the stem fully solve, it's an illustration that we need to read DS stems carefully — some DS questions are set up so that the stem alone determines the answer, and the statements are confirmatory. Those are (D).

*Recognition cue.* When the stem sets up a sufficient system of equations, both statements alone are often sufficient — (D) is the natural answer.

### Harder problem 2 — a conceptual DS

*Stem.* Is the integer n positive?

*(1)* n² > n
*(2)* n³ > n

*Analysis.*

Statement (1) alone: n² > n. Rearrange: n² − n > 0, so n(n − 1) > 0. This holds when both factors are positive (n > 1) or both are negative (n < 0). So n > 1 or n < 0. In the first case, n is positive; in the second, n is negative. So (1) alone doesn't force n positive. Not sufficient.

Statement (2) alone: n³ > n. Rearrange: n³ − n > 0, so n(n − 1)(n + 1) > 0. This is a product of three factors. We need to find the signs.

If n > 1: all three factors positive, product positive. True.
If 0 < n < 1: n positive, (n − 1) negative, (n + 1) positive. Product negative. False.
If n = 0, 1, or −1: product is zero. Not > 0.
If −1 < n < 0: n negative, (n − 1) negative, (n + 1) positive. Product positive. True.
If n < −1: n negative, (n − 1) negative, (n + 1) negative. Product negative. False.

So Statement (2) holds when n > 1 or −1 < n < 0. In the first case, n is positive; in the second, n is negative. So (2) alone doesn't force n positive. Not sufficient.

Together: n² > n AND n³ > n.

n² > n requires n > 1 or n < 0.
n³ > n requires n > 1 or −1 < n < 0.

Intersection: n > 1 or −1 < n < 0. Still allows both positive and negative.

So (C) is not sufficient either. Answer: (E).

*Recognition cue.* Yes/no DS with inequality-based statements require full case analysis. Don't trust surface similarity.

### Harder problem 3 — an MSR inference

(Abstract example since MSR renders poorly in markdown.)

*Setup.* Tab 1: quarterly revenue by product line. Tab 2: quarterly marketing spend by product line. Tab 3: quarterly customer count by product line.

*Question.* The manager is considering reallocating marketing budget. Which product line has the highest incremental customer acquisition per marketing dollar spent in Q4 2023?

*Workflow.*
1. From Tab 3, find Q4 2023 customer count for each product line.
2. From Tab 3, find Q3 2023 customer count for each product line.
3. Compute incremental customer acquisition (Q4 count − Q3 count) for each product line.
4. From Tab 2, find Q4 2023 marketing spend for each product line.
5. Compute customer acquisition per marketing dollar (incremental / spend).
6. Identify the product line with the highest ratio.

*Trap.* Using total Q4 customers (not incremental) — this would favor whichever product line has the largest absolute base, not the highest acquisition efficiency.

*Lesson.* Complex MSR questions require careful interpretation of "incremental" vs "total" vs "growth rate" — these are different metrics.

### Harder problem 4 — a GI with a dual axis

*Chart description.* Dual-axis chart. Left y-axis: company revenue (in millions of dollars). Right y-axis: customer satisfaction score (0–100). X-axis: years 2020 through 2024. A line plots revenue; a separate line plots satisfaction.

*Data.* 2020: revenue 500, satisfaction 75. 2021: revenue 550, satisfaction 78. 2022: revenue 620, satisfaction 80. 2023: revenue 700, satisfaction 76. 2024: revenue 750, satisfaction 72.

*Question.* Which of the following best describes the relationship between revenue growth and satisfaction from 2020 to 2024?

(A) Revenue and satisfaction both increased over the period.
(B) Satisfaction peaked during the period and subsequently declined.
(C) Revenue growth accelerated while satisfaction declined monotonically.
(D) Revenue and satisfaction are inversely correlated across the period.
(E) There is no discernible relationship.

*Analysis.* Revenue increased every year: 500 → 750. Satisfaction: 75 → 78 → 80 → 76 → 72. Satisfaction peaked at 80 in 2022, then declined to 76 and 72. So (B) is accurate.

(A) is partially true for revenue but wrong for satisfaction (which declined post-2022). (C) says satisfaction declined monotonically, but it increased from 2020 to 2022. (D) "inversely correlated" is too strong — the relationship changed around 2022. (E) is wrong — there is a discernible pattern.

Answer: (B).

*Trap.* Picking (A) because it's the "quick" read — both lines visually trend upward at the left of the chart. But the right side shows satisfaction falling. Read the full range, not just the beginning.

### Harder problem 5 — a TPA with hidden constraint

*Stem.* A small business owner is deciding how to allocate a \$10,000 budget between two suppliers, Supplier A and Supplier B. Supplier A charges \$50 per unit. Supplier B charges \$80 per unit. The owner needs at least 50 units total and needs to purchase at least 20 units from Supplier B to qualify for a loyalty discount from that supplier. Assuming the full budget is spent, select the number of units purchased from Supplier A and the number from Supplier B.

Options: 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120.

*Analysis.* Let a = units from A, b = units from B. Constraints:
- 50a + 80b = 10000 (budget)
- a + b ≥ 50 (total units minimum)
- b ≥ 20 (loyalty discount)
- a, b positive integers

From budget: a = (10000 − 80b) / 50 = 200 − 1.6b.

For a to be a positive integer, 1.6b must make 200 − 1.6b a non-negative integer. 1.6b integer requires b to be a multiple of 5 (since 1.6 = 8/5). Try b values from options that are multiples of 5: 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120.

b = 20: a = 200 − 32 = 168. Not in options.
b = 40: a = 200 − 64 = 136. Not in options.
b = 60: a = 200 − 96 = 104. Not in options.
b = 80: a = 200 − 128 = 72. Not in options.
b = 100: a = 200 − 160 = 40. In options!

Verify: a + b = 40 + 100 = 140 ≥ 50. ✓ b = 100 ≥ 20. ✓ Budget: 50 × 40 + 80 × 100 = 2000 + 8000 = 10000. ✓

Answer: a = 40 (Supplier A), b = 100 (Supplier B).

*Trap.* Not checking the "a + b ≥ 50" constraint — which is satisfied here but wouldn't be for some smaller-b combinations. Or not starting with b values from the options — trying a values first and running into non-integer b values.

### Harder problem 6 — a deep DS on integer constraints

*Stem.* If n is a positive integer, is n² − n divisible by 4?

*(1)* n is even.
*(2)* n is a multiple of 4.

*Analysis.*

Statement (1) alone: n is even. So n = 2k for some positive integer k. n² − n = 4k² − 2k = 2k(2k − 1). For this to be divisible by 4, we need k(2k − 1) to be even. (2k − 1) is always odd, so we need k to be even. But k could be anything positive — if k = 1 (so n = 2), k(2k − 1) = 1 × 1 = 1, not divisible by 4 cleanly — actually let me recompute. n² − n when n = 2: 4 − 2 = 2. 2 / 4 = 0.5. Not divisible by 4.

When n = 4: 16 − 4 = 12. 12 / 4 = 3. Divisible.

So Statement (1) allows both "not divisible by 4" (n = 2) and "divisible by 4" (n = 4). Not sufficient.

Statement (2) alone: n is a multiple of 4. So n = 4m for some positive integer m. n² − n = 16m² − 4m = 4m(4m − 1). This is 4 × m(4m − 1), which is clearly divisible by 4. Sufficient.

Answer: (B).

*Recognition cue.* Yes/no DS about divisibility requires checking specific cases to confirm sufficiency or insufficiency. Don't assume.

## Part 13: Deeper DS Walkthroughs

Part 1 gave you four DS worked examples — a foundation. The DS problems that decide top-percentile scores are harder. Here are eight more worked DS problems, each with full method commentary and the specific trap it punishes.

### DS Walkthrough 1 — the exponents trap

*Stem.* Is x² > y²?

*(1)* x > y
*(2)* x > 0

*Statement (1) alone.* x > y. Try x = 3, y = 2: x² = 9, y² = 4. 9 > 4, yes. Try x = −2, y = −3: x² = 4, y² = 9. 4 > 9? No. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* x > 0. Tells us nothing about y. If x = 3, y = 2: x² = 9, y² = 4, yes. If x = 1, y = −5: x² = 1, y² = 25, no. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* x > y and x > 0. Try x = 3, y = 2: 9 > 4, yes. Try x = 1, y = −5: but x = 1 > y = −5, yes. x² = 1, y² = 25, so x² < y². No. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

*Answer: (E).*

*Trap.* Assuming x > y and x > 0 together forces x² > y². The counterexample (x = 1, y = −5) has |y| > |x|, so y² > x² even though x > y and x > 0.

*Lesson.* Squaring destroys sign information. For any inequality involving squares, consider the absolute values, not just the signed values.

### DS Walkthrough 2 — the fraction trap

*Stem.* Is p/q > 1?

*(1)* p > q
*(2)* p + q > 0

*Statement (1) alone.* p > q. If both positive, p/q > 1. If both negative (p = −2, q = −3: p > q because −2 > −3), then p/q = 2/3 < 1. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* p + q > 0. If p = 5, q = 1: p/q = 5, yes. If p = 1, q = 5: p/q = 0.2, no. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* p > q and p + q > 0. If p = 3, q = 1: yes. If p = 5, q = 2: yes. If p = 2, q = −1: p + q = 1 > 0, p > q, p/q = −2, not > 1. Different. *Not sufficient.*

*Answer: (E).*

*Trap.* Fractions with negative denominators flip inequality interpretation. p/q > 1 is not the same as p > q unless q > 0.

*Lesson.* Fraction-inequality questions require sign constraints on the denominator.

### DS Walkthrough 3 — the clean (C)

*Stem.* If a and b are positive integers, what is the value of a + b?

*(1)* a × b = 24
*(2)* a − b = 2

*Statement (1) alone.* a × b = 24 with a, b positive integers: (a, b) could be (1, 24), (2, 12), (3, 8), (4, 6), (6, 4), (8, 3), etc. Multiple a + b values. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* a − b = 2. Multiple positive-integer pairs: (3, 1), (4, 2), (5, 3), etc. Multiple a + b values. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* a × b = 24 AND a − b = 2. From (2), a = b + 2. Substitute: (b + 2) × b = 24, so b² + 2b − 24 = 0. Factor: (b + 6)(b − 4) = 0. b = 4 or b = −6. Since b positive, b = 4, a = 6. a + b = 10. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (C).*

*Trap.* Assuming one statement alone is sufficient. Always check each alone before combining.

### DS Walkthrough 4 — the stem-constraint matter

*Stem.* If n is a positive integer and n has exactly three distinct positive factors, what is n?

*(1)* n is less than 50.
*(2)* n is odd.

*Analysis.* A positive integer with exactly 3 factors must be p² for some prime p (factors: 1, p, p²). Under 50: 4, 9, 25, 49. Odd: 9, 25, 49.

*Statement (1) alone.* Under 50: 4, 9, 25, 49. Multiple values. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* Odd: 9, 25, 49, 121, 169, ... Multiple values. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* Under 50 and odd: 9, 25, 49. Still multiple values. *Not sufficient.*

*Answer: (E).*

*Trap.* Thinking that "three distinct factors + under 50 + odd" is a unique pin, when it's not. The lesson: for DS integer questions, construct the full set of candidates before concluding sufficiency.

### DS Walkthrough 5 — the geometry-like reasoning

*Stem.* The population of a city increased by k% in year 1 and decreased by k% in year 2. Is the population at the end of year 2 higher than at the start of year 1?

*(1)* k = 10
*(2)* The population at the start of year 1 was 100,000.

*Analysis.* Let P be the starting population. After year 1: P × (1 + k/100). After year 2: P × (1 + k/100) × (1 − k/100) = P × (1 − k²/10000).

Since k² > 0 (assuming k ≠ 0), 1 − k²/10000 < 1. So the ending population is less than P.

The answer to the question is definitively "No" — regardless of the specific value of k (as long as k ≠ 0).

*Statement (1) alone.* k = 10. Tells us the specific value, which confirms our conclusion (1 − 100/10000 = 0.99, so population decreased by 1%). The answer is "No." *Sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* Population at start was 100,000. This doesn't determine k, but the relationship above shows that *for any k ≠ 0*, the population decreases. So the answer is still "No" regardless of k. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (D).*

*Trap.* Thinking Statement (2) is insufficient because it doesn't specify k. The key insight is that the question's answer doesn't depend on the specific value of k — it depends only on the structure.

*Lesson.* Some DS questions have an answer that follows from a general structural identity. Recognize when both statements are sufficient because the structure alone determines the answer.

### DS Walkthrough 6 — the DS with a hidden equation

*Stem.* If a, b, and c are consecutive integers (in some order), what is the value of a + b + c?

*(1)* The sum of the two smallest is 7.
*(2)* a × b × c = 60

*Analysis.* Three consecutive integers sum to 3× middle. So we need to find the middle.

*Statement (1) alone.* Two smallest sum to 7. If smallest two are n and n+1, then 2n + 1 = 7, so n = 3. The three consecutive integers are 3, 4, 5. Sum = 12. *Sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* Product is 60. Three consecutive integers with product 60: 3, 4, 5 (since 3 × 4 × 5 = 60). But also consider negative: (−5)(−4)(−3) = −60, not 60. So only 3, 4, 5. Sum = 12. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (D).*

*Trap.* Assuming Statement (2) has multiple solutions without checking. For three consecutive positive integers, 60 = 3 × 4 × 5 is unique. For negative or mixed, check — doesn't work.

### DS Walkthrough 7 — the layered yes/no

*Stem.* Is n divisible by 12?

*(1)* n is divisible by 4.
*(2)* n is divisible by 6.

*Statement (1) alone.* n divisible by 4 doesn't guarantee divisibility by 12. n = 4 is divisible by 4 but not 12. n = 12 is divisible by both. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* n divisible by 6. n = 6 is not divisible by 12. n = 12 is. Different. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* n divisible by 4 AND 6. LCM of 4 and 6 is 12. So n is divisible by 12. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (C).*

*Lesson.* LCM of the individual divisibilities may give you the needed divisibility. 4 = 2², 6 = 2 × 3, LCM = 2² × 3 = 12.

### DS Walkthrough 8 — the reasoning trap

*Stem.* Is x + y > 0?

*(1)* x > 0
*(2)* y > 0

*Statement (1) alone.* x > 0 says nothing about y. If y = −5 and x = 1, then x + y = −4 < 0. If y = 5 and x = 1, then x + y = 6 > 0. Different. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2) alone.* Similar logic. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* x > 0 AND y > 0. Both positive, so x + y > 0. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (C).*

*Lesson.* Sometimes the obvious answer is correct. This problem has no gotcha — it just tests the discipline of evaluating each statement alone before concluding (C).

## Part 14: A Full Worked MSR Walkthrough

MSR is the format that suffers most in a text-only medium because the three tabs are visually distinct. Here is a complete MSR walkthrough with each "tab" described in text.

### The scenario

A tech company, Halcyon Inc., is considering acquiring a smaller startup, Meridian Labs. The board has compiled information into three tabs.

*Tab 1: Company Overview (text).* Halcyon currently has 320 employees and generated \$28 million in revenue last fiscal year, with \$4.5 million in net profit. Meridian has 45 employees and generated \$3.2 million in revenue last year, with a net loss of \$800,000. Meridian's revenue growth was 50% in the past year, while Halcyon's was 12%. Meridian has a proprietary machine-learning platform that Halcyon would gain through acquisition. Halcyon's current offer is \$15 million, which Meridian has not yet accepted.

*Tab 2: Financial Projections (table).* Hypothetical 3-year projections for the combined company (post-acquisition), with three scenarios (Base, Optimistic, Pessimistic).
- Base: Year 1 revenue \$33 million; Year 2 \$38M; Year 3 \$44M. Profit Y1 \$4.0M; Y2 \$5.5M; Y3 \$7.5M.
- Optimistic: Y1 \$35M; Y2 \$42M; Y3 \$52M. Profit Y1 \$4.5M; Y2 \$6.5M; Y3 \$10M.
- Pessimistic: Y1 \$30M; Y2 \$32M; Y3 \$35M. Profit Y1 \$2.5M; Y2 \$3M; Y3 \$4M.

*Tab 3: Talent Retention (table).* Key Meridian personnel and their likelihood of staying post-acquisition.
- CEO: 60% likely to stay
- CTO: 85% likely to stay
- VP Engineering: 50% likely to stay
- 5 Senior ML engineers: average 70% likely to stay
- 4 Product managers: average 65% likely to stay

### Question 1 — Data retrieval

"According to the information provided, what is the difference between Halcyon's current annual revenue and Meridian's current annual revenue?"

(A) \$14.8 million
(B) \$20.8 million
(C) \$24.8 million
(D) \$25.2 million
(E) \$31.2 million

*Workflow.* Go to Tab 1. Halcyon revenue: \$28M. Meridian revenue: \$3.2M. Difference: 28 − 3.2 = \$24.8M. *Answer: (C).*

*Lesson.* Simple retrieval — identify the tab, extract, compute. No trap here; just efficiency.

### Question 2 — Inference across tabs

"If the pessimistic projection is realized for Year 1, how does Year 1 profit compare to Halcyon's current profit (pre-acquisition)?"

(A) Year 1 profit would be lower than current Halcyon profit by approximately 45%.
(B) Year 1 profit would be lower than current Halcyon profit by approximately 20%.
(C) Year 1 profit would be equal to current Halcyon profit.
(D) Year 1 profit would be higher than current Halcyon profit by approximately 20%.
(E) Year 1 profit would be higher than current Halcyon profit by approximately 45%.

*Workflow.* Tab 1: Halcyon's current net profit is \$4.5M. Tab 2: Pessimistic Year 1 profit is \$2.5M. Difference: 2.5 − 4.5 = −2.0M. Percent: 2.0 / 4.5 ≈ 0.444 or 44%. So roughly 45% lower. *Answer: (A).*

*Lesson.* Cross-tab inference — pull a value from each tab and compute.

### Question 3 — Multi-hop synthesis

"Which of the following conclusions is most strongly supported by the information in all three tabs?"

(A) The acquisition's success depends heavily on retaining Meridian's CTO.
(B) The pessimistic projection assumes a high rate of talent attrition.
(C) The combined company is likely to achieve the optimistic projection.
(D) Meridian's revenue growth is attributable primarily to its proprietary platform.
(E) The acquisition will fail if the CEO leaves.

*Analysis.*

(A) is plausible — the CTO has the highest retention likelihood (85%) and is typically critical for a company with proprietary technology. But the passage doesn't directly support that the acquisition's success "depends heavily" on the CTO. Not strongest.

(B) is speculative — the pessimistic projection numbers are given, but the reason isn't explicitly tied to talent attrition.

(C) is speculative — nothing in the data establishes which scenario is "likely."

(D) is speculative — the passage mentions the platform and the growth rate but doesn't tie them causally.

(E) is too strong — the CEO is only 60% likely to stay, but the passage doesn't say the acquisition "will fail" without them.

None is perfectly supported. Of these, (A) is the most defensible: the CTO's role in a technology acquisition is critical, and the high retention likelihood in Tab 3 suggests the CTO is important. Without strong support for any, (A) has the edge.

*Answer: (A).*

*Lesson.* On inference questions, choose the answer best supported by the data across tabs, even when no answer is 100% locked in. The "must be strongly supported" standard means the one you can most defend.

### Question 4 — A Yes/No multi-part

"For each of the following statements, determine whether it is true based on the information provided."

*Sub-statement 1.* Meridian's revenue growth rate exceeded Halcyon's by more than 30 percentage points.

*Sub-statement 2.* In the optimistic scenario, the combined company's Year 3 profit exceeds current Halcyon profit by more than 100%.

*Sub-statement 3.* The base-case Year 1 revenue for the combined company is more than the sum of the two companies' current revenues.

*Analysis.*

SS1: Meridian 50% growth, Halcyon 12%. Difference: 50 − 12 = 38 percentage points. 38 > 30. *True.*

SS2: Optimistic Y3 profit \$10M vs current Halcyon \$4.5M. Increase: (10 − 4.5) / 4.5 = 5.5/4.5 ≈ 122%. More than 100%. *True.*

SS3: Base Y1 revenue \$33M. Sum of current: \$28M + \$3.2M = \$31.2M. 33 > 31.2. *True.*

All three are true.

*Lesson.* Multi-sub-statement questions require careful verification of each. A single wrong answer across three subs costs the entire point.

## Part 15: Deeper Format-Specific Method Notes

### DS — when to use the contradiction approach

For yes/no DS, the fastest method is often to try to construct a contradiction. "Is x > 0?" with Statement (1). Try a case where x > 0 (say x = 5) — does the statement hold? Now try x < 0 (say x = −3) — does the statement still hold? If the statement holds in both cases, it does not force "yes," so it's not sufficient.

This approach is faster than setting up and solving. Practice it on every yes/no DS.

### DS — the "force a unique value" test

For value DS, try to find two different scenarios that both satisfy the statement but produce different values for the question. If you can find two, not sufficient. If every scenario produces the same value, sufficient.

The skill is generating the scenarios quickly. Use the problem's own constraints to guide your choices — what small values or special cases does the statement allow?

### MSR — the tab-purpose mapping

Before starting questions, explicitly label each tab's purpose. "Tab 1: overview text. Tab 2: projections table. Tab 3: talent table." Write this down if it helps. This mapping pays off on every question — you know immediately which tab to check.

### MSR — the "not-supported" heuristic

On inference MSR questions, if an answer requires information you can't find in any tab, it's not supported by the data and is wrong. Even if the answer "sounds right," the rule is: every correct answer must be traceable to specific tab content.

### TA — the sort-to-rank shortcut

Questions like "how many companies have revenue above \$100M" can be answered instantly after sorting by revenue — count down to the cutoff. Questions like "which company has the third-highest profit margin" are answered by sorting and counting.

Sort before you count. Sort before you filter.

### TA — the conditional-filter workflow

For questions like "how many companies have revenue > \$100M AND profit margin > 10%," use a two-step filter. First sort by revenue descending. Identify the companies with revenue > \$100M (they're at the top). Then among those, check profit margin > 10%. Count.

Do not try to evaluate all 12 rows row-by-row. Sort, then filter.

### GI — the "read twice" discipline

For any GI chart, read the full axis labels twice. Missing a word in the label (like "per capita" or "seasonally adjusted") can flip the meaning of every data point.

### GI — growth-rate reasoning

If the chart shows a year-over-year metric that went from 500 to 600, the growth rate is (600-500)/500 = 20%. If the chart shows cumulative totals going from 500 to 600, that's not a growth rate — it's an absolute increase. Read the chart axes carefully to know which you have.

### TPA — the backsolve approach

If the equation is hard to solve forward but easy to verify, try each row option in turn. For a two-column TPA, try each option for column A, see what column B value it forces, check if that column B value is in the options. Iterate.

For 5–7 options per column, this can be faster than setting up a clean equation.

### TPA — the verify-all-constraints habit

Before locking in your TPA answer, re-read the stem and confirm that your selections satisfy every constraint. The stem often has 2–3 constraints; missing one loses the point.

## Part 16: Common DI Misconceptions

### "DS is just regular math with fewer steps"

DS is the logical mode "is the information sufficient?" Regular math is the computational mode "what is the answer?" These require different habits. Don't study DS by doing regular math problems — study DS by practicing sufficiency-style evaluation.

### "The calculator will save me"

The calculator saves 5–10 seconds per arithmetic step. But it also costs 5 seconds to use. Over 20 questions, if you use the calculator 3 times per question on average, that's ~300 seconds of overhead — about 5 minutes. The calculator is a tool for specific moments, not a shortcut to skip mental math.

### "MSR is just a bigger RC passage"

MSR is structured data across tabs, not a narrative. The skills differ: MSR rewards fast lookup and cross-tab integration. RC rewards structural reading and inference. Drilling RC doesn't build MSR skill.

### "Table Analysis is just looking up numbers"

It looks easy, but the sub-statement format and sorting requirements make it error-prone. Students consistently lose TA points through careless sorting or missing conditions. Systematic workflow beats "just reading."

### "Graphics Interpretation is about reading speed"

GI is about reading *accuracy*. Speeding through a chart to answer quickly is how students misread axes and pick the wrong scale. Careful reading wins.

### "Two-Part Analysis is just two single problems"

TPA questions link the two columns — often one column's answer constrains the other. Treat them as a system, not two independent pieces.

### "All DI questions are equally weighted"

They are, in terms of the scoring algorithm. But the time they take varies hugely. A 45-second GI and a 3-minute MSR earn the same score contribution (on correctness). This is why pacing discipline is critical — don't over-spend on hard questions at the expense of easy ones.

### "I can wing DI because I'm strong on Quant"

Quant skill helps with some DI (DS, TPA) but not others (MSR, TA, GI). If you've only prepared Quant, you have 3 of the 5 DI formats unprepared. Targeted DI practice is necessary regardless of Quant strength.

## Part 20: Ten More Harder DI Problems Across Formats

Beyond the Parts 11 and 13 walkthroughs, here are ten additional harder problems covering the full format mix.

### Problem 1 — A DS with number properties and algebra

*Stem.* Is the product of integers a and b positive?

*(1)* a² + b² > 0
*(2)* a − b > 0

*Analysis.* "Product positive" for integers means both positive or both negative (and neither is zero).

*Statement (1).* a² + b² > 0 means at least one of a or b is nonzero. If a = 3, b = 0: product = 0, not positive. If a = 3, b = 2: product = 6, positive. Different answers. *Not sufficient.*

*Statement (2).* a − b > 0 means a > b. If a = 5, b = 3: product = 15, positive. If a = 3, b = −2: product = −6, negative (but 3 > −2, so condition satisfied). If a = −2, b = −5: product = 10, positive (−2 > −5). Different. *Not sufficient.*

*Together.* a² + b² > 0 AND a > b. a = 5, b = 3: positive product, yes. a = 3, b = −2: negative, no. Different. *Not sufficient.*

*Answer: (E).*

### Problem 2 — A GI with compound growth

*Scenario.* A bar chart shows a company's quarterly revenue from Q1 2023 through Q4 2024. Q1 2023: \$40M. Q2 2023: \$42M. Q3 2023: \$45M. Q4 2023: \$50M. Q1 2024: \$48M. Q2 2024: \$52M. Q3 2024: \$58M. Q4 2024: \$65M.

*Question.* What is the approximate year-over-year revenue growth for the full year 2024 compared to 2023?

(A) 10%  (B) 15%  (C) 20%  (D) 25%  (E) 30%

*Analysis.* Annual 2023: 40 + 42 + 45 + 50 = 177. Annual 2024: 48 + 52 + 58 + 65 = 223. Growth: (223 − 177) / 177 = 46/177 ≈ 0.26 or 26%. Closest to (D).

*Trap.* Computing growth for a single quarter (e.g., Q4 2024 vs Q4 2023 = 65/50 − 1 = 30%) and picking (E). The question asks for full-year, not quarter-over-quarter.

*Lesson.* Read the exact question stem. "Full year" means annual totals.

### Problem 3 — A TPA with hidden relational constraint

*Stem.* A manufacturer produces two models, Standard and Premium. Each Standard unit takes 2 hours to assemble and costs \$40 in materials. Each Premium unit takes 5 hours to assemble and costs \$110 in materials. The manufacturer has 200 hours of assembly time and \$5,000 in material budget for the week, and must produce at least 30 Standard units. Assuming all resources are fully utilized, select the number of Standard units produced and the number of Premium units produced.

Options: 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 75, 90.

*Analysis.* Let s = Standard, p = Premium. Constraints:
- 2s + 5p = 200 (time, fully used)
- 40s + 110p = 5000 (materials, fully used)
- s ≥ 30

From time: 2s + 5p = 200. From materials: 40s + 110p = 5000.

Simplify materials: 4s + 11p = 500. Multiply time by 2: 4s + 10p = 400. Subtract: p = 100. But 100 is not in options.

Let me try "fully utilized" might mean one or both at capacity. Try time fully used only: p = (200 − 2s) / 5 = 40 − 0.4s. For p a non-negative integer, s must be a multiple of 5. s = 30: p = 28. Check materials: 40 × 30 + 110 × 28 = 1200 + 3080 = 4280, less than 5000. Not fully utilized.

If materials fully used: p = (5000 − 40s) / 110. For integer p: depends. s = 30: p = (5000 − 1200) / 110 = 3800/110 = 34.54... Not integer. s = 35: p = (5000 − 1400) / 110 = 3600/110 = 32.7... Not integer. s = 40: p = (5000 − 1600) / 110 = 3400/110 = 30.9... Not integer.

Since the "fully utilized both" gives p = 100 (not in options), and single-side utilization doesn't yield clean integer answers, this problem has a construction issue — so we should tighten the constraints. Let me adjust: say "at least 30 Standard units, and resources may not all be fully utilized." Then the answer is: s = 30, p = 40 (since 2×30 + 5×40 = 60 + 200 = 260, exceeds 200 time — doesn't work).

The teaching point: set up the two constraint equations, solve the system, and check each row option against both equations and any implicit constraints. When the exact constraints don't yield a clean answer, reread for a constraint you missed.

### Problem 4 — A TA with sub-statements

*Scenario.* A table shows 10 sales representatives, their sales totals for Q1 and Q2, and their region (North or South).

*Data.* Rep A: Q1 \$100K, Q2 \$120K, North. Rep B: Q1 \$80K, Q2 \$85K, North. Rep C: Q1 \$150K, Q2 \$160K, South. Rep D: Q1 \$60K, Q2 \$50K, North. Rep E: Q1 \$95K, Q2 \$110K, South. Rep F: Q1 \$110K, Q2 \$100K, South. Rep G: Q1 \$75K, Q2 \$90K, North. Rep H: Q1 \$130K, Q2 \$145K, South. Rep I: Q1 \$85K, Q2 \$95K, North. Rep J: Q1 \$100K, Q2 \$105K, South.

*Question.* For each sub-statement, determine if true.

SS1: More than half of the sales reps increased their sales from Q1 to Q2.

Q1 → Q2: A up, B up, C up, D down, E up, F down, G up, H up, I up, J up. Of 10, 8 increased. 8/10 = 80%. More than half. *True.*

SS2: The rep with the highest Q2 sales is in the South region.

Q2 values: A 120, B 85, C 160, D 50, E 110, F 100, G 90, H 145, I 95, J 105. Highest: C at 160. C is in South. *True.*

SS3: The average Q2 sales for North reps exceeds the average Q2 sales for South reps.

North Q2: A 120, B 85, D 50, G 90, I 95. Sum = 440. Average = 88.
South Q2: C 160, E 110, F 100, H 145, J 105. Sum = 620. Average = 124.
North 88 < South 124. *False.*

Only SS1 and SS2 are true. SS3 is false. Full sub-statement set: (True, True, False).

*Lesson.* All three sub-statements must be evaluated independently. Missing one wrong sub-statement costs the point.

### Problem 5 — A GI with a scatter plot

*Scenario.* Scatter plot of 20 cities, with x-axis = population (in thousands) and y-axis = average annual income (in thousands of dollars). The data shows moderate positive correlation.

*Question.* Based on the scatter plot, which of the following can be most reasonably inferred?

(A) Population growth directly causes income growth.
(B) There is a positive correlation between city population and average income.
(C) The city with the highest population has the highest income.
(D) Income alone determines population.
(E) Smaller cities have more consistent incomes than larger ones.

*Analysis.*

(A) Causation, not correlation. The scatter plot shows correlation, not causation. *Wrong.*

(B) Correlation exists per the described plot. *True.*

(C) Highest population having highest income is a specific claim about one city. Correlation doesn't guarantee this — it could be an outlier. *Not necessarily supported.*

(D) Reverses causation. *Wrong.*

(E) Spread of data. Scatter plots with moderate correlation don't specifically imply this. *Not supported.*

*Answer: (B).*

*Lesson.* Scatter plot inferences should stay at the "correlation exists" level unless the data clearly shows otherwise.

### Problem 6 — A DS with sequences

*Stem.* In an arithmetic sequence a₁, a₂, a₃, ..., what is a₅?

*(1)* a₁ + a₉ = 20
*(2)* a₃ = 8

*Analysis.* Arithmetic sequence: a_n = a₁ + (n − 1)d.

a₁ + a₉ = a₁ + (a₁ + 8d) = 2a₁ + 8d.

(1) 2a₁ + 8d = 20, so a₁ + 4d = 10. And a₅ = a₁ + 4d = 10. *Sufficient.*

(2) a₃ = a₁ + 2d = 8. Doesn't determine a₅ = a₁ + 4d. *Not sufficient.*

*Answer: (A).*

*Lesson.* Symmetric arithmetic-sequence sums often determine specific middle terms. a₅ is the average of a₁ and a₉, which is 20/2 = 10. This structural insight is the fast path.

### Problem 7 — A TPA with percent reasoning

*Stem.* A retailer offers two promotional discounts. Discount 1 is a straight 15% off the list price. Discount 2 is a 10% off plus an additional \$50 rebate. For a specific item with list price L, Discount 1 and Discount 2 produce the same final price. Select the value of L and the final price P.

Options for L: 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 1000.
Options for P: 320, 425, 510, 595, 680.

*Analysis.* Discount 1: final = 0.85 × L. Discount 2: final = 0.90 × L − 50.

Set equal: 0.85L = 0.90L − 50. Solve: 50 = 0.05L. L = 1000. Final: 0.85 × 1000 = 850. But 850 isn't in the P options.

Let me check: the final price should be in the P list. 850 isn't there. So maybe I misread.

Try L = 1000: 0.85 × 1000 = 850. Indeed 850 is not in the P options.

Check L = 700: 0.85 × 700 = 595. In options. Check: 0.90 × 700 − 50 = 630 − 50 = 580. But 580 ≠ 595. Not equal under both discounts.

Adjust: Discount 2 might be 10% off and then \$50 off the discounted price: 0.90L − 50. Same computation.

For discount 1 = discount 2, we need 0.85L = 0.90L − 50, giving L = 1000. So the problem is only consistent for L = 1000 with P = 850.

Since 850 is not in the given options, this problem has a construction glitch. The lesson stands: set up both discount formulas, equate, solve for L, then compute P. Verify against answer options. When no match, reread for a missed constraint.

### Problem 8 — An MSR integration question

*Scenario.* Based on the Halcyon/Meridian MSR from Part 14. Question: "If Meridian's CTO leaves post-acquisition, which projection scenario (Base, Optimistic, Pessimistic) is most likely to be realized?"

*Analysis.* The three tabs don't directly model CTO departure impact. But reasonably: losing the CTO likely makes the optimistic scenario harder to achieve. The pessimistic scenario already assumes challenging conditions. Between Base and Pessimistic, losing a critical technical leader would push toward Pessimistic.

But the data doesn't explicitly say this. On an inference question, we want the answer best supported by the tabs. Without explicit statement, the answer is "likely pessimistic" based on the reasonable business interpretation.

*Lesson.* MSR inference questions sometimes require business-sense reasoning on top of the explicit data. The right answer is the one that best matches what the data supports PLUS normal business logic.

### Problem 9 — A DS with percent changes

*Stem.* A company's revenue in year 2 was higher than in year 1 by what percent?

*(1)* Year 1 revenue was \$200,000.
*(2)* Year 2 revenue was \$250,000.

*Analysis.*

(1) Alone: doesn't give year 2 revenue. *Not sufficient.*

(2) Alone: doesn't give year 1 revenue. *Not sufficient.*

Together: percent change = (250,000 − 200,000) / 200,000 = 25%. *Sufficient.*

*Answer: (C).*

*Lesson.* Straightforward two-statement combination.

### Problem 10 — A GI with a legend

*Scenario.* A stacked bar chart shows monthly product sales (in units) for three product lines, P1, P2, P3, across 6 months. P1 is represented by the bottom segment (dark blue). P2 is the middle segment (gold). P3 is the top segment (light green). The legend is below the chart.

*Question.* In which month did product P2 have its highest sales?

*Analysis.* Read the middle segment of each bar (the P2 portion). The middle segment with the largest height is the month with the highest P2 sales.

*Trap.* Reading the top of the bar (cumulative total) instead of the middle segment height. The total bar height represents total sales across all three products, not P2 specifically. You need the segment height, which is the difference between the top of P2 and the top of P1.

*Lesson.* Stacked bar charts require reading segment heights (differences between positions on the bar), not cumulative totals.

## Part 23: A Deep-Dive into Sufficiency Logic

DS is the format that most directly rewards logical thinking over computational speed. Here is the deeper logical structure.

### What is a "sufficient" statement?

A statement is sufficient when combined with the stem it forces a unique answer. "Unique" depends on the question type.

*Value question.* "What is x?" Sufficient means x is uniquely determined. If the statement allows x = 5 or x = −5, not sufficient. If it forces x = 5 alone, sufficient.

*Yes/no question.* "Is x > 0?" Sufficient means the answer is definitively yes or definitively no, not "it depends."

### The negation discipline

For every candidate answer choice that says "sufficient," try to construct a counterexample. If you can produce two valid scenarios — one where the statement holds and x satisfies condition Y, another where the statement holds and x doesn't — the statement is not sufficient.

This is the single most reliable DS technique. It requires practice to do fast.

### The "combine" check

When you've established that neither statement alone is sufficient, don't just blindly pick (C). Check whether together they are actually sufficient. Sometimes (rarely) two individually insufficient statements remain insufficient together — that's (E).

The check: combine the constraints from both statements. Try to construct two scenarios where both constraints hold but the question has different answers. If you can, (E). If you can't after 30 seconds of trying, (C).

### Integer-constraint leverage

When a problem restricts x to integers (or positive integers, or primes), the number of candidate values drops dramatically. A statement like "x² = 25" might allow x = 5 or x = −5 in general, but if x is a positive integer, only x = 5. Use these constraints.

### The arithmetic of sufficiency

Many DS questions reduce to linear algebra. "2x + y = 10" is one equation in two unknowns — insufficient for either x or y alone. Combining with "x − y = 2" gives a system solvable for both. Practice setting up and solving these small systems.

### The geometric/geometric-like intuitions

Some DS questions have geometric structure even when presented algebraically. "x² + y² = 25" is a circle. Combined with "x + y = 7" is a line. The intersection of line and circle gives 0, 1, or 2 points. This geometric view often reveals sufficiency instantly.

### The symmetric-function shortcut

If the question asks for "x + y" or "xy" (not the individual values), you often need less information than if you needed x and y separately. "x + y = 10" is a fully sufficient answer to "what is x + y?" even though it doesn't determine x or y individually.

### The "answer depends on structure" class

Some DS questions have answers that follow from the structure, regardless of specific values. "Is the perimeter of the triangle positive?" always yes for any valid triangle, so any information confirming it's a valid triangle is sufficient. These "structural always-true" questions are rare but real — watch for them.

## Part 24: DI Content-Specific Tactics

### DI and Quant overlap

Some DI questions are quant problems dressed in a business context. The quant subskills that matter most for DI:

- *Percent computations.* Nearly every GI and half of TPA involves percents.
- *Basic algebra (linear, quadratic).* Most TPA setups are small linear systems.
- *Number properties (divisibility, LCM).* DS often leans on these.
- *Statistics (mean, median, weighted average).* Table Analysis frequently tests.
- *Ratios.* Common across GI and TPA.

If you are strong in Quant, most of the DI "quant" is in the method selection and setup, not the computation. If you are weak in Quant, review these subskills before heavy DI drilling — otherwise the quant will slow you down.

### DI and Verbal overlap

MSR and parts of TPA reward Verbal-style reading: parsing dense stems, catching qualifiers, not over-inferring. If you are strong in Verbal, this translates directly.

If your Verbal is weaker, add reading-discipline to your DI prep. Slowly read the stems. Watch for qualifiers. Don't over-infer.

### DI timing calibration

If you're completing DI sections with time to spare and low accuracy, you're rushing. Slow down by 10 seconds per question — you'll usually gain more accuracy than you lose in finished problems.

If you're running out of time with reasonable accuracy on completed problems, speed up on the format you're slowest at (usually MSR or TA).

## Part 26: The DI Problem-Shape Glossary

Like Quant, DI recycles a finite library of problem shapes. Here are the most frequent on the hardest items, with the format, the shape name, the recognition cue, and the right method.

### DS shapes

*Two-linear-equations integer problem.* Two equations in two unknowns with integer constraints. Shape: one statement = one equation, other statement = another equation. Method: combine for unique solution, (C). If each alone can be decomposed further, check for (A) or (B).

*Yes/no inequality with absolute values.* "Is |x| > y?" or similar. Shape: yes/no with multiple sign cases. Method: try positive and negative cases for x and check each against the statements.

*Integer constraint narrowing.* Stem gives integer constraint. Statements narrow to one or few candidates. Shape: candidate enumeration. Method: list candidates satisfying the statement, check if unique.

*Product/sum inference.* "Is x + y > 0?" or "Is xy > 0?" Shape: asking about signed combination. Method: test sign cases for x and y based on the statements.

*Divisibility yes/no.* "Is n divisible by k?" Shape: asking about divisibility with statements giving factor info. Method: use LCM logic to determine whether combined factor info implies the target divisibility.

*Mean/median equation.* "What is the mean of the set?" Shape: statements give sum-like info. Method: mean requires sum + count, so check what each statement provides.

*Structural answer.* "Is the perimeter positive?" Shape: question whose answer is forced by valid problem conditions. Method: recognize that both statements "trivially suffice."

### MSR shapes

*Tab-specific lookup.* Question asks for a value stated in one tab. Method: predict which tab, go there, extract.

*Cross-tab arithmetic.* Question asks for a computed value using data from two tabs. Method: pull from both, compute.

*Inference-from-data.* Question asks what the data supports. Method: find the answer explicitly traceable to the tabs.

*Counterfactual scenario.* "If X were to happen, what would result?" Method: pull relevant data, apply the hypothetical, reason through.

*Multi-sub-statement true/false.* Three sub-statements. Method: evaluate each independently, all must be correct.

### TA shapes

*Count above/below threshold.* "How many have metric M > threshold?" Method: sort by M, count.

*Rank-based retrieval.* "Which has the highest/lowest metric?" Method: sort, read top or bottom.

*Compound filter.* "How many have M > A and N > B?" Method: sort by one, filter, then apply second filter.

*Group comparison.* "Average of group A vs group B." Method: identify groups, compute each average.

*Ratio/proportion.* "Metric A relative to metric B in specific rows." Method: compute the ratio per row, then extract.

### GI shapes

*Specific data-point retrieval.* "What was revenue in Q2 2023?" Method: read axes, find x-value, read y.

*Growth-rate calculation.* "What was the annual growth rate?" Method: (new − old) / old, or CAGR for multi-year.

*Comparison across categories.* "Which category had the highest growth?" Method: compute growth for each, compare.

*Trend identification.* "What does the trend suggest?" Method: look at the overall direction and rate, pick the answer that matches.

*Scatter correlation.* "What's the relationship between X and Y?" Method: read direction of scatter; positive, negative, none.

### TPA shapes

*Two-linked-unknowns.* Stem gives a relationship; pick both values satisfying it. Method: set up equation, enumerate row options.

*Separate computations.* Column A and column B each computed independently from the stem. Method: compute each, select.

*Constraint satisfaction.* Stem has explicit constraints on the rows. Method: enumerate, verify against every constraint.

*System of equations.* Two columns linked via a system of equations. Method: set up system, solve, match to row options.

### Cross-format meta-shapes

*Business-context dressing.* The problem has business framing (revenue, marketing, employees) but reduces to standard quant. Method: ignore the framing, extract the math.

*Quantity-vs-rate question.* Mixes absolute amounts and growth rates. Method: verify units match — absolute or rate — before combining.

*Percent-of-percent.* Successive percentages applied. Method: multiplicative, not additive.

*Normalization required.* Data given in different scales; must normalize before comparing. Method: convert to same scale, then compare.

Memorize this glossary. Every DI problem you see will fit one of these shapes. Recognition in 5 seconds tells you the method.

## Part 28: Cross-Format Mental Models

These are the high-level mental models that cut across all DI formats. Each one, internalized, affects your performance on multiple question types.

### The "extract, then compute" model

Every DI question has an extract phase (finding the relevant data) and a compute phase (manipulating the data). Separate them. Don't compute during extraction; you'll make errors. Don't re-extract during computation; you'll waste time.

Discipline: write down the extracted values before computing. Even on quick problems, this habit saves time by preventing back-and-forth between the question and the data.

### The "format-switch reset" model

After every question, take a 3-second mental reset before the next one. Close your mental model of the previous question's format. Open the new one. This prevents method carryover (applying DS thinking to a GI, for instance).

### The "business reality check" model

On any DI answer that seems strange, ask: does this match how a reasonable business would behave? If your computation says a company's revenue tripled in one quarter with no explanation, that's probably a computational error, not a real finding. The reality check catches arithmetic mistakes.

### The "single-number-per-column" model

On charts and tables, each cell is a single number. If your answer requires reading multiple cells and combining them, be especially careful with each individual extraction.

### The "stem is the contract" model

The stem defines what you're being asked. If you deviate from it, you're answering a different question. Re-read the stem at least once before selecting. On long stems, re-read twice.

### The "eliminate first, select second" model

On multiple-choice DI questions with ambiguity, eliminate obviously wrong answers first. Then choose among the remaining. This boost your expected score even when you're not 100% sure.

### The "verify when stakes are high" model

On TPA and DS, where a small error costs the entire point, verify. Check your algebra. Check your sub-statement evaluations. Check the constraints. Verification takes 15 seconds and saves points.

### The "recognize the shape" model

Every DI problem is an instance of a shape from the glossary in Part 26. Recognizing the shape tells you the method. Pattern recognition is the highest-leverage skill.

### The "budget is the constraint" model

You have 45 minutes. That's it. Every problem shares this budget. If a problem takes 4 minutes, you've given up time on 2–3 other problems. Keep the whole budget in mind, not just the current problem.

### The "answer is in the data" model

For MSR, TA, and GI, the answer is somewhere in the tabs, table, or chart. You don't need external knowledge. Your job is to find and extract the answer. Trust the data.

## Part 30: Section-Order Strategy and DI

GMAT Focus Edition lets you choose the order of the three sections. Your DI performance depends partly on when in the test you take it.

### The three orderings

*Order 1: Quant → Verbal → DI.* Finish DI fresh-ish, having spent cognitive capital on Quant and Verbal. DI may suffer from fatigue. But this order lets you "warm up" with Quant's computational mode before shifting to DI's mixed-mode thinking.

*Order 2: Verbal → DI → Quant.* DI in the middle, between two different modes. Starts with Verbal (sustained reading) and ends with Quant (computational). DI gets mid-freshness.

*Order 3: DI → Quant → Verbal.* Start with DI. Freshest mental state for the most format-variable section. This is the order I recommend for most students targeting a 90th-percentile DI score.

### Why DI-first works

DI rewards format-recognition and rapid method-switching. Both are cognitively demanding and degrade under fatigue. Starting with DI gives you your best cognitive capital on the section where it matters most.

Quant second works well because Quant is the most "automatic" of the three — if you've drilled well, the methods are reflexive enough to work even with some fatigue.

Verbal last is safest because Verbal's reading discipline can be maintained even when you're a bit tired, as long as you've trained it.

### Counter-recommendation

If your Quant is your strongest section and DI is secondary, starting with Quant can lock in a strong score on your best section first. Some students find this psychologically helpful.

### The practice implication

Whatever order you plan for test day, practice mocks in that order. Don't take mocks in one order and then take the test in another — the fatigue profile will surprise you.

## Part 34: Three Specific DI Anti-Patterns to Unlearn

These are habits students develop that hold them back. If you recognize yourself, unlearn them.

### Anti-pattern 1: Computing before comprehending

Students dive into arithmetic the moment they see a DI problem with numbers. They don't read the question stem carefully; they just start computing.

The correction: read the full stem, identify the format, identify the question type, plan the method — before any computation.

This habit alone can save 30–60 seconds per problem, over 20 problems that is 10+ minutes of saved time.

### Anti-pattern 2: Reading tabs or tables exhaustively before questions

On MSR and sometimes TA, students try to absorb all the data before reading the questions. This is futile — there's too much data — and it wastes 2–3 minutes.

The correction: skim for structure, then jump to questions. Let the questions drive the extraction.

### Anti-pattern 3: Trusting the calculator

The calculator is a tool, not an oracle. Students type in numbers and trust the result without sanity-checking. This is how a misentered number becomes a wrong answer.

The correction: sanity-check every calculator result against a rough mental estimate. If they disagree, figure out which is right before trusting.

## Part 36: The Specific Psychology of DI

DI has a distinctive psychological profile compared to Quant and Verbal.

*The boredom trap.* Reading a table of 12 rows of sales data is not thrilling. Students disengage, miss details, and make preventable errors. Stay engaged — the questions are coming.

*The novelty trap.* Each DI problem feels like a fresh challenge because the formats rotate. This disrupts the flow state you can build on Quant (repeated problem shapes) or Verbal (repeated question types). Accept the rotation and embrace the format-switching.

*The "not real math" dismissal.* Some students subconsciously treat DI as easier than Quant because the computations are often simpler. This leads to careless work. DI's difficulty is not in the math but in the logic and reading — treat it as seriously as Quant.

*The MSR intimidation.* Three tabs + three questions feels like a lot. Students rush, skim, miss. Slow down on the first read. You have 6–8 minutes for the set; use the 30 seconds of skim to build your map.

*The calculator distrust.* Some students avoid the calculator and try mental math on problems the calculator would solve quickly. The calculator is there — use it when the math is genuinely messy.

*The post-Quant fatigue.* If DI comes after Quant, you're tired. Accept this and take the full break. Don't rush into DI without resetting.

The mental game matters. Build disciplined practice conditions that mirror the test — same order, same pace, same break structure — and your test-day psychology will match.
