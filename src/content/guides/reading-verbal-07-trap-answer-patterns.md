# Chapter 2.7 — Verbal Trap Answer Patterns

## Core idea

GMAT Verbal wrong answers are not random. The test engineers a *finite library of trap patterns* across CR and RC, and each wrong answer on every problem is built on one or two of these patterns. Memorize the patterns, and elimination becomes mechanical: scan each answer choice, ask "which trap is this," reject. The skill is *pattern recognition on the wrong answers*, not just on the right one. Strong solvers eliminate four trap answers in twenty seconds and select the survivor.

This chapter is the trap inventory. Read it once to load the patterns into recognition memory, then return to it weekly during your error-log reviews to re-classify mistakes by trap.

(4) Without trap-pattern recognition, every answer choice looks "kind of plausible" and elimination is gut-based — exactly what the test exploits. (5) Reject — the named trap is your reason to eliminate.

## Why it matters

Three reasons trap recognition deserves dedicated treatment.

*Most Verbal mistakes come from selecting trap answers, not missing the right answer*. The student finds the right answer plausible but picks a trap because the trap was *more* plausible to them. The trap was engineered to be more plausible. Recognizing the trap pattern lets you reject what the engineer was selling.

*The patterns repeat across hundreds of problems*. The same trap pattern that catches you on a CR weakening question will catch you on an RC inference question. The vocabulary differs; the underlying error mode is the same. Mastering the trap inventory generalizes broadly.

*Trap recognition compresses the elimination phase*. A student who can name a trap in three seconds eliminates four answers in fifteen seconds, leaving 60-90 seconds for the real comparison between IN and MAYBE. A student who evaluates each answer slowly without trap-pattern recognition runs out of time on hard problems.

## Mental model

Picture trap answers as *engineered counterfeits*. Each one looks like real currency at first glance and reveals itself as fake on closer inspection. The closer the inspection, the more the trap reveals. Strong solvers inspect quickly because they know what the counterfeits look like. Weak solvers inspect slowly and still miss the tells.

The ten patterns in this chapter are the *tells*. Once you can name them, the counterfeits become visible.

## GMAT recognition signals

The ten recurring trap patterns, each with its signature.

*Too broad.* The answer makes a claim wider than the passage supports. "All large companies have this problem" when the passage discussed *some* large companies. The signal: extreme quantifiers — *all, every, none, never, always*.

*Too narrow.* The answer captures one detail or paragraph but misses the broader scope the question asks about. Common on main idea and primary purpose questions. The signal: the answer is *true* but doesn't address what the question is actually after.

*Extreme language.* Stronger language than the passage supports. "The theory is wrong" when the passage said "the theory has limitations." Signal: superlatives, absolutes, strong evaluative language without textual support.

*Reversed logic.* The answer does the opposite of what the question asked. Strengthens when the question said weaken; weakens when the question said strengthen. Signal: the content is relevant but the *direction* is wrong.

*Unsupported.* The answer makes a claim the passage doesn't address one way or the other. Common on inference questions. Signal: when you ask "where does the passage say this," you can't find it.

*Partially right, wrong function.* The answer correctly describes content from the passage but misidentifies its function. "The third paragraph introduces a new theory" when the third paragraph in fact *challenges* a theory. Signal: the content is right; the *role* is wrong.

*Wrong viewpoint.* The answer assigns a position to the wrong party — to the author when the passage actually attributes it to a critic, or vice versa. Signal: the answer correctly captures a position but wrong about who holds it.

*Outside knowledge.* The answer is true in the world but not supported by the passage. Signal: when you ask "where does the passage say this," you can only support it with what *you* know, not what the passage said.

*Distorted comparison.* The answer compares two things in a way the passage didn't. "Theory A is more accurate than Theory B" when the passage compared their *scopes*, not accuracy. Signal: the comparison axis differs between the answer and the passage.

*Causal exaggeration.* The passage suggests correlation or partial causation; the answer claims full or strict causation. "Smoking causes cancer" when the passage said "smoking is a risk factor for cancer." Signal: causation language stronger than the passage's wording.

## Method

A four-step trap-elimination protocol. Run it on every Verbal answer choice.

*Step 1. Read the answer choice carefully.* Don't skim. Words like *all, never, only, most, some, sometimes* matter. Read in full.

*Step 2. Run the trap checklist.* For each answer, ask: is this *too broad*? *Too narrow*? *Extreme*? *Reversed*? *Unsupported*? *Partially right but wrong function*? *Wrong viewpoint*? *Outside knowledge*? *Distorted comparison*? *Causal exaggeration*? Most wrong answers fall under one or two of these. The named trap is your reason to eliminate.

*Step 3. Mark the answer.* IN if it survives the checklist. OUT if it hits a trap. MAYBE if it's borderline.

*Step 4. Compare survivors.* If two answers survive, compare them on the dimensions the question is most sensitive to. Often one is *more directly* responsive to the question; that's usually the right one.

## Common traps

Five meta-traps in trap elimination itself.

*Recognizing the trap but second-guessing yourself.* You correctly identified that an answer is too broad, then you talked yourself into picking it because "it might still be right." The fix: trust your trap recognition. Once you've named the trap, eliminate.

*Failing to name the trap explicitly.* You feel an answer is wrong but can't articulate why. This is risky because your gut may be wrong. Force yourself to name the specific trap. If you can't name it, the answer might actually be correct.

*Eliminating on weak grounds.* "I don't like the wording" is not a reason to eliminate. "This contains extreme language not supported by the passage" is. Use the named trap inventory.

*Picking based on what's familiar.* Picking the answer that uses passage vocabulary because it feels safe. Trap answers often use passage vocabulary deliberately. The right answer often paraphrases. Don't reward familiarity.

*Forgetting to check for half-right.* An answer can be partially right and still wrong. The first half might match the passage perfectly while the second half slips into a trap. Read the *whole* answer choice before judging.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating trap-pattern recognition on a CR strengthening question.

*Argument.* In a recent study of online retailers, those that offered free shipping had higher customer return rates than those that did not. Therefore, free shipping caused customers to spend more.

*Question stem.* Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the conclusion?

*Pre-think.* The conclusion is causal: free shipping causes higher spending. The strengthening answer should rule out alternative causes or establish the causal mechanism.

*Sample answer choices and trap recognition.*

(A) Some online retailers in the study did not advertise their free-shipping policy. — Trap: *unsupported* / *out of scope*. The argument is about whether free shipping caused higher spending; advertising practices are a separate variable not directly addressed.

(B) Customers always prefer free shipping over flat-rate shipping. — Trap: *extreme language* (*always*) and *wrong direction* (preferences are about choice, not spending levels).

(C) Customers who use free shipping also tend to be wealthier on average. — Trap: *causal exaggeration* on the wrong side — this introduces an alternative cause (wealth), which would *weaken*, not strengthen.

(D) When retailers offering free shipping introduced a $5 shipping fee, customer return rates dropped sharply. — IN. This intervention evidence directly supports the causal claim: changing the free-shipping variable changed spending behavior.

(E) Free shipping has been a marketing standard for over a decade. — Trap: *outside knowledge / out of scope*. The history of marketing doesn't bear on the causal question.

*Trap-elimination summary.* (A) is unsupported. (B) is extreme. (C) is reversed direction (would weaken). (E) is out of scope. (D) is the survivor.

*Answer.* (D).

The point of this example is not that (D) is the right answer — it's that *naming each trap explicitly* makes elimination fast. A student who runs the trap checklist eliminates the four wrong answers in 25 seconds. A student who evaluates each answer slowly without the checklist might spend a minute on each.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Too broad vs. right scope

*Setup.* RC main-idea question. Passage discusses the impact of a *specific 2018 regulatory change* on the banking industry. Choice (A): "Government regulation has shaped the banking industry over the past century." Choice (B): "A 2018 regulatory change has had specific consequences for the banking industry."

*Thinking process.* (A) is true and topical, but generic — fits any passage on banking regulation, not this specific one. (B) is calibrated to what the passage actually argues.

*Solution.* (B).

*Common mistake.* Picking (A) because it sounds reasonable and substantive. Trap subtype: too broad — fits passages on the topic generally, doesn't capture this passage's specific argument.

*Takeaway.* On main-idea questions, ask "is this answer distinctive to *this* passage, or could it describe many passages on the topic?" Distinctive matches.

### Example 2 — Reversed direction

*Setup.* CR strengthen question. Argument: "The new training program improved employee performance." Candidate answer: "Some employees who completed the training did not subsequently improve performance metrics."

*Thinking process.* The candidate provides counter-evidence — employees who didn't improve. That weakens the argument, not strengthens it. Direction is reversed.

*Solution.* OUT. The answer would weaken if the question had asked for a weakener; it doesn't strengthen.

*Common mistake.* Picking it because the candidate is "about" the training program and "about" performance. Reading for direction, not just topic-match, is the discipline.

*Takeaway.* Always ask: "does this answer *strengthen* or *weaken*?" Topic-match alone doesn't tell you direction. Many trap answers are reversed-direction matches.

### Example 3 — Wrong viewpoint attribution

*Setup.* RC author-attitude question about the policy's critics. Passage: "Critics argue the policy is short-sighted; however, in my view, the policy reflects a careful balance of competing interests." Candidate: "The author shares the critics' view that the policy is short-sighted."

*Thinking process.* The "however, in my view..." signals the author *disagrees* with the critics. The critics hold one view; the author holds another. The candidate misattributes the critics' position to the author.

*Solution.* OUT. The author's view is opposite the critics'.

*Common mistake.* Picking the candidate because the words "short-sighted" appeared in the passage. But the words appeared *attributed to the critics*, not as the author's claim.

*Takeaway.* Always check attribution markers. A position can appear in a passage without the author endorsing it. Sometimes the passage explicitly *opposes* the position it presents.
