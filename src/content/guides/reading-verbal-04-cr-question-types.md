# Chapter 2.4 — CR Question Types

## Core idea

Critical Reasoning has *nine recurring question types*, and each one has its own right-answer shape, its own trap patterns, and its own pre-thinking framework. The four-step framework from Chapter 2.3 is the same for every type, but Step 3 — the pre-think — looks different depending on the task. This chapter goes through each type in turn, gives the recognition signals from the question stem, names the right-answer shape, and catalogs the traps each type uses most.

Memorize this chapter. The recognition signals should compress to two-second judgments. Once you see "weaken" in the stem, you should immediately know *the right answer must undercut the bridge between evidence and conclusion*, and you should be scanning the stimulus for that bridge before you've finished reading the question.

### Quick check

4. The right-answer shape is determined by what?
5. Why is type-recognition the foundation for the entire CR section?

*Answers.* (1) Nine — strengthen, weaken, assumption, inference, evaluate, paradox, method, role, boldface. (2) Pre-think looks different depending on the task. The framework is constant; the pre-think shape is task-specific. (3) The task type from the question stem (e.g., "would weaken" → weaken type) and the right-answer shape (e.g., "alternative cause for the correlation"). (4) The task — strengtheners add support; weakeners undercut bridges; inferences must follow; etc. (5) Without type-recognition in 2-3 seconds, you read the stimulus generically and miss the targeted filter that makes pre-thinking discriminative.

## Why it matters

Three reasons each question type deserves its own treatment.

*The right-answer shapes are distinct*. A strengthening answer adds support; a weakening answer subtracts it. An assumption answer names the implicit bridge; an inference answer derives a new claim from the stimulus. These are different operations. Treating them as one operation produces task-confusion errors.

*The trap patterns are type-specific*. Each question type has characteristic wrong answers — out-of-scope on assumption questions, over-inference on inference questions, opposite-direction on strengthen/weaken. Knowing the type-specific traps lets you eliminate faster.

*The CR section is roughly 35-45% of Verbal*. Two to three CR questions per Verbal section, with the section having about ten total Verbal questions. CR competence is high-leverage.

## Mental model

Think of the question types as *nine different surgeries on the argument's bridge*. Strengthen reinforces a beam. Weaken removes a beam or undercuts the foundation. Assumption asks you to name a load-bearing beam that wasn't visible. Inference asks you to extend a new beam from existing structure. Evaluate asks what test would tell you if the bridge is sound. Explain a paradox asks how two seemingly contradictory facts can both be true. Method asks what construction technique the author used. Role asks what function a specific beam plays. Boldface asks the same about a marked sentence.

Each surgery has its own technique. Match the surgery to the question stem; deploy the technique.

## GMAT recognition signals

Each type announces itself in the question stem. Memorize the standard phrasings.

*Strengthen.* "Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the argument above?" Or: "Which of the following provides the strongest support for the conclusion?" Recognition: any "strengthen" or "support" verb directed at the conclusion.

*Weaken.* "Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the argument above?" Or: "Which of the following, if true, would most call into question the conclusion?" Recognition: weaken, undermine, cast doubt, call into question.

*Assumption.* "The argument depends on which of the following assumptions?" Or: "Which of the following is an assumption required by the argument?" Recognition: any "assumption" vocabulary, often paired with "required" or "depends on."

*Inference.* "Which of the following can be most reasonably inferred from the passage above?" Or: "If the statements above are true, which of the following must also be true?" Recognition: "inferred," "follows," "must be true," "implies."

*Evaluate.* "Which of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the argument?" Or: "The answer to which of the following questions would be most relevant in evaluating the conclusion?" Recognition: "evaluate," "useful to know," "most relevant."

*Explain the paradox.* "Which of the following, if true, would best explain the apparent paradox?" Or: "Which of the following best resolves the apparent contradiction?" Recognition: "paradox," "discrepancy," "contradiction," "explain," "resolve."

*Method of reasoning.* "The argument proceeds by..." Or: "Which of the following best describes the method of reasoning used in the argument?" Recognition: "method," "technique," "proceeds by."

*Role / function.* "What role does the statement [X] play in the argument?" Or: "The statement [X] serves which of the following functions?" Recognition: "role," "function," "purpose."

*Boldface.* Two parts of the argument are bolded; the question asks what role each plays. Recognition: bolded text in the stimulus.

## Method

The right-answer shape for each type, with the pre-thinking framework and the trap subtypes that catch most wrong answers. The major types (strengthen, weaken, assumption, inference) get the deepest treatment because they account for most of the CR section; the minor types are still on the test and worth knowing cold.

### Strengthen

*Right-answer shape.* Adds support to the conclusion. Three common forms appear repeatedly:

1. *Rules out an alternative explanation* for the evidence. The argument claims X causes Y; the answer rules out a confounding variable that would have explained the correlation without X causing Y.
2. *Provides a mechanism* connecting the evidence to the conclusion. The argument shows X and Y co-occur; the answer offers the causal pathway by which X would produce Y.
3. *Adds new supporting evidence* of the same type. A second, independent observation that points the same direction as the existing evidence.

*Pre-think.* "What gap in the argument would close if I added the right fact? What alternative explanation is the conclusion vulnerable to, and what would rule it out?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Strengthens a premise, not the conclusion.* The answer makes the argument's evidence more credible (e.g., "the study had a large sample size") but doesn't strengthen the inference from evidence to conclusion.
- *Strengthens a related-but-different claim.* Plausibly supports a slightly more general or more specific version of the conclusion, but not the conclusion as stated.
- *Provides irrelevant supportive context.* Sounds positive about the argument's topic but doesn't engage with the specific bridge.
- *Establishes a positive correlation that isn't causation.* If the conclusion is causal, additional correlation evidence does not strengthen — it restates what the argument already had.

*Prototype.* "An internal review found that engineers who attend the firm's optional architecture-review meetings author more high-impact code commits than engineers who do not; therefore attending architecture-review meetings causes engineers to write higher-impact code." A strengthening answer: "When engineers who had previously not attended the meetings began attending regularly, the rate at which they authored high-impact commits subsequently rose over the following year." Why it strengthens: intervention evidence — engineers changing their attendance behavior produced changes in commit quality — rules out reverse causation (it isn't that already-strong engineers chose to attend) and rules out selection effects (it isn't that some pre-existing trait made them both attend and write high-impact code).

### Weaken

*Right-answer shape.* Undercuts the bridge between evidence and conclusion. Five recurring forms:

1. *Alternative cause.* A third variable explains both the evidence and the supposed effect.
2. *Reverse causation.* The conclusion's causal arrow is backwards.
3. *Unrepresentative sample.* The data does not generalize to the population the conclusion claims.
4. *Counter-evidence.* New data contradicts the existing data.
5. *Hidden assumption is false.* The argument's bridge assumption turns out not to hold in the relevant case.

*Pre-think.* "What specific alternative explanation, hidden flaw, or counter-fact would expose the bridge as weak?" The more specific your pre-think, the faster elimination becomes.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Contradicts a premise, not the conclusion.* Challenges the evidence (e.g., "the study was small") rather than the inference from evidence to conclusion.
- *Reverses direction.* On a quick read sounds negative, but a careful read shows it actually strengthens. (E.g., naming a *mechanism* by which the conclusion's claim could be true reads as "and here's why X causes Y," which is a strengthener.)
- *Out-of-scope negative.* The answer says something negative about a related topic but doesn't engage with the argument's bridge.
- *Claim is true but irrelevant.* The negative claim is true in the world but doesn't bear on the specific inference being made.

*Prototype.* "Companies that adopted a four-day workweek over the past five years saw employee retention rates rise; therefore the four-day workweek causes higher retention." Weakening answer: "Most companies that adopted the four-day workweek were already industries (e.g., software, professional services) experiencing rising retention rates across the same period regardless of workweek policy." Why it weakens: introduces a confounding variable (industry-specific labor-market conditions) that explains the correlation without requiring the workweek policy to be the cause.

### Assumption

*Right-answer shape.* The *unstated* claim the argument requires to be true. Without this claim, the inference from evidence to conclusion fails. With it, the argument holds.

*Pre-think.* "What is the author assuming but not saying? What load-bearing support sits beneath the bridge from evidence to conclusion?"

*The negation test — the single most reliable assumption-question discipline.* Take each candidate answer and negate it. If the negation *destroys* the argument, the answer is a required assumption. If the negation leaves the argument intact, the candidate isn't required, even if it's consistent with the argument.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Consistent with the argument but not required.* The answer is something that could be true given the argument, but isn't necessary for it. Negation leaves the argument standing.
- *Too strong.* States the assumption in stronger form than the argument needs ("all" when "most" suffices, "always" when "in this case" suffices). Often the "weaker" version of the same statement is the right answer.
- *Restates a premise.* Paraphrases evidence the argument already gave; not an unstated assumption.
- *States the conclusion in disguise.* Sounds like an assumption but is essentially the conclusion under different wording.
- *Addresses an irrelevant possibility.* Rules out an alternative the argument never had to worry about.

*Prototype.* "A revamped onboarding process at one regional office increased new-hire productivity by 25% in the first six months; therefore rolling the same onboarding process out to all regional offices will increase new-hire productivity company-wide." Required assumption: "The other regional offices' new hires resemble the test office's new hires in the relevant respects (role, experience level, training requirements)." Negate it: other offices' new hires are systematically different — say, they handle different functions or have different baseline training — so the productivity gain wouldn't transfer. Argument destroyed. The negation test confirms the assumption is required.

### Inference

*Right-answer shape.* A claim that *must follow* from the evidence and reasoning of the passage, even if not stated explicitly. The bar is *must*, not *could* and not *probably*.

*Pre-think.* "What additional claim is *logically forced* by what the passage establishes?" Specificity helps: an inference can usually be derived in one logical step from the stated information.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Could be true.* The passage doesn't rule it out, but doesn't require it either. Almost all over-inferences are this type.
- *Adds unstated reasoning.* The answer requires combining the passage with an inference the passage didn't make.
- *Too strong.* The passage suggests a tendency or partial relationship; the answer claims a certainty or universal.
- *Outside the passage.* The claim is true in the world but not derivable from the passage's text alone.
- *Confuses sufficient with necessary.* "If X, then Y" is misread as "if Y, then X" or as "Y only if X."
- *Mistakes the part for the whole.* The passage establishes something about a subset; the answer generalizes to the full population.

*Prototype.* Passage: "The price difference between local-ingredient and imported-ingredient restaurants accounts for half the satisfaction gap between them." Required inference: "Even controlling for price, local-ingredient restaurants have higher customer satisfaction." Why it follows: if half the gap is price, then half the gap is *not* price; that residual gap is what controlling-for-price would isolate.

### Evaluate

*Right-answer shape.* A *question* whose answer would either strengthen or weaken the argument depending on which way the answer goes. The right candidate is diagnostic — knowing the answer would shift your confidence in the conclusion meaningfully.

*Pre-think.* "What unknown, if known, would tell me whether the argument is sound? What variable, if I learned its value, would change my view?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Question whose answer doesn't change the argument's validity.* The information requested is irrelevant to the inference at hand.
- *Question about an already-stated fact.* Asks something the passage already told you; restates rather than tests.
- *Question that's too narrow.* Tests a peripheral aspect that doesn't bear on the central inference.
- *Question whose answer is one-sided.* Either yes or no would strengthen (or both would weaken); not actually diagnostic.

### Explain the paradox

*Right-answer shape.* A fact that *resolves both apparently contradictory observations*. Both observations stay true; the answer reveals why they coexist without contradiction.

*Pre-think.* "What additional fact, if true, would make both observations consistent — without denying either one?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Resolves only one observation.* Explains why one is true but doesn't address the other.
- *Contradicts one of the observations.* The "explanation" actually denies one of the stated facts.
- *Unrelated supplementary fact.* True and topical, but doesn't bear on the contradiction itself.
- *Restates the paradox in new words.* Sounds like an explanation but is really a re-description.

*Prototype.* Paradox: "Sales of brand X grew sharply this year, yet brand X's market share declined." Resolution: "The overall market for the product category grew at a faster rate than brand X's sales did." Both facts remain true; the apparent contradiction dissolves once you recognize sales and share have different denominators.

### Method of reasoning

*Right-answer shape.* An *abstract* description of how the argument structures its inference — the logical move from evidence to conclusion, stripped of the specific topic.

*Pre-think.* "Stripped of the topic, what kind of reasoning move did the author make? Generalization from a sample? Causal inference from correlation? Analogy from a parallel case? Process of elimination? Refutation of an opposing view?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Misidentifies the conclusion.* Describes the argument as if a premise were the conclusion, or vice versa.
- *Describes a different argument structure.* True of some argument, not this one.
- *Right move, wrong direction.* Describes the inference as the inverse of what was actually done (e.g., "general to specific" when the argument went specific to general).
- *Mismatches scope.* Describes a narrow move when the argument made a broad one, or vice versa.

### Role / function

*Right-answer shape.* A description of what specific *role* a designated sentence or claim plays in the argument's structure. The five common roles: main conclusion, evidence supporting the conclusion, intermediate conclusion, counterpoint the author opposes, qualification or background.

*Pre-think.* "Is this sentence the main conclusion, supporting evidence, an intermediate step, a view the author is rejecting, or framing context?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Right content, wrong function.* Describes accurately what the sentence says, but misidentifies why the author included it.
- *Treats counterpoint as author's view.* Confuses a claim the passage presents (perhaps to refute) with one the author endorses.
- *Calls a premise a conclusion.* The most common role-confusion error.
- *Treats background as evidence.* Mistakes scene-setting for active support of the inference.

### Boldface

*Right-answer shape.* Two role-descriptions — one for each bolded portion. You're solving role/function twice on the same passage, with the constraint that both descriptions must fit.

*Pre-think.* "Identify each bolded part's role independently. What role does the first bolded portion play? The second?"

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Gets one role right and one wrong.* The most common boldface mistake; one description fits, the other doesn't. Test each portion independently.
- *Reverses the two role-assignments.* Both descriptions are correct in the abstract, but they're assigned to the wrong bolded portions.
- *Both descriptions plausible but neither fits this argument.* "What could be true of some argument" matches, but not what's true of this one specifically.

The boldface format rewards careful, independent role-mapping. Read each bolded portion alone, decide its role, then evaluate which answer choice matches *both* roles correctly.

## Common traps

Six recurring CR-type traps that cut across multiple types.

*Out-of-scope.* The answer is true, or relevant, or even interesting — but doesn't engage with the argument's specific structure. Fix: ask "does this answer touch the conclusion or the bridge to it?"

*Reverse direction.* The answer does the opposite of what the question asked. Strengthens when you needed weaken, or vice versa. Fix: re-read the question stem if you're confused.

*Extreme language.* "Always," "never," "all," "none," "only" are usually too strong unless the argument explicitly supports that strength. Fix: scan answer choices for extreme quantifiers and demand explicit support.

*Half-right.* The answer addresses one part of the argument but not the relevant part. Fix: ask "does this answer address the *exact* link the question is testing?"

*Outside knowledge.* The answer is true in the world but not supported by the argument. Fix: only use what the passage provides.

*Wrong scale or scope.* The answer applies to a different time period, a different population, a different domain. Fix: match the answer's scope to the argument's scope.

The answer's content is relevant but the direction is wrong. (4) An answer with words like "always," "never," "all," "must" — usually too strong unless explicitly supported by the passage. (5) They're true in the world but not supported by the passage. Verbal answers must be supported by what's stated; outside knowledge doesn't qualify.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating type recognition and pre-thinking on an assumption question.

*Stimulus.* A regional logistics firm tested a route-optimization algorithm at one of its distribution centers and saw a 15% reduction in average daily fuel consumption per vehicle. The firm's operations director concluded that deploying the algorithm at all of the firm's other distribution centers would meaningfully reduce the firm's overall fuel costs.

*Question stem.* The argument depends on which of the following assumptions?

*Step 1 — Task.* Assumption. Right-answer shape: an unstated claim required for the argument to follow.

*Step 2 — Skeleton.* Conclusion: deploying the algorithm at the firm's other centers will meaningfully reduce overall fuel costs. Evidence: at one center, the algorithm produced a 15% per-vehicle fuel reduction. Generalization argument: from one center to all centers.

*Step 3 — Pre-think.* The argument generalizes from a single test site to the firm's whole network. The required assumption must bridge that generalization — most plausibly that the *other distribution centers operate under conditions similar enough* to the test center that they would respond comparably to the algorithm. Without that bridge, the test result might not transfer.

*Step 4 — Evaluate sample answer choices.*

(A) The other distribution centers operate vehicles similar in fuel-efficiency profile to those at the test center. — Test by negation: if the other centers' vehicles have very different fuel profiles, the algorithm's effect might not transfer. The argument's projection collapses. STRONG IN.

(B) The route-optimization algorithm requires significant computing resources to operate. — Negation makes the algorithm cheaper to run, which would only make deployment easier — doesn't bridge the generalization. OUT.

(C) The test center has the highest baseline fuel consumption of any of the firm's centers. — Negation makes the test center either average or below average; the 15% reduction could still apply elsewhere. The negation does not destroy the argument. OUT.

(D) Drivers at the other distribution centers will adopt the algorithm's recommended routes when prompted. — Negation: drivers at other centers ignore the algorithm. Then no fuel savings would materialize there. The argument's conclusion collapses. STRONG MAYBE — the argument assumes the algorithm will actually be used, not just installed.

(E) The 15% reduction at the test center occurred over at least a six-month period. — Time-frame detail. The argument's conclusion stands regardless of how quickly the test result was measured. OUT.

*Comparing IN to MAYBE.* (A) and (D) both pass the negation test. The question is which is more *fundamentally* required. (A) bridges *whether the algorithm could work* at other centers — the structural similarity assumption. (D) bridges *whether the algorithm will be applied* at other centers — the deployment assumption. Both are required, but (A) is the more direct generalization bridge: if conditions don't transfer, even perfect deployment wouldn't reproduce the savings.

*Answer.* (A).

*Trap to avoid.* The trap on this kind of problem is picking (D) on a quick read because it sounds operationally important, without recognizing that the argument is fundamentally about whether the *technical* result transfers to similar systems. (D) is a deployment-mechanics assumption; (A) is a transfer-of-technical-effect assumption. Generalization arguments hinge on the transfer assumption first.

*Time spent.* About 90 seconds.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Strengthen vs. supports a premise

*Setup.* Argument: "Companies that adopted flexible scheduling saw productivity rise 8%; therefore flexible scheduling causes productivity gains." A strengthen question. Two candidate answers: (A) "The companies in the study were independently audited to verify their productivity numbers." (B) "When companies that had not adopted flexible scheduling later did, their productivity also rose."

*Thinking process.* (A) makes the *evidence* more credible (the productivity numbers are reliable) but doesn't strengthen the inference from evidence to conclusion. (B) provides intervention evidence that flexible scheduling is causal — directly strengthens the conclusion.

*Solution.* (B). Strengtheners must engage with the bridge from evidence to conclusion, not just confirm the evidence.

*Common mistake.* Picking (A) because it's positively-toned about the data. The trap subtype: strengthens a premise, not the conclusion.

*Takeaway.* On strengthen questions, ask "does this answer support the *inference*, or just the *evidence*?" Only the inference-supporters are strengtheners.

### Example 2 — Negation test on assumption

*Setup.* Argument: "The CEO's pay grew 12% last year while company revenue grew only 4%; therefore the CEO is overpaid." Assumption question. Candidate answer: "The CEO's pay should grow at roughly the same rate as company revenue."

*Thinking process.* Negate it: the CEO's pay should *not* grow at the same rate as revenue. If true, the argument's reasoning collapses (pay growth could exceed revenue growth without indicating overpayment). The negation destroys the argument, so the candidate is a required assumption.

*Solution.* Yes — required assumption.

*Common mistake.* Skipping the negation test and picking based on whether the candidate "sounds true." Many true-sounding statements aren't required by the argument; they're just consistent with it.

*Takeaway.* On every assumption candidate, run the negation test. If negation destroys the argument, it's required. If negation leaves the argument intact, it's not.

### Example 3 — Inference: must vs. could

*Setup.* Passage states: "The technology has been adopted by some Fortune 500 companies, and adoption rates have grown each year for the past three years." Inference question. Candidate: "All Fortune 500 companies will eventually adopt the technology."

*Thinking process.* "All... will eventually" is a strong claim. The passage establishes *some* have adopted and adoption is *growing*. Neither implies *all* will eventually. The candidate is a possible extrapolation but not a logical necessity.

*Solution.* OUT. Could be true; doesn't *have to* be true.

*Common mistake.* Treating "growing each year" as implying eventual full adoption. Trends often plateau or reverse; "growing" doesn't logically force "all eventually."

*Takeaway.* On inference questions, hold rigorously to "must follow." Trends, suggestions, and extrapolations are not entailments.
