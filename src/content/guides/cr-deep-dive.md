## The CR Meta-Model

Before the per-type deep dives, a quick meta-model of what CR is and what it rewards.

### What CR tests

CR measures your ability to analyze an argument's logical structure at speed. Every CR argument has a conclusion, one or more premises, and (usually) an unstated assumption that bridges the gap between them. Your job is to read the argument, identify its structure in 30 seconds, identify the question type, and produce the answer that correctly relates to that structure.

Above the 70th percentile, CR is not about reading speed or vocabulary. It is about *argument structure fluency*. A student who can look at an argument and in 10 seconds label the conclusion, the premises, and the gap — that student has the foundation. Everything else builds on it.

### The three hardest things about CR

*First,* catching qualifiers. "Most," "some," "a few," "usually" change the logical weight of a statement. Skimming past them is fatal.

*Second,* pre-thinking before reading answers. The moment you read the answer choices without having predicted what the right one should look like, you are vulnerable to trap answers that "sound right."

*Third,* matching answer scope to argument scope. The correct answer's claim should match the argument's claim in scope — not broader, not narrower. Students consistently pick answers that are too strong or too extended.

### The decision tree for every CR question

1. Read the argument carefully. Identify conclusion, premises, and the gap between them.
2. Identify the question type from the stem.
3. Pre-think the answer the question type expects.
4. Scan the answer choices. Eliminate ones that don't match your prediction or obvious type-specific traps.
5. Pick among the survivors. If more than one seems plausible, apply the scope and language matching test.

This tree runs in 15–30 seconds after the initial read. Practice it until reflexive.

### The nine question types, by frequency

1. *Strengthen* — ~18%. The most common type.
2. *Weaken* — ~15%.

Prioritize your study time by frequency. Master the top four, and you've covered about 57% of all CR questions.

## Part 1: Strengthen — Deep Treatment

Strengthen is the most common CR type. The question asks which of five answer choices, if added to the argument, would most increase the argument's support for the conclusion.

### What "strengthen" means precisely

A strengthener closes the gap between premises and conclusion. It makes the conclusion more likely to follow from the given evidence. It does not have to prove the conclusion — just increase its plausibility relative to the current state.

Critically, the strengthener does not have to be the "best possible" strengthener in the world. It has to be the strongest among the five given. If an answer choice weakly supports the conclusion and the other four are either neutral or weakening, the weak support is the correct answer.

### The strengthen decision tree

1. Identify the conclusion and premises.
2. Identify the gap — the unstated assumption.
3. Predict: what would, if true, make the argument stronger? (This is usually something that closes the specific gap.)
4. Scan answer choices for matches. Eliminate choices that:
   - Are out of scope (introduce a topic the argument doesn't address)
   - Weaken the argument (wrong direction)
   - Are irrelevant (don't affect the argument's gap either way)
5. Among remaining, pick the one that closes the gap most directly.

### Common strengthen gaps

*Causal argument gap.* "A and B are correlated; therefore A causes B." Strengthen by: ruling out reverse causation, ruling out a confounding variable, or showing the proposed causal mechanism is plausible.

*Prediction gap.* "X happened in the past; therefore X will happen in the future." Strengthen by: showing the underlying conditions persist.

*Generalization gap.* "Sample shows X; therefore population shows X." Strengthen by: showing the sample is representative.

*Policy-from-evidence gap.* "Data shows X; therefore policy Y should be adopted." Strengthen by: showing policy Y specifically addresses what the data shows.

*Analogy gap.* "Situation A has property P; situation B is like A; therefore B has P." Strengthen by: showing A and B are similar in the relevant respect.

### Strengthen worked example 1 — a causal argument

*Argument.* "A 10-year study found that employees who work at companies with on-site gyms are 30% less likely to experience chronic back pain than employees at companies without on-site gyms. Therefore, providing on-site gyms reduces employees' risk of chronic back pain."

*Gap analysis.* This is correlation-as-causation. The correlation could be explained by: reverse causation (healthier employees choose companies with gyms), selection bias (gym-providing companies are more likely to have other wellness features that actually prevent back pain), or confounding (these are typically wealthier companies with better ergonomic workspaces).

*Pre-thought strengthener.* Something that rules out one or more of the alternatives. "The companies were matched on ergonomics, wellness programs, and employee demographics, and the effect persisted" would close most gaps.

*Answer choices (hypothetical).*
(A) On-site gyms typically include a variety of exercise equipment, including stretching areas.
(B) Companies with on-site gyms tend to have higher employee retention rates than companies without them.
(C) The study controlled for employees' age, weight, occupation type, and preexisting back conditions, and the 30% difference persisted.
(D) Employees who use on-site gyms report higher job satisfaction than employees who do not.
(E) Back pain is the most common cause of workplace absenteeism in many industries.

*Analysis.*

(A) Provides detail about gyms but doesn't close the causation gap. Out of direct scope.

(B) Retention rate is a related but separate outcome; doesn't strengthen the back-pain claim.

(C) Directly rules out several confounds (age, weight, occupation, prior conditions) and shows the effect persists. Closes the gap strongly.

(D) Job satisfaction is a different outcome.

(E) Provides general context about back pain but doesn't strengthen the specific causal claim.

*Answer: (C).*

### Strengthen worked example 2 — a prediction argument

*Argument.* "For the past five years, City X's property tax revenues have grown by approximately 5% per year. City officials are confident that next year's property tax revenues will also grow by approximately 5%."

*Gap analysis.* Prediction from precedent. The assumption is that the factors driving past growth will persist. Risks: population might be declining, property values might be peaking, tax rates might change.

*Pre-thought strengthener.* Something that confirms the underlying drivers persist.

*Answer choices.*
(A) City X's population has been stable for the past decade and is projected to remain stable.
(B) Property tax rates are set annually by the city council.
(C) Nationwide, property tax revenues grew by an average of 4% last year.
(D) City X has recently approved construction of several new residential developments that will be completed next year.
(E) City X has historically adjusted tax rates to compensate for growth variance.

*Analysis.*

(A) Stable population supports stable-to-growing property values, which supports continued revenue growth. Strengthens.

(B) Tax rates are set annually — this could go either way and doesn't support the specific 5% projection.

(C) National trend is context but doesn't strengthen the City X projection.

(D) New developments = new properties = new tax revenue. This is a specific reason to expect growth continuation. Also strengthens, possibly more directly than (A).

(E) Adjusting rates for variance could mean the 5% is engineered rather than organic — ambiguous effect.

Between (A) and (D), (D) is the stronger strengthener because it gives a concrete reason for growth, not just absence of decline. *Answer: (D).*

### Strengthen worked example 3 — a policy argument

*Argument.* "To reduce traffic congestion during rush hour, the city should implement a congestion pricing scheme charging drivers \$5 per entry into the downtown area during peak hours. This policy has been shown to reduce congestion in cities of similar size."

*Gap analysis.* Policy-from-evidence. The argument uses evidence from similar-size cities. The gap: is this city actually similar in the relevant way? And will the specific implementation work here?

*Pre-thought strengthener.* Something confirming the city is comparable or the implementation will work.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Congestion pricing is popular with commuters in other cities.
(B) The city has already invested in necessary infrastructure (sensors, signage) to implement the scheme.
(C) Drivers in the city have demonstrated willingness to pay tolls on highways.
(D) The city's traffic congestion is similar in magnitude and pattern to cities where congestion pricing has succeeded.
(E) Other cities that implemented congestion pricing saw a 20% reduction in downtown traffic.

*Analysis.*

(A) Popularity is not relevance to effectiveness.

(B) Infrastructure readiness is about implementation feasibility, not whether the pricing will reduce congestion.

(C) Toll-paying willingness is related but tangential.

(D) Directly closes the comparability gap — confirms the analogy.

(E) Repeats the original evidence, doesn't add new support.

*Answer: (D).*

### Strengthen worked example 4 — an analogy argument

*Argument.* "Country X's recent decision to enact mandatory universal health insurance is likely to succeed. In Country Y, which has a similar economy and population size, mandatory universal health insurance was implemented five years ago and has significantly reduced healthcare costs."

*Gap analysis.* Analogy. The gap: are X and Y similar in the relevant respect? Economy and population size are mentioned, but other factors (existing healthcare infrastructure, cultural attitudes toward insurance, political stability) might matter more.

*Pre-thought strengthener.* Something confirming more relevant similarities.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Both countries have similar life expectancy and birth rates.
(B) Country X has a higher GDP per capita than Country Y.
(C) Both countries have comparable healthcare infrastructure and political support for insurance reform.
(D) Country Y saw a 10% cost reduction in year one of implementation.
(E) Mandatory insurance schemes generally reduce overall healthcare costs.

*Analysis.*

(A) Demographic similarity is relevant but secondary.

(B) GDP difference, if anything, weakens the analogy.

(C) Directly addresses the most relevant similarities (infrastructure, political support) for whether the policy will succeed.

(D) Reinforces Country Y's success but doesn't address X's similarity.

(E) General claim about insurance schemes, not the specific X-Y analogy.

*Answer: (C).*

### Strengthen worked example 5 — a subtle case

*Argument.* "A study of 500 college students found that students who sleep at least 8 hours per night scored on average 15% higher on exams than students who sleep less than 6 hours per night. Therefore, college administrators should encourage students to sleep at least 8 hours per night."

*Gap analysis.* The correlation between sleep and scores could be driven by: more conscientious students happening to sleep more, or students with less demanding coursework having more sleep time, or pre-existing cognitive ability being correlated with sleep habits.

*Pre-thought strengthener.* Something controlling for these alternatives.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Colleges that have implemented sleep-awareness programs have seen a reduction in student stress.
(B) A follow-up study in which students were randomly assigned to sleep schedules found that those assigned to 8+ hours scored 10% higher.
(C) Students who study more tend to sleep less.
(D) Sleep deprivation has been linked to poor physical health.
(E) Most college students report getting less than 7 hours of sleep per night.

*Analysis.*

(A) Stress reduction is related but secondary to the score claim.

(B) Randomized assignment rules out self-selection — a strong strengthener. If assigned students (not self-selected) still show the effect, the causal claim is much more robust.

(C) This introduces a potential confound (study time) without resolving it.

(D) Related but not specific to score performance.

(E) Context about prevalence, not the causal claim.

*Answer: (B).* Randomization is the gold standard for causal claims; this answer directly addresses the gap.

### Strengthen traps, expanded

*The "explanation of premise" trap.* An answer that explains why one of the premises is true. This is not a strengthener — the argument already treats the premise as given. The strengthener should support the conclusion, not the premise.

*The "detail about a term" trap.* An answer that elaborates on a term in the argument without closing the gap. "On-site gyms typically include stretching areas" doesn't affect whether they reduce back pain.

*The "tangentially related" trap.* An answer on the general topic that doesn't address the specific argument. "Back pain affects many workers" is topic-relevant but argument-irrelevant.

*The "wrong-subject strengthener" trap.* An answer that supports a claim similar to but different from the actual conclusion. If the argument concludes about Company A, but the answer supports a claim about Company B, it doesn't strengthen.

*The "too-strong strengthener" trap.* An answer that proves more than the argument needs — an over-claim that goes further than the evidence supports. This is usually wrong when the argument itself was hedged.

### Strengthen speed drill

For every strengthen question, in 20 seconds:

1. Identify conclusion (5 sec).
2. Identify gap (10 sec).
3. Predict strengthener (5 sec).

Then 40 seconds to scan answer choices and pick. Total: 60 seconds per question. If you're above 90 seconds, you're re-reading too much.

## Part 2: Weaken — Deep Treatment

Weaken mirrors strengthen in structure. The question asks which answer choice, if added to the argument, would most decrease the argument's support for the conclusion.

### What "weaken" means precisely

A weakener opens the gap between premises and conclusion. It makes the conclusion less likely to follow from the given evidence. It does not have to disprove the conclusion — just reduce its plausibility.

### The weaken decision tree

1. Identify conclusion and premises.
2. Identify gap.
3. Predict: what would, if true, make the argument weaker? Usually something that introduces an alternative explanation or suggests the premise doesn't lead to the conclusion.
4. Scan choices. Eliminate:
   - Out of scope
   - Strengthens (wrong direction)
   - Irrelevant
5. Pick the strongest weakener among survivors.

### Common weaken moves

*Introduce alternative cause.* If the argument says "A caused B," offer a different plausible cause for B that the argument didn't consider.

*Reverse causation.* If the argument says "A caused B," suggest that B might actually have caused A.

*Challenge representativeness.* If the argument generalizes from a sample, suggest the sample is unrepresentative.

*Raise side-effect or implementation issue.* If the argument is about a policy, raise an implementation issue that would undermine the benefit.

*Attack the analogy.* If the argument is an analogy, show the cases differ in the relevant respect.

*Question the premise's scope.* If the argument uses evidence from one context, suggest the conclusion applies to a different context.

### Weaken worked example 1 — alternative cause

*Argument.* "A study of patients with chronic migraines found that those who drank herbal tea daily reported 25% fewer migraine days than those who did not. Therefore, drinking herbal tea reduces the frequency of chronic migraines."

*Gap analysis.* Causal claim from correlation. Likely weakener: alternative explanation for the correlation.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Herbal tea contains compounds that have been shown to have anti-inflammatory effects.
(B) Patients who drink herbal tea regularly are more likely than other patients to have adopted other healthful habits, such as regular exercise and meditation, that independently reduce migraine frequency.
(C) Chronic migraines are more common in adults over 40 than in younger adults.
(D) The study's participants were all adults between 25 and 55.
(E) Herbal tea is widely available and inexpensive.

*Analysis.*

(A) Would strengthen (mechanism).

(B) Introduces a powerful confound — the tea-drinking correlation is explained by other healthful habits that also reduce migraines. Strong weakener.

(C) Context but not a challenge to the study's findings.

(D) Describes the sample without challenging its representativeness.

(E) Practical detail, not causal challenge.

*Answer: (B).*

### Weaken worked example 2 — reverse causation

*Argument.* "Among patients with mild anxiety, those who engage in regular exercise report significantly lower anxiety levels than those who do not exercise. The researchers conclude that regular exercise reduces anxiety."

*Gap analysis.* Could be reverse causation — people with lower anxiety might be more likely to exercise because their lower anxiety makes them more motivated and energetic.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The benefits of exercise for anxiety vary by age.
(B) Patients with severe anxiety are less likely to exercise regularly than patients with mild anxiety.
(C) Anxiety levels are notoriously difficult to measure reliably.
(D) Regular exercise has other health benefits beyond anxiety reduction.
(E) Patients with lower baseline anxiety are more likely to initiate and sustain regular exercise routines than patients with higher anxiety.

*Analysis.*

(A) Side note, doesn't challenge the causal claim.

(B) Reinforces the correlation but doesn't address the causal direction.

(C) Methodological issue but general.

(D) Strengthens by showing exercise has other benefits.

(E) Directly proposes reverse causation — low anxiety causes exercise habits, not the other way around. Strongest weakener.

*Answer: (E).*

### Weaken worked example 3 — challenge representativeness

*Argument.* "A survey of business school students at a prestigious university found that 70% of graduates who started their own companies within two years of graduation reported higher career satisfaction than those who joined established firms. The university is considering encouraging more students to pursue entrepreneurship based on this finding."

*Gap analysis.* Generalizing from a specific sample. The students who started companies are likely a self-selected group — probably those already predisposed to high satisfaction.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Students who start companies are typically those with higher entrepreneurial aptitude and self-confidence, both of which independently predict career satisfaction.
(B) The survey was conducted online.
(C) Career satisfaction is an important predictor of long-term career success.
(D) Business schools typically teach entrepreneurship in their curriculum.
(E) Some students who started companies also reported financial stress.

*Analysis.*

(A) Challenges the representativeness — the students in the entrepreneurial group differ from others in ways that independently drive satisfaction. Strong weakener.

(B) Survey method detail.

(C) Strengthens importance of the finding.

(D) Context.

(E) Acknowledges some downside but doesn't challenge the satisfaction claim.

*Answer: (A).*

### Weaken worked example 4 — attack the analogy

*Argument.* "City A should implement a bike-sharing program. City B, a neighboring city with similar demographics, implemented a bike-sharing program three years ago and has seen a 15% reduction in downtown traffic congestion. A similar program in City A would produce comparable results."

*Gap analysis.* Analogy argument. The gap: are A and B similar in the relevant respects? The argument mentions demographics; other factors (geography, existing public transit, climate) matter.

*Answer choices.*
(A) City B's bike-sharing program is subsidized by a local technology company.
(B) City A has steeper hills and a more dispersed layout than City B, making biking less attractive for everyday commuting.
(C) Bike-sharing programs have been widely adopted in cities worldwide.
(D) Demographics of City A and City B are similar.
(E) City A has a strong cycling culture.

*Analysis.*

(A) A difference in implementation, but not in the underlying conditions that affect bike adoption.

(B) Directly attacks the analogy — hilly, dispersed geography makes biking infeasible for daily use, regardless of demographic similarity. Strong weakener.

(C) Strengthens the general concept.

(D) Strengthens the analogy.

(E) Strengthens.

*Answer: (B).*

### Weaken worked example 5 — the "premise challenge" (rare)

*Argument.* "Patients who received the new vaccine reported 50% fewer symptoms of the illness than patients who did not receive the vaccine. Therefore, the vaccine reduces the symptoms of the illness."

*Gap analysis.* Direct causal claim from randomized-looking data. Possible weakeners: challenge the premise itself (the "50% fewer" might be wrong), introduce alternative cause, challenge the study's methodology.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Some patients in the vaccine group also received an over-the-counter symptom reliever.
(B) The vaccine has side effects in 10% of recipients.
(C) The symptom reports were self-reported, and patients in the vaccine group knew they had been vaccinated, potentially leading to placebo effects.
(D) The illness is generally mild in 70% of untreated patients.
(E) Vaccination rates vary by region.

*Analysis.*

(A) Introduces a co-intervention that might explain the symptom reduction — alternative cause. Weakens.

(B) Side effects, not symptom reduction.

(C) Placebo effect from knowing they were vaccinated could explain reported symptom reduction. Also weakens, possibly stronger — placebo challenges the core causal claim directly.

(D) Context about illness severity, not the specific claim.

(E) Irrelevant.

Between (A) and (C): (C) is stronger because placebo effect affects the core measurement (reported symptoms), not an external confound. *Answer: (C).*

### Weaken traps, expanded

*The "irrelevant weakener."* An answer that is negative-sounding but doesn't actually address the argument's gap. "The policy has side effects" doesn't weaken the claim that the policy works if the argument was only about effectiveness.

*The "wrong-direction answer."* An answer that actually strengthens. Common under time pressure when students don't carefully check direction.

*The "too-mild weakener."* An answer that is weakly negative but doesn't truly challenge the argument. Compare to a stronger alternative in the choices.

*The "premise detail" trap.* An answer that adds detail to a premise without changing how the argument works.

*The "out-of-scope weakener."* An answer challenging something the argument didn't claim.

### Weaken speed drill

Same as strengthen: 60 seconds per question. Pre-think the weakener in 20 seconds, scan in 40.

## Part 3: Assumption — Deep Treatment

Assumption questions ask: what must be true for the argument to work? The correct answer is an unstated claim the argument relies on. Without it, the argument collapses.

### The negation test

The single most reliable assumption-question technique. For each answer choice, ask: "If I negate this, does the argument fall apart?"

If yes (negation destroys the argument), the answer is an assumption.
If no (argument survives the negation), the answer is not an assumption.

Apply the test systematically. It catches assumption-question mistakes almost all the time.

### Assumption vs strengthen — the distinction

Students often confuse these. A strengthener is something that, if added, makes the argument better. An assumption is something the argument already needs to work.

Operationally: strengtheners can be optional extras. Assumptions are required.

Example: "A caused B" argument. A strengthener: "A's proposed mechanism is plausible." An assumption: "No factor other than A can explain B's presence." The strengthener adds support; the assumption is the unstated foundation.

### Assumption worked example 1 — the classic causal

*Argument.* "Employees in the marketing department have higher job satisfaction than employees in the engineering department. Therefore, the marketing department's management style is more effective than the engineering department's."

*Gap.* The conclusion attributes satisfaction difference to management style. Alternatives: different work content, different compensation, different company policies for the two departments.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Employees in marketing are generally paid more than employees in engineering.
(B) Job satisfaction is not substantially affected by factors other than management style across the two departments.
(C) All marketing managers use the same management style.
(D) Job satisfaction is a good measure of employee performance.
(E) The two departments have the same overall size.

*Analysis.*

Apply negation test to each.

(A) Negated: "Marketing and engineering are paid the same." Argument continues — pay isn't the variable being discussed. Not necessarily an assumption.

(B) Negated: "Job satisfaction is substantially affected by factors other than management style across the two departments." If true, the difference in satisfaction might be due to those other factors, not management. Argument collapses. *Assumption.*

(C) Negated: "Not all marketing managers use the same management style." Argument doesn't require uniformity. Not an assumption.

(D) Negated: "Job satisfaction is not a good measure of employee performance." The argument is about satisfaction, not performance. Not necessary.

(E) Size irrelevant to management comparison.

*Answer: (B).*

### Assumption worked example 2 — a subtle one

*Argument.* "The local bookstore has reported a 15% decline in sales over the past year. The owner attributes the decline to increased online competition. Therefore, if the bookstore offers a broader selection of rare and specialty titles — which online retailers typically lack — sales will rebound."

*Gap.* The argument assumes customers who shifted to online would return for rare/specialty titles. Also assumes the shift is purely competition-driven, not reflecting broader changes in reading habits.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The local bookstore has historically stocked primarily mainstream titles.
(B) Customers interested in rare and specialty titles would choose the local bookstore over online retailers.
(C) Online bookstores have increased their sales over the past year.
(D) The bookstore's staff is familiar with rare and specialty titles.
(E) Rare and specialty titles generally sell at higher margins than mainstream titles.

*Analysis.*

(A) Negated: "The local bookstore has stocked rare and specialty titles all along." If true, adding more wouldn't help — the specialty approach has already been tried. The argument's "broader selection" proposal assumes this is a new strategy. Possibly an assumption.

(B) Negated: "Customers interested in rare titles would choose online retailers anyway." If true, adding specialty titles doesn't help. Argument collapses. Strong candidate for assumption.

(C) Online sales data is context, not required for the argument.

(D) Staff familiarity with specialty titles might matter for selection-quality but isn't required.

(E) Margins not mentioned in the argument.

Between (A) and (B): (B) is more fundamental. The argument's logic requires that specialty-loving customers would return to the bookstore if it stocked specialties. Without this assumption, the proposal fails.

*Answer: (B).*

### Assumption worked example 3 — a research-type argument

*Argument.* "A recent study found that workers who take short, frequent breaks throughout the workday are 20% more productive than workers who take one or two long breaks. Based on this finding, the company should require all workers to take short, frequent breaks."

*Gap.* The assumption that what the study measured applies to this specific workforce, and that required breaks (as opposed to chosen breaks) produce the same effect.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Workers who take breaks voluntarily produce different results than workers who take mandatory breaks.
(B) The study's findings are applicable to this company's workforce.
(C) Productivity is a key measure of workplace success.
(D) The company's current break policy allows for long breaks.
(E) Workers prefer short, frequent breaks over one long break.

*Analysis.*

(A) Negated: "Workers who take voluntary and mandatory breaks produce the same results." The argument proposes mandatory breaks based on a study — presumably of voluntary takers. If the two modes produce the same results, the argument works. If not, it fails.

Wait — negating (A) to "workers who take voluntary and mandatory breaks produce different results" would actually weaken the argument. So (A) negated fails the argument — which means (A) as written is saying "different results," which seems to imply the mandatory is different from voluntary — but the argument assumes they produce similar results. So the positive version of (A) actually states: "Workers who take breaks voluntarily produce different results than workers who take mandatory breaks." If true, the study (voluntary) doesn't apply to the proposal (mandatory). That would undermine the argument.

So (A) as written would undermine the argument. That means (A) is NOT an assumption — it's actually the opposite of an assumption.

Let me re-read. The argument requires that the study's result (from whichever regime it studied) applies to the proposal. The study tracked workers who already take frequent breaks — probably voluntary. The argument proposes mandatory. The assumption the argument relies on: "Voluntary and mandatory break regimes produce similar productivity effects." Equivalently: NOT "Voluntary and mandatory produce different effects."

So the negation-fail pattern: (A) says "voluntary and mandatory produce different results" — if TRUE, argument fails. So (A) IS the negation of an assumption, meaning the argument assumes (A) is FALSE. In answer-choice form, the assumption would say "voluntary and mandatory produce similar results."

But (A) is written as "produce different results" — which is the non-assumption direction. So (A) is NOT the assumption as written.

The answer choices don't appear to include a proper assumption matching what the argument needs (similar results from voluntary and mandatory). Let me check (B).

(B) Negated: "The study's findings are not applicable to this company's workforce." If true, argument fails. *Assumption.*

This is the clearer assumption — directly addresses the generalizability gap.

*Answer: (B).*

### Assumption worked example 4 — constrained scope

*Argument.* "A city council member argues that the city should require all restaurants to display calorie counts on menus. She cites studies showing that diners eat 10% fewer calories on average when calorie counts are posted. Therefore, requiring calorie counts will reduce obesity in the city."

*Gap.* Two gaps here. First, studies showed reduction in calories, but the argument claims reduction in obesity. Second, the policy assumes diners will see and respond to the counts.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The 10% reduction in calories per meal will be sustained over time.
(B) Restaurants will comply fully with the calorie-display requirement.
(C) The studies' findings will generalize to the city's dining population.
(D) Reducing per-meal calorie consumption will reduce obesity rates.
(E) The policy will not impose excessive costs on restaurants.

*Analysis.*

(A) Negated: "The 10% reduction won't be sustained." Argument relies on sustained reduction to claim obesity effect. Plausibly an assumption.

(B) Negated: "Restaurants won't comply." Argument fails if restaurants don't comply. But restaurants complying is a separate implementation issue.

(C) Negated: "Studies won't generalize." Argument fails. Possible assumption.

(D) Negated: "Reducing per-meal calories won't reduce obesity." This is the core causal link — the argument needs per-meal reduction to translate to obesity reduction. Without this, the argument collapses. *Strong assumption candidate.*

(E) Cost is separate.

Between (A), (C), and (D), (D) is the most fundamental — it's the core causal step from "calorie reduction" to "obesity reduction." (A) and (C) address study-generalizability, (D) addresses policy-outcome chain. Arguments often have multiple assumptions; the question asks for the most important one.

*Answer: (D).*

### Assumption worked example 5 — a tricky scope

*Argument.* "A new medical treatment has been approved after clinical trials showed a 30% reduction in mortality among patients with the disease. Based on this approval, public health officials recommend that all patients with the disease receive the treatment."

*Gap.* The 30% reduction was in the trial population. The recommendation is for all patients with the disease. Gaps: the trial population might differ from the general population, the treatment might have side effects in some subgroups, or cost-effectiveness might matter for "all patients."

*Answer choices.*
(A) The clinical trials included patients from diverse demographic backgrounds.
(B) The treatment produces the same benefits for all patients with the disease, not just those in the clinical trial population.
(C) No other treatment has been shown to produce greater mortality reduction.
(D) The treatment is affordable and widely available.
(E) The disease is a leading cause of mortality in the population.

*Analysis.*

(A) Negated: "The trials included only one demographic." Argument weakens but may not collapse.

(B) Negated: "The treatment does not produce the same benefits for all patients." Argument collapses — the recommendation extends to all patients, and if it doesn't work for all, the recommendation fails. *Assumption.*

(C) Comparative efficacy, not a required assumption for this recommendation.

(D) Cost/access isn't the argument's focus.

(E) Importance of the disease, not argument structure.

*Answer: (B).*

### Assumption traps, expanded

*The "strengthener disguised as assumption"* trap. An answer that would support the argument but isn't required. Distinguish by the negation test.

*The "too-strong assumption" trap.* An answer claiming something absolute ("all," "always") when the argument only requires something qualified ("most," "usually").

*The "too-weak assumption" trap.* An answer saying "possibly" or "sometimes" when the argument needs a stronger claim.

*The "restated premise" trap.* An answer that just restates one of the premises. The premises are already given; they're not unstated assumptions.

*The "wrong-subject assumption" trap.* An answer that addresses a topic the argument didn't focus on.

### Assumption speed drill

70–80 seconds per question. Assumption questions are slightly slower than strengthen/weaken because the negation test takes 10 extra seconds.

## Part 4: Inference — Deep Treatment

Inference (or must-be-true) questions ask what must be true given the information in the passage. The correct answer is a claim you can deduce from the passage — not one that goes beyond it.

### The cardinal rule

*Inference answers are one logical step from the passage, using the passage's own terms.* Two-step inferences are usually wrong. Claims that introduce new concepts or extrapolate are usually wrong.

### The support test

For each answer choice, ask: "Can I point to a specific sentence or two sentences in the passage that support this claim?" If yes, candidate. If no, eliminate.

### Inference worked example 1 — a restatement

*Passage.* "Recent research suggests that small increases in sleep duration can improve cognitive performance. In a study of 300 adults, those who increased their nightly sleep by 30 minutes for four weeks showed 10% improvement in reaction time and memory tests compared to a control group that maintained baseline sleep duration."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Sleep duration is the single most important factor in cognitive performance.
(B) Increasing sleep by 30 minutes per night for four weeks can improve cognitive test performance.
(C) All adults would benefit from increasing their sleep by 30 minutes per night.
(D) The adults in the study were initially sleep-deprived.
(E) Sleep duration is more important for memory than for reaction time.

*Analysis.*

(A) Too strong. The passage doesn't claim sleep is the "single most important" factor.

(B) Direct restatement of the study's finding. Supported by the passage explicitly. *Candidate.*

(C) Extrapolates beyond the study population.

(D) The passage doesn't say the adults were sleep-deprived — it says they increased by 30 minutes from baseline, but baseline could have been adequate.

(E) The passage says both reaction time and memory improved by 10%; it doesn't compare the two.

*Answer: (B).*

### Inference worked example 2 — a single-step logical deduction

*Passage.* "All members of the city council are elected for two-year terms. The terms are staggered, with half of the council elected in odd-numbered years and the other half in even-numbered years. The mayor is elected separately for a four-year term."

*Answer choices.*
(A) The mayor is not a member of the city council.
(B) In any given year, at least some council members are up for election.
(C) Half of the council is elected every year.
(D) Council elections occur every year.
(E) The mayor serves concurrently with a changing set of council members during a four-year term.

*Analysis.*

(A) Not directly stated. The mayor is elected separately but might still be a council member.

(B) Passage says half are elected in odd years, half in even — so every year has elections. Supported. *Candidate.*

(C) True per the passage (half of the council is elected in odd years, meaning every year half are elected, over a two-year cycle).

(D) Yes — odd and even years both have elections. Supported.

(E) Over a four-year mayoral term, the council is fully replaced once (each council member serves 2 years, so all are replaced in 2 years, and twice in 4). So the mayor would serve alongside different council members. This is a two-step inference.

Between (B), (C), (D), (E): (B) and (D) are most directly supported. (C) is a close paraphrase of the passage. (E) is the strongest deduction from the passage structure.

For the strictly narrowest inference, (B) or (D) are safest. (E) stretches by requiring a calculation about overlap.

*Answer: (B) or (D).* Depending on choice, (B) is probably safest because it's explicitly in the passage.

### Inference worked example 3 — an "if-then" chain

*Passage.* "If a country increases its minimum wage significantly, low-wage employers face higher costs. To offset these costs, employers typically either raise prices, reduce hours for low-wage workers, or automate jobs. Each of these responses has negative effects on low-wage workers, either through higher costs of goods, reduced earnings, or job loss."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Countries should not increase their minimum wage.
(B) Significant minimum-wage increases are likely to have some negative effects on low-wage workers.
(C) Automation is the most common employer response to minimum-wage increases.
(D) Price increases are the least harmful response to higher wages.
(E) Minimum-wage increases always harm low-wage workers more than they help.

*Analysis.*

(A) Policy recommendation, not an inference from the passage's logic.

(B) The passage says all three employer responses have negative effects. So minimum-wage increases, via those responses, have negative effects. Supported. *Candidate.*

(C) Not specified in the passage.

(D) Comparative ranking not in the passage.

(E) Too strong — the passage says there are negative effects but doesn't weigh them against positive effects (higher wages when jobs remain).

*Answer: (B).*

### Inference worked example 4 — a subtle "cannot be inferred"

*Passage.* "A recent survey of 500 small business owners found that those who have used social media for marketing report higher customer acquisition rates than those who have not. Interestingly, however, the difference in customer acquisition rates was much larger among businesses in their first three years of operation than among more established businesses."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Social media is a more effective marketing tool for newer businesses than for older ones.
(B) Established businesses are less likely to adopt social media marketing than newer businesses.
(C) Customer acquisition rates are higher for newer businesses generally.
(D) The 500 small business owners in the survey represent a diverse cross-section of industries.
(E) Small businesses should use social media marketing for customer acquisition.

*Analysis.*

(A) Close but subtle. The passage says the *difference* in customer acquisition rates was larger for newer businesses. This means social media's effect was larger for newer businesses. Does that mean "more effective"? Yes, if effectiveness is measured by acquisition-rate difference. Reasonable inference.

(B) Not stated.

(C) Not stated — only the social-media effect difference between new and old is reported.

(D) Not specified.

(E) Policy recommendation, not inference.

*Answer: (A).*

### Inference worked example 5 — a "complex passage"

*Passage.* "Recent analyses of workplace productivity data suggest that productivity gains from meetings tend to plateau after 45 minutes. Meetings longer than 45 minutes show diminishing returns in decision quality and decisions reached per hour. Some companies have responded by setting a default meeting length of 45 minutes. These companies report no decrease in decision quality but save an average of 3 hours per employee per week compared to their previous meeting norms."

*Answer choices.*
(A) All companies should adopt 45-minute default meeting lengths.
(B) Companies that adopted 45-minute defaults made the same decisions they would have made with longer meetings.
(C) Companies using 45-minute defaults have experienced improvements in decision quality relative to their previous norms.
(D) Meeting productivity data has been ignored by most companies.
(E) A default meeting length affects how employees prepare for meetings.

*Analysis.*

(A) Extrapolation, not an inference.

(B) The passage says these companies "report no decrease in decision quality." If no decrease, decisions are at least as good. This is supported. *Candidate.*

(C) "No decrease" is not "improvement" — those are different. The passage doesn't claim improvement. Wrong.

(D) Not supported.

(E) Speculation, not in the passage.

*Answer: (B).*

### Inference traps, expanded

*The "paraphrase with a twist" trap.* The answer is very close to the passage but changes one word — "most" becomes "all," or "reduces" becomes "eliminates."

*The "out-of-passage extrapolation" trap.* The answer is about the topic but introduces claims not supported.

*The "too-strong" trap.* The answer over-extends a passage claim.

*The "unsupported comparison" trap.* The passage gives individual data; the answer compares groups not explicitly compared.

*The "policy recommendation" trap.* The answer suggests what should be done; the passage only describes what is.

### Inference speed drill

60–80 seconds per question. The technique is elimination-based: check each answer against the passage and eliminate when the answer introduces unsupported claims.

## Part 5: Flaw in Reasoning — Deep Treatment

Flaw questions ask: what is wrong with how the argument reaches its conclusion? The answer describes a logical error in the argument's structure.

### The core flaw types

Most flaw answers describe one of these patterns.

*Correlation mistaken for causation.* "A and B are correlated; therefore A causes B" without ruling out alternatives.

*Sample bias.* Generalizing from a sample that doesn't represent the population.

*Necessary confused with sufficient.* Treating a necessary condition as if it were sufficient, or vice versa.

*False dichotomy.* Presenting two options as if they were the only possibilities.

*Ad hominem.* Attacking the person rather than the argument.

*Straw man.* Refuting a weakened version of an opposing claim.

*Circular reasoning.* Using the conclusion as support for a premise that supports the conclusion.

*Hasty generalization.* Drawing a broad conclusion from insufficient evidence.

*Appeal to authority or tradition.* Concluding from the fact that an authority or tradition endorses something.

*Equivocation.* Using a word with multiple meanings ambiguously between those meanings.

### Flaw worked example 1 — correlation-as-causation

*Argument.* "Cities with higher median incomes have more art museums per capita than cities with lower median incomes. Therefore, to increase median incomes, cities should invest in building more art museums."

*Flaw.* The argument uses a correlation (higher income and more museums) to support a causal claim (museums raise incomes). The correlation could be due to reverse causation (wealthier cities can afford museums) or a confound (both are driven by other factors).

*Answer choices.*
(A) The argument assumes that all art museums are equally effective.
(B) The argument treats a correlation between two variables as if it established a specific causal effect of one on the other.
(C) The argument ignores the cost of building new art museums.
(D) The argument assumes that museums are the only way to increase income.
(E) The argument fails to distinguish between different types of art.

*Analysis.*

(A) Not the core flaw.

(B) Directly describes the flaw. *Answer.*

(C) Cost is a practical concern, not the reasoning flaw.

(D) This is a potential separate flaw (false dichotomy-like) but not the main one.

(E) Not relevant to the argument's logic.

*Answer: (B).*

### Flaw worked example 2 — sample bias

*Argument.* "A magazine's survey of its readers found that 85% prefer detailed investigative articles over short news summaries. The magazine's editors conclude that their target audience prefers long-form journalism."

*Flaw.* The survey was of the magazine's existing readers — who are already the kind of people who chose to read this magazine. Their preferences don't generalize to the "target audience" (which might be broader).

*Answer choices.*
(A) The argument relies on a sample that is likely unrepresentative of the broader target audience.
(B) The argument assumes that magazine readers always read what they prefer.
(C) The argument fails to define "detailed investigative article."
(D) The argument ignores the cost of producing long-form content.
(E) The argument treats the magazine's editors as the sole judges of audience preference.

*Analysis.*

(A) Directly describes the sample bias. *Answer.*

(B) Not the core flaw.

(C) Definition issue, not the logical flaw.

(D) Practical concern.

(E) Not the issue.

*Answer: (A).*

### Flaw worked example 3 — necessary vs sufficient

*Argument.* "To score highly on standardized tests, students need to practice the material. Students at School X practice regularly. Therefore, students at School X will score highly on standardized tests."

*Flaw.* Practice is necessary but not sufficient. Scoring highly requires many factors, of which practice is one. Having practice doesn't guarantee high scores.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The argument confuses a necessary condition for high scores with a sufficient one.
(B) The argument ignores the role of teacher quality.
(C) The argument doesn't specify what "high scores" means.
(D) The argument assumes all students at School X practice equally.
(E) The argument relies on a definition of "practice" that is too vague.

*Analysis.*

(A) Directly names the flaw. *Answer.*

(B), (D), (E) are potential secondary issues but not the core logical flaw.

(C) Definition issue.

*Answer: (A).*

### Flaw worked example 4 — appeal to tradition

*Argument.* "The company has always maintained open-office layouts, and has been successful throughout its history. Therefore, we should not switch to a hybrid remote/in-office model, as doing so would deviate from our proven approach."

*Flaw.* The argument relies on the fact that the open-office approach has coexisted with success, without establishing that the open-office approach caused or was necessary for success. This is appeal to tradition.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The argument assumes that changes to organizational structure always produce negative results.
(B) The argument relies on the fact that a particular approach has been maintained historically as grounds for maintaining it, without establishing that the approach is what has driven success.
(C) The argument ignores the benefits of remote work.
(D) The argument treats hybrid and fully remote models as equivalent.
(E) The argument assumes that employees prefer open offices.

*Analysis.*

(A) Too strong — the argument doesn't claim all changes are negative.

(B) Directly describes the appeal-to-tradition flaw. *Answer.*

(C), (D), (E) are tangential.

*Answer: (B).*

### Flaw worked example 5 — a subtle one

*Argument.* "A company implemented a new training program. In the six months following implementation, the company's sales increased by 15%. The CEO concludes that the training program was responsible for the sales increase."

*Flaw.* Over six months, many factors could change (market conditions, product launches, marketing campaigns, seasonal trends). The correlation between training and sales doesn't prove the training caused the increase.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The argument assumes that a period following an event is caused by the event, without ruling out other potential causes.
(B) The argument relies on too short a time period.
(C) The argument assumes the CEO's judgment is reliable.
(D) The argument ignores the cost of the training program.
(E) The argument fails to define what "new" means in the context of the training program.

*Analysis.*

(A) Names the "post hoc ergo propter hoc" flaw — treating a post-intervention change as caused by the intervention without ruling out other causes. *Answer.*

(B) Time-period issue is related but not the core logical flaw.

(C), (D), (E) are secondary.

*Answer: (A).*

### Flaw traps, expanded

*The "irrelevant flaw" trap.* The answer describes a real logical flaw, but it's not the one this argument makes.

*The "missing premise" trap.* The answer points out a premise the argument didn't include, without showing the argument relies on that missing premise.

*The "generic critique" trap.* The answer is a general methodological concern (sample size, precision) without addressing the specific reasoning structure.

*The "attack the conclusion" trap.* The answer disputes the conclusion's truth rather than the argument's logic.

### Flaw speed drill

60–80 seconds per question. The key is identifying the reasoning pattern (causal, sample, necessary/sufficient, etc.) in the argument before scanning answers.

## Part 6: Paradox / Resolve Discrepancy — Deep Treatment

Paradox questions present two or more apparently contradictory facts. The correct answer reconciles them.

### The reconciliation test

For each answer choice, ask: "Does this answer make both of the stated facts simultaneously true?"

If yes, candidate. If it only addresses one fact or contradicts either, eliminate.

### Paradox worked example 1 — a common business paradox

*Passage.* "A company's profitable product line A had its advertising budget cut by 30% last year. Despite this, sales of product line A increased by 12% over the year. This result surprised the company's marketing analysts, who had projected flat or declining sales given the budget cut."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Advertising budgets are generally less effective than product features in driving sales.
(B) The company's competitors also increased their advertising budgets during the year.
(C) A new regulatory change made the competing products of a major competitor less available, shifting market share to product line A.
(D) Product line A has historically performed well during periods of economic growth.
(E) Consumer surveys indicate high awareness of product line A.

*Analysis.*

(A) General claim, doesn't specifically resolve.

(B) Doesn't resolve — if anything, more competition would further reduce sales.

(C) Major competitor's products became less available, shifting customers to product line A. This directly resolves the paradox. Sales could rise independent of advertising budget. *Answer.*

(D) Coincidence doesn't explain — unless we know there was growth, which the passage doesn't state.

(E) Awareness doesn't explain the sales increase after a cut.

*Answer: (C).*

### Paradox worked example 2 — a health/scientific paradox

*Passage.* "A clinical trial of a new pain medication found that 40% of patients experienced significant pain reduction. However, patient satisfaction with the medication was low — only 25% rated their experience as positive."

*Answer choices.*
(A) The pain reduction in the 40% was too modest to meet patient expectations.
(B) The medication had significant side effects that negatively affected the overall experience even for patients whose pain was reduced.
(C) Patients in the trial were comparing this medication to a previous one that provided more complete pain relief.
(D) Pain reduction is a common placebo-responsive outcome.
(E) The trial included patients with varied underlying conditions.

*Analysis.*

(A) If only 40% had reduction and only 25% were satisfied, some reduction cases weren't satisfying — could be because reduction was inadequate. Possible.

(B) Side effects explain why even patients with pain reduction might rate the experience poorly. This resolves the paradox by showing satisfaction depended on side effects too, not just pain reduction. *Strong candidate.*

(C) Plausible but more speculative.

(D) Placebo response applies to both groups.

(E) Variation doesn't specifically explain.

Between (A) and (B), (B) is stronger because it directly accounts for why many patients (presumably including those with pain reduction) would still be unsatisfied. (A) requires many assumptions about what "significant" means.

*Answer: (B).*

### Paradox worked example 3 — a social/policy paradox

*Passage.* "After a city implemented a significant increase in the minimum wage, employment in the city did not decline as many economists had predicted. In fact, overall employment levels in the affected industries remained stable, despite the higher labor costs."

*Answer choices.*
(A) The minimum wage increase was smaller than the increase some economists had assumed.
(B) Other cities in the region had similar minimum wage increases.
(C) The city's population grew significantly during the period, increasing consumer demand and supporting job creation despite higher costs.
(D) Some low-wage workers took on additional hours.
(E) Employers reduced hours rather than cutting jobs.

*Analysis.*

(A) Could resolve if the increase was small enough not to affect jobs.

(B) Regional context, doesn't directly resolve.

(C) Population growth → demand growth → job creation. This could offset the job-shedding effect of higher costs. Resolves. *Strong candidate.*

(D) Additional hours don't increase employment.

(E) Reducing hours is a different response, could be part of the picture.

Between (A) and (C), (C) provides a specific economic mechanism for why employment could hold steady despite higher costs.

*Answer: (C).*

### Paradox worked example 4 — subtle

*Passage.* "A survey found that employees of a certain technology firm are, on average, more satisfied with their jobs than employees of comparable firms, yet the firm's employee turnover rate is higher than the industry average."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Satisfaction surveys at this firm are conducted more frequently than at comparable firms.
(B) The technology firm attracts highly ambitious employees who are more likely to be recruited away for opportunities at other companies.
(C) Comparable firms pay lower salaries than the technology firm.
(D) The survey did not include employees who had already left the firm.
(E) Some employees at the technology firm have second jobs.

*Analysis.*

(A) Survey frequency doesn't explain higher satisfaction paired with higher turnover.

(B) Ambitious employees are highly recruited — so they leave for other opportunities even while being satisfied at their current job. Resolves the paradox. *Strong candidate.*

(C) Pay difference might explain satisfaction but not specifically the paradox.

(D) Selection bias in the survey — but this would bias satisfaction upward. Doesn't explain the turnover.

(E) Side jobs don't explain.

*Answer: (B).*

### Paradox worked example 5 — an economics paradox

*Passage.* "During the past economic downturn, the average price of goods fell by 5%, while wages fell by 3%. Some economists expressed surprise that consumer purchases did not increase significantly despite the relative increase in real purchasing power."

*Answer choices.*
(A) Many consumers lost their jobs entirely during the downturn, reducing their ability to purchase regardless of price levels.
(B) The price of basic necessities fell by more than the average.
(C) Savings rates increased during the downturn.
(D) Consumer credit was more difficult to obtain during the downturn.
(E) Inflation was expected to rise in the following year.

*Analysis.*

(A) Job loss means consumers couldn't afford to buy even at lower prices. Resolves. *Strong.*

(B) Not directly about total spending.

(C) Could be a consequence of (A) — not a root cause.

(D) Credit difficulty would indeed reduce purchases. Also plausible.

(E) Expected inflation might increase saving. Indirect.

Between (A) and (D), (A) is more direct — if people lost jobs, they couldn't consume. (D) requires credit-purchase link.

*Answer: (A).*

### Paradox traps

*The "explains one fact" trap.* The answer explains one side but not the other.

*The "contradicts a fact" trap.* The answer denies one of the stated facts, rather than reconciling both.

*The "tangential" trap.* The answer is on-topic but doesn't specifically resolve.

*The "too narrow" trap.* The answer addresses a specific element that doesn't cover the general paradox.

### Paradox speed drill

60–80 seconds per question. Read both facts carefully, pre-think the resolution, then scan.

## Part 7: Evaluate the Argument — Deep Treatment

Evaluate questions ask which question, if answered, would help you assess whether the argument's conclusion is strong or weak. The correct answer is a question whose yes/no answer would swing the argument in one direction or the other.

### The swing test

For each answer choice:

1. Suppose the answer to the question is "yes." Does that strengthen or weaken the argument?
2. Suppose the answer is "no." Does that strengthen or weaken the argument?
3. If yes and no both affect the argument (one strengthens, other weakens), the question is a good evaluator.
4. If both answers have similar effect (both strengthen, both weaken, or both neutral), it's not a useful evaluator.

### Evaluate worked example 1 — a business prediction

*Argument.* "The company's new product is projected to generate \$10 million in first-year revenue, based on market research showing that 25% of the target demographic expressed interest in the product."

*Gap.* Interest-to-purchase translation. "Expressed interest" in surveys doesn't always lead to actual purchase.

*Answer choices.*
(A) What is the size of the target demographic?
(B) What percentage of people who express interest in new products in surveys actually purchase those products when available?
(C) Who was the product designed by?
(D) How does the proposed price compare to competitor prices?
(E) What are the distribution channels for the product?

*Analysis.*

Apply the swing test.

(A) If demographic is big, supports higher revenue. If small, supports lower. Affects the argument. Possible evaluator.

(B) If high conversion (yes), interest translates to sales. If low (no), revenue is likely lower. Directly addresses the gap. *Strong evaluator.*

(C) Product designer affects quality but not directly the interest-revenue link.

(D) Price is relevant but not directly about the interest-revenue translation.

(E) Distribution affects reach but not the core interest-revenue question.

Between (A) and (B), (B) targets the specific gap (interest-to-purchase), while (A) addresses a different variable.

*Answer: (B).*

### Evaluate worked example 2 — a policy proposal

*Argument.* "To reduce air pollution in the city, the government should increase taxes on gasoline. A similar tax increase in a neighboring city reduced air pollution by 20% over three years."

*Gap.* Analogy. The two cities may differ in relevant ways.

*Answer choices.*
(A) What is the current air pollution level in the city?
(B) Is the city's transportation system similar to the neighboring city's, with respect to alternative modes (public transit, cycling infrastructure)?
(C) How much revenue will the tax generate?
(D) What is the population of the city?
(E) Will the tax be implemented in phases?

*Analysis.*

(A) Current level doesn't directly affect whether the tax will reduce pollution.

(B) If the transportation systems differ (e.g., this city has no public transit alternative), the tax might not reduce pollution. Directly affects analogy validity. *Strong.*

(C) Revenue is a separate question.

(D) Population could affect revenue but not directly whether the tax works.

(E) Implementation timing is about execution, not whether it works.

*Answer: (B).*

### Evaluate worked example 3 — a medical claim

*Argument.* "Studies show that adults who consume at least 5 servings of vegetables daily are 20% less likely to develop type 2 diabetes than adults who consume fewer servings. Based on this finding, public health officials recommend that all adults consume at least 5 servings of vegetables per day."

*Gap.* The observational studies might reflect other lifestyle factors of vegetable-consumers. Recommending "all adults" extends beyond the studied population.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Did the studies control for other dietary and lifestyle factors that might independently affect diabetes risk?
(B) What is the typical vegetable consumption in the general adult population?
(C) What types of vegetables were included in the studies?
(D) How old were the study participants on average?
(E) Are vegetables more affordable than other food types?

*Analysis.*

(A) If studies controlled well (yes), the causal claim is more supported. If not controlled (no), the claim is weak. Directly addresses the gap. *Strong evaluator.*

(B) Current consumption is context.

(C) Type matters somewhat but secondary.

(D) Demographics.

(E) Tangential.

*Answer: (A).*

### Evaluate traps

*The "on-topic but doesn't swing" trap.* The question is about the argument's topic but its answer wouldn't significantly affect whether the conclusion holds.

*The "both answers strengthen" trap.* Both yes and no answers would support the argument — not useful for evaluation.

*The "both answers weaken" trap.* Similar.

*The "too general" trap.* The question is too broad to meaningfully test the specific argument.

### Evaluate speed drill

60–80 seconds per question.

## Part 8: Boldface / Role of Statement — Deep Treatment

Boldface questions present an argument with one or two bolded statements and ask what role(s) those statements play in the argument.

### The core skill

Identify the logical role of each bolded statement. Is it a conclusion (intermediate or final)? A premise? An opposing view being presented? Evidence? A counterargument? A qualification?

### The classification grid

Label each bolded statement as one of:

- *Final conclusion:* the main claim the argument establishes.
- *Intermediate conclusion:* a conclusion that in turn supports the final conclusion.
- *Premise:* evidence or reasoning that supports a conclusion.
- *Opposing view:* a position the argument opposes.
- *Evidence against opposing view:* facts that undermine a stated or implied opposing view.
- *Qualification:* a hedge or restriction on a claim.

### Boldface worked example 1 — a common two-boldface structure

*Argument.* "(First boldface) A widely-held view among economists is that unemployment and inflation are inversely related. (Second boldface) However, several recent studies have observed periods of simultaneously high unemployment and high inflation, a phenomenon known as stagflation. These findings suggest that the traditional inverse relationship may be more complex than previously thought."

*Analysis of roles.*

First boldface: a view presented, which the argument then critiques or complicates. Opposing view being discussed.

Second boldface: evidence that complicates the first view. Evidence against the traditional view.

Answer: "The first is a traditionally accepted view; the second is evidence that complicates or contradicts that view."

### Boldface worked example 2 — conclusion-supporting structure

*Argument.* "(First boldface) Global smartphone sales have grown each year for the past decade. This growth has been particularly pronounced in emerging markets. (Second boldface) Given these trends, major smartphone manufacturers should focus their future investment on emerging-market consumers."

*Analysis.*

First boldface: a factual claim (premise) that serves as evidence for the final recommendation.

Second boldface: the final conclusion — a recommendation based on the evidence.

Answer: "The first is a premise; the second is the conclusion."

### Boldface worked example 3 — an intermediate-conclusion structure

*Argument.* "Recent studies have found that short, frequent breaks improve worker productivity. (First boldface) This finding suggests that workers should take breaks every 45 minutes rather than working uninterrupted for long periods. However, forcing employees into a strict break schedule may feel intrusive. (Second boldface) Companies should therefore encourage, rather than mandate, frequent breaks during the workday."

*Analysis.*

First boldface: an intermediate conclusion drawn from the studies — not the final recommendation.

Second boldface: the final conclusion — a policy recommendation that accounts for both the intermediate conclusion and the counterargument.

Answer: "The first is an intermediate conclusion that supports the argument's direction but not the final recommendation; the second is the final conclusion."

### Boldface worked example 4 — a subtle opposing-view handling

*Argument.* "(First boldface) Many critics argue that the new labor regulations will reduce business activity. However, historical data from similar regulations in other jurisdictions shows that business activity has remained largely stable after implementation. (Second boldface) Although the regulations will impose some short-term adjustment costs, their long-term impact on business activity is unlikely to be significant."

*Analysis.*

First boldface: an opposing view (critics' position).

Second boldface: the argument's final conclusion, which contradicts the opposing view and provides a nuanced alternative.

Answer: "The first is a view the argument opposes; the second is the argument's conclusion, which responds to that opposing view."

### Boldface worked example 5 — evidence interpretation

*Argument.* "(First boldface) A recent industry report predicts that renewable energy will provide 50% of global energy consumption by 2040. Given current rates of renewable adoption, this projection appears optimistic. Historical forecasts of renewable adoption have consistently underestimated the pace of change, suggesting that the 50% figure might be conservative. (Second boldface) Renewable energy is likely to make up at least half of global consumption by 2040, and possibly more."

*Analysis.*

First boldface: a prediction (premise or claim) that the argument evaluates.

Second boldface: the argument's final conclusion, synthesizing the historical tendency toward underestimation with the industry report.

Answer: "The first is a specific projection the argument examines; the second is the argument's final conclusion, which extends or modifies that projection."

### Boldface traps

*The "role reversal" trap.* The answer swaps the roles of the two boldfaces.

*The "too-abstract match" trap.* The answer uses abstract language that applies loosely but doesn't precisely match the argument's structure.

*The "conclusion mistaken for evidence" trap.* The answer identifies the second boldface as evidence when it's actually the conclusion.

*The "opposing view not clearly opposed" trap.* The answer identifies an opposing view when the argument doesn't clearly oppose it.

### Boldface speed drill

70–90 seconds per question. Boldface is slightly slower because the structural analysis is deeper.

## Part 9: Complete the Argument — Deep Treatment

Complete the argument (or "most logically completes") questions end with a blank. You fill in the blank with the answer that most logically completes the argument.

### The completion principle

The completion should:
- Follow logically from the premises given.
- Match the direction of the argument.
- Match the strength of the evidence (not too strong, not too weak).

### Complete worked example 1 — a standard

*Argument.* "Recent studies suggest that students who learn a second language in childhood develop stronger executive function and cognitive flexibility than monolingual students. Many school districts are considering introducing a second language in elementary school to capitalize on these benefits. However, implementing a new language program requires trained teachers, curriculum development, and funding, which are not always available. Therefore, ______"

*Direction.* The argument establishes benefit, then identifies constraints. The completion should combine these — recommending action conditional on resources.

*Answer choices.*
(A) All schools should immediately introduce second-language programs.
(B) Schools should introduce second-language programs only where trained teachers, curriculum, and funding are available.
(C) Second-language programs should be reserved for advanced students.
(D) Monolingual education is superior to bilingual education.
(E) Cognitive benefits of language learning are overstated.

*Analysis.*

(A) Ignores the constraints.

(B) Balances benefit and constraints. *Answer.*

(C) Doesn't match argument direction.

(D), (E) contradict the argument.

*Answer: (B).*

### Complete worked example 2 — a direction question

*Argument.* "A company is considering introducing a flexible work arrangement that would allow employees to work from home two days per week. Proponents argue this will improve work-life balance and reduce commute-related stress, boosting employee retention and productivity. Critics note that the policy may reduce opportunities for in-person collaboration and mentorship for junior employees. Both arguments have merit. The question is which effect will predominate. Therefore, ______"

*Direction.* The argument identifies a tension and frames the question. The completion should reflect the tension.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The flexible work policy should be abandoned entirely.
(B) Companies should implement the policy aggressively for all employees.
(C) The net impact of the policy on a specific organization will depend on how the organization manages the trade-offs between work-life balance and collaboration.
(D) Remote work is clearly more productive than in-office work.
(E) Junior employees should be excluded from the policy.

*Analysis.*

(A), (B) are too strong — they choose one side.

(C) Reflects the tension and acknowledges that outcome depends on management. Matches the direction. *Answer.*

(D) Takes a side too strongly.

(E) Is a specific policy recommendation not supported by the argument.

*Answer: (C).*

### Complete worked example 3 — evaluating alternative options

*Argument.* "A museum is considering two strategies for increasing attendance. Strategy A involves expanding evening hours, which appeals to working adults but requires additional staffing costs. Strategy B involves reducing admission prices, which attracts more visitors but lowers revenue per visitor. The museum's budget constraints are significant, and it can implement only one strategy at this time. Therefore, the museum's decision depends on which of the following factors: ______"

*Direction.* The argument presents a choice with trade-offs. The completion should identify what would determine the choice.

*Answer choices.*
(A) Whether the museum wants to expand its reputation.
(B) Whether the expected increase in revenue from increased attendance (at reduced prices) is greater or less than the additional staffing costs for evening hours.
(C) Whether the museum has parking available.
(D) How old the museum is.
(E) Whether the museum has a website.

*Analysis.*

(A) Reputation isn't the trade-off.

(B) Captures the financial trade-off — comparing revenue from (B) with costs from (A). Matches the argument. *Answer.*

(C), (D), (E) are tangential.

*Answer: (B).*

### Complete worked example 4 — an inferential completion

*Argument.* "Recent data shows that students who spend more than three hours per day on social media have lower average grades than students who spend less time. The university is considering imposing screen-time limits during exam periods. However, correlation between screen time and grades does not necessarily imply that reducing screen time will improve grades. Therefore, ______"

*Direction.* The argument cautions about inferring causation from correlation. The completion should reflect that caution.

*Answer choices.*
(A) The university should not attempt to address students' academic performance.
(B) Reducing screen time will definitely improve grades.
(C) Before implementing screen-time limits, the university should investigate whether the correlation reflects causation.
(D) Students should be encouraged to use social media extensively.
(E) The correlation is not meaningful.

*Analysis.*

(A) Too passive.

(B) Contradicts the argument's caution.

(C) Directly addresses the argument's caution and proposes a measured response. *Answer.*

(D), (E) don't match the direction.

*Answer: (C).*

### Complete worked example 5 — a policy implication

*Argument.* "The introduction of new regulations governing data privacy has been proposed as a way to protect consumers from misuse of personal information. Supporters argue that stronger regulations will deter harmful practices. Opponents counter that overly restrictive regulations will stifle innovation and disadvantage businesses. A balanced approach should consider both perspectives. Therefore, new data privacy regulations should ______"

*Direction.* The argument presents two views and suggests balance. The completion should reflect balance.

*Answer choices.*
(A) be fully aligned with the supporters' proposals.
(B) be structured to address consumer protection without imposing unreasonable burdens on businesses.
(C) not be implemented at all.
(D) focus exclusively on large corporations.
(E) be identical to the regulations in other industries.

*Analysis.*

(A), (C) are one-sided.

(B) Balances both perspectives. *Answer.*

(D), (E) are specific recommendations not justified by the argument.

*Answer: (B).*

### Complete traps

*The "too-strong completion" trap.* The answer makes a claim stronger than the argument's evidence supports.

*The "too-weak completion" trap.* The answer is technically true but doesn't meaningfully complete the argument.

*The "wrong direction" trap.* The answer contradicts the argument's direction.

*The "off-topic completion" trap.* The answer introduces a new topic.

### Complete speed drill

60–80 seconds per question.

## Part 10: The CR Argument-Shape Taxonomy

These are the thirty-plus argument shapes that the GMAT recycles. Learning them cold turns CR into pattern recognition.

### Causal shapes

1. *Correlation-to-causation.* A and B are correlated; argument claims A causes B. Gap: alternatives.

2. *Temporal-to-causation.* A happened before B; argument claims A caused B. Gap: coincidence or confound.

3. *Intervention-to-outcome.* Intervention X was applied; outcome Y occurred; argument claims X caused Y. Gap: control group or natural change.

4. *Mechanism-to-causation.* Mechanism M is plausible; argument claims M causes the outcome. Gap: M might not be the actual cause.

### Prediction shapes

5. *Trend-continuation.* Trend has occurred; argument predicts it continues. Gap: drivers may change.

6. *Analogy-prediction.* Case A had outcome O; this case is similar; argument predicts O. Gap: cases may differ.

7. *Historical-projection.* Historical rate was X; argument projects future rate. Gap: conditions may differ.

### Policy shapes

8. *Evidence-to-policy.* Data shows X; argument recommends policy Y. Gap: policy may not target data well.

9. *Goal-to-strategy.* Goal G is desired; argument proposes strategy S. Gap: other strategies may work better.

10. *Cost-benefit.* Benefits outweigh costs; argument recommends action. Gap: costs or benefits incompletely analyzed.

### Generalization shapes

11. *Sample-to-population.* Sample shows X; argument generalizes. Gap: sample representativeness.

12. *Specific-to-general.* Specific case had X; argument generalizes. Gap: generalizability.

### Comparative shapes

13. *Correlation-to-comparison.* Two groups differ on Y, which correlates with X; argument attributes difference to X.

14. *Analogy-comparison.* Two cases are similar; argument draws comparison. Gap: actual similarity.

### Conditional shapes

15. *If-then-causation.* If A, then B; argument claims A causes B. Gap: necessary vs sufficient.

16. *Necessary-as-sufficient.* X is needed for Y; argument claims X gives Y. Gap: X may be necessary but not sufficient.

17. *Sufficient-as-necessary.* X gives Y; argument claims Y requires X. Gap: other paths to Y.

### Explanatory shapes

18. *Observation-to-explanation.* Observation O exists; argument proposes explanation E. Gap: other explanations.

19. *Correlation-as-explanation.* A and B correlated; argument says one explains the other. Gap: actual causation direction.

### Expert-authority shapes

20. *Appeal-to-authority.* Expert/authority says X; argument concludes X. Gap: expert may be biased or wrong.

21. *Tradition.* Practice has been done; argument says keep doing. Gap: tradition doesn't guarantee correctness.

### Complex shapes

22. *Multi-premise synthesis.* Several premises combine to support a conclusion. Gap: premises may not combine cleanly.

23. *Counterargument-response.* Argument presents and responds to opposing view.

24. *Qualified-conclusion.* Argument hedges its claim with "likely" or "tends to." Gap: may be too weak or too strong.

25. *Policy-implementation-gap.* Data supports policy; implementation may differ from intent.

26. *Aggregate-vs-individual.* Aggregate pattern; argument extends to individuals. Gap: individuals may differ.

27. *Historical-parallel.* Historical case; argument draws parallel. Gap: context differs.

28. *Outcome-reversibility.* Change made; argument claims effect reversibly. Gap: change may be hard to reverse.

29. *Scale-dependent.* What works small may not work large. Gap: scaling issues.

30. *Context-independence.* Something works in one context; argument says works everywhere. Gap: context may matter.

Memorize these. For every CR argument, identify the shape in the first 5 seconds. That recognition tells you the gap and the trap patterns.

## Part 11: CR Speed Drills and Study Protocol

### Daily micro-drill (10 minutes)

Each morning: 3 CR questions, un-timed, with full written analysis. Identify the conclusion, premises, gap, and correct answer's relation to the gap. This builds argument-structure fluency.

### Weekly mid-drill (60 minutes)

Weekly: 20 CR questions, timed at 2:00 per question. Log mistakes by question type and trap pattern. Review every mistake with the "why did I pick that" analysis.

### Monthly mock section

Monthly: a full Verbal mock section. Track pacing and accuracy.

### The error log format for CR

For each missed CR:

- Question type
- Argument shape (from Part 10)
- What you picked
- What was correct
- Trap pattern from the compendium
- Root cause (method error, reading error, pre-thinking skipped, etc.)

### The 6-week improvement plan

Week 1: Question type review. Read this chapter end-to-end. Focus on the three most-common types (Strengthen, Weaken, Assumption).

Week 2: Pre-thinking discipline. Every CR question — write down your prediction before looking at answers.

Week 3: Pattern recognition. For each question, identify the argument shape before identifying the gap.

Week 4: Trap pattern study. Review the compendium. Identify which traps you fall for.

Week 5: Timed practice. Build up from 2:30 per question to 2:00.

Week 6: Mock conditions. Full section pacing.

## Part 12: CR Elite Habits

What separates top 1% from top 10% CR scorers:

### They identify the question type in 3 seconds

The stem signals are memorized. No time lost re-reading stems.

### They pre-think every answer

No exception. Even on easy-looking questions.

### They name the argument shape

Every question, they label the shape. Pattern recognition is the advantage.

### They eliminate systematically

Bottom-up elimination. Remove obvious wrongs first, then compare survivors.

### They catch qualifiers

"Most," "some," "all," "always" are reflexive triggers for careful reading.

### They trust their pre-think

When an answer matches their pre-think, they pick it without second-guessing — unless a stronger one appears.

### They walk away at 2:30

Every type. No grinds.

### They review every error

Trap pattern labeled. Root cause identified. Lesson for next time noted.

### They rebuild weak types

When a question type emerges as weak, they drill it specifically until the pattern recognition is automatic.

### They don't fall for attractive restatements

The correct answer rarely restates the conclusion. Restatements are usually traps.
