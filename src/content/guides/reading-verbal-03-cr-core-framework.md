# Chapter 2.3 — Critical Reasoning Core Framework

## Core idea

This is the *four-step solving process* that all Critical Reasoning problems run on, regardless of question type. The earlier chapters laid the groundwork: the precision-reading mindset, the argument-skeleton vocabulary. This chapter assembles them into a procedure you run on every CR problem. By the end of this chapter, the four steps should compress to about ninety seconds total per problem, run reflexively.

The framework is the spine. *CR Question Types* (Chapter 2.4) is the muscle — the specific right-answer shapes for each task type. The framework here is what runs the muscle.

(5) The ability to *target* the failure — if the failure is at Step 3 (pre-think), you drill pre-thinking, not generic CR drills.

## Why it matters

Three reasons the framework deserves dedicated treatment.

*Without the framework, students hit problems unevenly*. Some problems they happen to read correctly and pre-think well; others they read carelessly and trap themselves. The framework removes the variance — every problem gets the same disciplined treatment. Variance reduction is itself a score lever, since CR contains a high proportion of "tempting but wrong" answers.

*The four steps each have specific failure modes*. Skipping the question-stem read produces task confusion. Skipping the pre-think produces gut-based answer selection. Rushing the elimination produces trap selection. Failing to confidence-check produces accepted-best-of-bad answers. Naming the steps lets you target the failure.

*The framework compresses with practice*. At first, ninety seconds per CR problem feels tight. After three to four weeks of explicit framework practice, the same ninety seconds feels comfortable, and the protocol runs in the background while you think about content. Compression is the goal.

## Mental model

The framework is best held as a *fixed sequence with no skippable step*. Step 1 (question stem) tells you what task you're being asked to perform. Step 2 (read the stimulus) gives you the case. Step 3 (pre-think) gives you the criterion. Step 4 (eliminate) applies the criterion to the candidates. Each step has a specific purpose and produces a specific output that the next step needs as input. Skip any one step and the next is operating without its required input.

The fixed-sequence reminder also covers a second discipline: don't drift mid-process. If you read the question stem as "weaken" but a few sentences into the stimulus you start half-treating it as "assumption" because the argument feels assumption-shaped, your Step 1 output has been overwritten and your Step 4 evaluation will use the wrong criterion. Lock in the task at Step 1 and refuse to drift.

(4) Your Step 1 output gets overwritten silently; Step 4 evaluation uses the wrong criterion; you pick the wrong-task answer. (5) Hard problems are exactly where the temptation to skip discipline is greatest, so the protocol must be locked in.

## GMAT recognition signals

The four steps trigger on different signals.

*Step 1 — Question stem* — triggered as soon as you see the problem. The stem tells you the task: weaken, strengthen, assumption, inference, evaluate, paradox, method, role, boldface. Memorize the vocabulary and recognize the task in two seconds.

*Step 2 — Stimulus* — triggered after the stem. The stem primes the reading. If the stem is "weaken," you read the stimulus *looking for the assumption that connects evidence to conclusion*, because that's what a weakener will undercut. The reading is targeted, not generic.

*Step 3 — Pre-think* — triggered after the stimulus. Don't look at the answer choices yet. Ask: "what would the right answer have to do?" Be specific. "An alternative cause for the correlation" is more useful than "something that weakens." Specificity is what makes pre-thinking discriminative.

*Step 4 — Eliminate* — triggered after the pre-think. Now look at the answer choices. Run each one against the pre-think. Mark IN, OUT, or MAYBE. The right answer is among the INs and MAYBEs.

## Method

The four steps in detail, with timing budgets.

*Step 1 — Read the question stem (3-5 seconds).* Identify the task type. Common stems: "Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the argument?" → weaken. "The argument above depends on which of the following assumptions?" → assumption. "Which of the following is an inference that can be drawn from the passage above?" → inference. "Which of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the argument?" → evaluate. "Which of the following, if true, would best explain the apparent paradox?" → explain. Memorize the standard stems for each task type.

*Step 2 — Read the stimulus (30-45 seconds).* Read once, carefully. Identify the conclusion. Identify the evidence. Note the structure (causal, comparison, prediction, recommendation, explanation). For longer stimuli, write a one-line skeleton summary on scratch.

*Step 3 — Pre-think (10-20 seconds).* Ask "what would the right answer have to do?" Be specific. For weaken: "expose an alternative cause" or "show the comparison is invalid" or "establish reverse causation." For strengthen: "rule out the alternative explanation" or "establish the mechanism." For assumption: "name the bridge between evidence and conclusion." For inference: "identify a claim that *must* follow from the evidence."

*Step 4 — Evaluate the answer choices (30-50 seconds).* Read each choice carefully. Run it against the pre-think. Use IN/OUT/MAYBE marking. After the first pass, you should have one IN or two MAYBEs. If you have one IN, that's likely the answer — but do a quick sanity check by re-reading it against the question stem. If you have two MAYBEs, compare them head to head: which one *more directly* does what the question requires?

*Total budget: 75-120 seconds per CR problem.*

## Common traps

Six framework-execution traps.

*Skipping the question-stem read*. Diving into the stimulus without knowing what task you're being asked. Symptom: you read the argument, finish reading, look at the question stem, and have to re-read the argument with the right filter. Fix: stem first, always.

*Skipping the pre-think*. Going straight from the stimulus to the answer choices. Symptom: you find yourself comparing answer choices to each other instead of to a specific requirement. Fix: even a five-second pre-think beats no pre-think.

*Pre-thinking too vaguely*. "Something that weakens" is not a useful pre-think. "An alternative cause for the correlation" is. The specificity is what makes pre-thinking discriminative. Fix: force yourself to articulate the *form* of the right answer, not just its function.

*Falling for the close-but-wrong answer*. Answer choices A and C both seem plausible. You pick A because it sounds slightly better. The fix is to compare A and C *to the pre-think and the question*, not to each other. The one that more directly does what the question asks is right.

*Selecting an out-of-scope answer*. The answer addresses something the argument didn't claim. It might be true; it might even be relevant in a broader sense; but it doesn't strengthen, weaken, or fill the assumption *of this particular argument*. Fix: re-read the answer and ask "does this engage with the argument's specific structure?"

*Rushing on hard problems*. When a CR problem feels hard, the temptation is to rush — to cut the pre-think, to skim the answer choices. This is exactly backwards. Hard problems need *more* framework, not less. Fix: when a problem feels hard, slow down deliberately and run the full protocol with extra care.

## Original mini-example

A worked example to demonstrate the full four-step protocol on an inference question.

*Stimulus.* Recent surveys of restaurants in the city show that establishments using locally sourced ingredients have, on average, higher customer satisfaction ratings than those relying primarily on imported ingredients. However, restaurants using local ingredients also charge significantly more per meal. The price difference accounts for roughly half of the satisfaction gap.

*Question stem.* Which of the following can be most reasonably inferred from the passage above?

*Step 1 — Task.* Inference. The right answer must *be derivable from* the passage. Not "could be true," not "is suggested by" — *must follow*.

*Step 2 — Stimulus skeleton.* Two facts: (1) local-ingredient restaurants have higher satisfaction *and* charge more; (2) the price difference accounts for half the satisfaction gap. The argument doesn't draw a final conclusion; it presents data.

*Step 3 — Pre-think.* What must follow from these facts? If price accounts for half the gap, then *something other than price* accounts for the other half. The non-price factor is what's left of the satisfaction gap when price is removed. So an inference candidate: "Even controlling for price, restaurants using local ingredients have higher satisfaction." Another candidate: "Local-ingredient restaurants offer something beyond just charging more that contributes to satisfaction."

*Step 4 — Evaluate sample answer choices.*

(A) Restaurants using local ingredients have, on average, lower customer satisfaction than those using imported ingredients when prices are matched. — OUT, contradicts the implication that the gap is only halved by price, not eliminated.

(B) Customer satisfaction is determined entirely by price. — OUT, extreme; the passage explicitly says price accounts for *half* the gap, not all of it.

(C) Even after accounting for price differences, restaurants using local ingredients have higher customer satisfaction than those using imported ingredients. — IN. This follows directly: if price accounts for half, then half remains unexplained by price.

(D) Imported ingredients cause lower customer satisfaction. — OUT, the passage shows correlation, not causation, and "cause" is too strong.

(E) Most customers would prefer to pay more for local ingredients. — OUT, the passage doesn't speak to customer preferences about paying more.

*Answer.* (C).

*Trap to avoid.* The trap answer is (B) — extreme language. Some readers misread "the price difference accounts for half" as "the price difference accounts for all" because the structure is unfamiliar. The discipline of pre-thinking and reading carefully prevents the over-simplification.

*Time spent.* About 80 seconds. Stem read: 3 seconds. Stimulus: 35 seconds. Pre-think: 15 seconds. Evaluation: 30 seconds. Sanity check: 5 seconds.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Step 1 (question stem) priming the read

*Setup.* The same stimulus could anchor either a strengthen, weaken, or assumption question. Argument: "Companies that offer flexible work arrangements report lower employee turnover." A strengthen stem would ask for support; a weaken stem would ask for an alternative cause; an assumption stem would ask for a required bridge.

*Thinking process.* Reading the stimulus *with the question type in mind* changes what you notice. If the stem says "weaken," you read the argument *looking for the assumption* — that's what a weakener will undercut. If the stem says "strengthen," you read *looking for gaps to close*.

*Solution.* The same words on the page produce different mental highlights depending on what task you're being asked to perform.

*Common mistake.* Reading the stimulus generically without question-priming, then having to re-read it through the question-type lens. Costs 30+ extra seconds per problem.

*Takeaway.* Always read the question stem first. The stimulus reads differently depending on what you're looking for.

### Example 2 — Vague pre-think vs. specific pre-think

*Setup.* Argument concludes that a new policy will reduce X. Question asks for a weakener.

*Thinking process.* Vague pre-think: "Something that weakens." Useful only as a starting point. Specific pre-think: "An alternative cause for the observed reduction in X" or "Evidence that the policy mechanism wouldn't actually produce the projected reduction" or "A reason the populations the policy applies to differ in relevant ways from the test population."

*Solution.* Specific pre-thinks let you scan answer choices in seconds and reject those that don't match the form.

*Common mistake.* Settling for the vague pre-think and then comparing answer choices to each other rather than to a specific requirement. Trap answers feel competitive in this comparison; the specific pre-think exposes them as out-of-shape.

*Takeaway.* The specificity of your pre-think determines the precision of your elimination. Force yourself to articulate the *form* of the right answer, not just its function.

### Example 3 — IN/OUT/MAYBE marking

*Setup.* Five answer choices on a CR weakening question. You've pre-thought "alternative cause for the correlation."

*Thinking process.* (A) Mentions a related-but-irrelevant statistic — OUT. (B) Introduces a confounding variable that explains the correlation — IN. (C) Strengthens by providing a mechanism — OUT (wrong direction). (D) True in general but doesn't engage with the argument's structure — OUT. (E) Mentions a possible alternative cause but doesn't establish it — MAYBE.

*Solution.* Compare (B) and (E). (B) directly establishes the alternative; (E) only raises the possibility. (B) is stronger.

*Common mistake.* Marking too many answers as MAYBE because of indecision. The discipline forces a binary: either it matches the pre-think (IN/MAYBE) or it doesn't (OUT). Use OUT aggressively.

*Takeaway.* Aggressive elimination in the first pass leaves a small set to compare carefully. Most CR problems should reduce to one IN or two MAYBEs after the first pass.
