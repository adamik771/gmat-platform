# Chapter 2.6 — RC Question Types

## Core idea

Reading Comprehension has *eleven recurring question types*, each with its own right-answer shape and trap patterns. The skeleton-mapping protocol from Chapter 2.5 is the input; this chapter gives the output — what to do with the skeleton when each question type fires. Some types are answered directly from the skeleton (main idea, primary purpose). Others require returning to a specific paragraph (detail, function, meaning in context). Others require an inference grounded in the skeleton (inference, application, viewpoint attribution). Knowing the type tells you whether to use the skeleton, return to the passage, or both.

### Quick check

2. The skeleton-mapping protocol from Chapter 2.5 is the *input*; this chapter gives what?

4. The right-answer shape varies by type — give an example.

*Answers.* (1) Eleven. (2) The output — what to do with the skeleton when each question type fires. (3) Main idea / primary purpose use the whole skeleton. Detail / function questions require returning to a specific region. (4) A main-idea answer captures the whole passage's structure; a detail answer reproduces a specific fact. (5) Different protocols save time and prevent type-confusion errors — using the wrong protocol on a question type produces wrong answers.

## Why it matters

Three reasons each type deserves its own treatment.

*The right-answer shape varies by type*. A main idea answer captures the whole passage's structure. A detail answer reproduces a specific fact. A function answer names a paragraph's role. Treating these as the same task produces wrong answers. Naming the type forces the right framework.

*The return-to-passage discipline differs by type*. Detail questions always require returning. Main idea questions usually don't. Inference questions require returning to the relevant paragraph and reasoning forward. The chapter teaches when to return.

*The trap patterns are type-specific*. Main idea traps include "too narrow" and "too broad." Detail traps include "passage language but wrong meaning." Inference traps include over-inference. Each type has its own trap inventory.

(3) Main-idea traps include too-narrow and too-broad. Detail traps include passage-language-but-wrong-meaning. (4) Hold rigorously to "must follow," not "could follow" — over-inference is the most common inference-question failure. (5) The right protocol depends on the type; type-recognition is the gate to applying the right protocol.

## Mental model

Think of the eleven types as *eleven different requests for information from the passage's mental map*. Some requests are about the whole map (main idea, primary purpose, organization). Some are about a region (function, meaning in context). Some are about specific landmarks (detail, viewpoint attribution). Some require extending the map beyond what's drawn (inference, application).

Match the request to the right interaction with the map. Don't try to answer a "specific landmark" question by squinting at the whole map. Don't try to answer a "whole map" question by zooming into one region.

(5) Don't try to answer a "specific landmark" question by squinting at the whole map; don't try to answer a "whole map" question by zooming into one region. Type-recognition saves substantial time.

## GMAT recognition signals

The eleven types announce themselves in distinct stems.

*Main idea.* "Which of the following best summarizes the main idea of the passage?" Or: "The passage is primarily concerned with..." Looking for: the central claim or argument that the whole passage builds.

*Primary purpose.* "The primary purpose of the passage is to..." Or: "The author's primary purpose is to..." Looking for: what the author is *trying to do* (argue, describe, compare, evaluate, refute, propose).

*Detail.* "According to the passage, X..." Or: "The passage states that..." Looking for: a specific fact stated in the passage. Always return to verify.

*Inference.* "Which of the following can be most reasonably inferred from the passage?" Or: "The passage suggests that..." Looking for: a claim that *must follow* from the passage but isn't explicitly stated.

*Function.* "The author mentions [X] primarily in order to..." Or: "The third paragraph functions to..." Looking for: the *purpose* of a specific sentence or paragraph within the larger structure.

*Tone.* "The tone of the passage can best be described as..." Looking for: the author's emotional or evaluative stance — neutral, skeptical, enthusiastic, critical, qualified.

*Author attitude.* "The author's attitude toward [X] can best be described as..." Looking for: how the author specifically regards a position or person mentioned in the passage. Note: the author may have different attitudes toward different things in the passage.

*Organization.* "Which of the following best describes the organization of the passage?" Looking for: the *structural pattern* — comparison, chronological, problem-and-solution, claim-and-counter, etc.

*Meaning in context.* "In line X, the word [Y] most nearly means..." Looking for: how a specific word or phrase is used *in this passage's context*, which may differ from its general meaning.

*Viewpoint attribution.* "Which of the following positions would [person/group X] most likely endorse?" Looking for: a claim consistent with the position the passage attributes to that person or group.

*Application.* "If the author's reasoning were applied to [a new scenario], which of the following conclusions would follow?" Looking for: the author's reasoning extended to a parallel case.

## Method

The right-answer shape for each type, with the return-to-passage protocol and the trap subtypes that catch most wrong answers. The major types (main idea, primary purpose, detail, inference, function) account for most of the RC section and get the deepest treatment; the minor types still appear and reward type-specific recognition.

### Main idea

*Right-answer shape.* Captures the whole passage's central claim or argument — what survives if you compress the four paragraphs into a single sentence. The answer must apply to the *entire passage*, not just one paragraph or one detail.

*Pre-think.* "If I had to summarize this passage in one sentence, what would I write? What's the central argument or thesis the whole passage builds toward?"

*Method.* Use the skeleton (from Chapter 2.5). The main idea is what the skeleton's *progression* points to — not paragraph 1's setup, not paragraph 4's specific conclusion, but the synthesis the whole structure produces.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Too narrow.* Captures one paragraph's content but misses the broader scope. Often the answer is true of the passage but only describes a part.
- *Too broad.* Generic enough to fit any passage on the topic; doesn't distinguish what *this* passage specifically argues.
- *Detail-mistaken-for-thesis.* Picks a specific fact from the passage and treats it as the main idea.
- *Reverses the author's stance.* Captures a position the passage discusses but the author *opposes*, mistaking it for the main argument.

### Primary purpose

*Right-answer shape.* A verb-led description of what the author is *doing*. The verb matters as much as the topic. Common purpose verbs: *argue, describe, compare, contrast, evaluate, refute, defend, propose, explain, trace, summarize, criticize, advocate*.

*Pre-think.* "What is the author *trying to do* with this passage? Argue for something? Describe a phenomenon neutrally? Refute a position? Compare two things?"

*Method.* Identify the verb first; the topic is secondary. A passage can be "about" the same topic but have very different purposes (describe vs. argue vs. critique).

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Wrong verb, right topic.* Says "argue" when the passage describes neutrally, or "describe" when the passage argues. Topic match is not enough.
- *Wrong direction.* Says "defend" when the passage critiques, or vice versa.
- *Too specific or too narrow.* "To explain why caffeine improves insulin sensitivity" when the passage is broader.
- *Mismatches the passage's tone.* Says "to enthusiastically endorse" when the passage qualifies; "to criticize" when the passage merely raises questions.

### Detail

*Right-answer shape.* A specific fact or statement from the passage, often paraphrased rather than quoted directly. The answer is *in the passage*; the question is whether you can locate it.

*Pre-think.* "Where in the passage was this discussed?" Use your skeleton to identify which paragraph the question targets, then return to verify.

*Method.* Always return to the passage. Don't trust memory on detail questions — the passage often says something subtler than your recollection captures.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Passage language, wrong meaning.* The answer uses words from the passage but refers to a different fact than the one asked about.
- *Adjacent fact.* The answer paraphrases a fact that's near the relevant sentence but not the right one.
- *Distorted detail.* The fact is in the passage but with a quantifier, qualifier, or scope changed (e.g., "most" becomes "all").
- *Reasonable inference, but not stated.* The answer is something the passage implies but doesn't explicitly state — wrong for a detail question, possibly right for an inference question.
- *Outside-passage fact.* True in the world, but not stated in the passage.

### Inference

*Right-answer shape.* A claim that *must follow* from what the passage says, even if not explicitly stated. The bar is *must*, not *probably* and not *plausibly*.

*Pre-think.* "What additional claim is logically forced by the passage's content? What can I derive in one logical step from what the passage states?"

*Method.* Return to the relevant paragraph. Identify what the passage actually establishes, then ask what *follows necessarily*. Avoid extending reasoning beyond what the passage forces.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Could be true.* The passage doesn't rule out the claim, but doesn't require it. Almost all over-inferences are this type.
- *Adds an unstated step.* Requires combining the passage with reasoning the passage didn't make.
- *Too strong.* The passage establishes a tendency or partial relationship; the answer claims certainty or universality.
- *Outside the passage.* True in the world but not derivable from the text alone.
- *Sufficient/necessary confusion.* "If X then Y" misread as "if Y then X."
- *Wrong scope of inference.* The passage establishes something about a subset; the answer generalizes to the whole.

### Function

*Right-answer shape.* A description of what *role* a specific sentence, paragraph, or phrase plays in the passage's larger structure — why the author included it. Common roles: introduces a topic, presents a viewpoint the author will later refute, supports the main argument, qualifies a claim, provides an example, signals a transition.

*Pre-think.* "Why did the author include this sentence/paragraph? How does removing it change the argument?"

*Method.* Return to the cited portion. Read it in context — what came before it, what comes after it. The role becomes visible in the surrounding structure.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Right content, wrong function.* Accurately describes what the sentence says, but misidentifies why the author included it.
- *Treats counterpoint as endorsement.* Mistakes a view the passage presents (perhaps to refute) for a view the author endorses.
- *Mistakes evidence for conclusion.* The sentence supports a claim; the answer says it *is* a claim.
- *Calls background "argument."* Frames scene-setting as active support of the inference.

### Tone

*Right-answer shape.* The author's *evaluative or emotional stance* toward the topic. Common tones: neutral, skeptical, enthusiastic, critical, qualified, ambivalent, ironic, scholarly, polemical.

*Pre-think.* "What word choices reveal the author's stance? Neutral exposition or qualified endorsement? Cautious skepticism or active critique?"

*Method.* The author's word choices reveal the tone. Watch for evaluative adjectives ("regrettable," "impressive," "puzzling," "questionable") and qualifying phrases ("appears to," "may have," "raises doubts about"). The accumulation of these words across paragraphs sets the tone.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Too extreme.* The passage is mildly critical; the answer says "scornful." The passage is qualified; the answer says "enthusiastic."
- *Wrong direction.* Captures the opposite valence (positive when the passage was negative, or vice versa).
- *Captures one paragraph's tone, not the overall.* The passage shifts tone; the answer freezes on one section's tone.
- *Picks "neutral" when the passage is mildly evaluative.* Passages rarely have purely neutral tones; small word-choice cues signal the author's lean.

### Author attitude

*Right-answer shape.* The author's specific *evaluative position* toward a person, viewpoint, theory, or practice mentioned in the passage. Distinct from tone (which is overall) — attitude targets a *specific object*.

*Pre-think.* "What is the author's specific stance toward the X mentioned in the question? Does the author endorse it, oppose it, or remain neutral about it?"

*Method.* Re-read the passage for *attribution markers* ("according to," "scholars argue," "the author concludes," "as critics have noted"). The marker tells you whether a position is the author's or someone else's. Then identify how the author specifically regards that position.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Confuses presented-view with author's view.* The passage discusses a view; the answer attributes it to the author when the passage actually attributes it to a critic, scholar, or theory.
- *Mistakes the author's tone-of-mention for endorsement.* The author can mention a view neutrally without endorsing it.
- *Too strong.* The author qualifies; the answer says "fully endorses."
- *Too weak.* The author clearly endorses; the answer says "is neutral about."

### Organization

*Right-answer shape.* A description of the passage's *structural pattern* — what each paragraph does and how they connect. Common patterns: claim-then-support, problem-then-solution, comparison-and-contrast, chronological development, refutation-of-opposing-view-then-defense, presentation-of-multiple-views.

*Pre-think.* "What does each paragraph do? What's the relationship between paragraph 1 and paragraph 2? Between paragraph 2 and paragraph 3?"

*Method.* Use the skeleton. The right organization-answer is a high-level description of the skeleton's pattern.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Right content, wrong pattern.* Describes what the passage talks about, not how it's structured.
- *Wrong order.* Describes the pattern but reverses the sequence ("evidence, then claim" vs. "claim, then evidence").
- *Wrong relationship between paragraphs.* Says "the second paragraph supports the first" when actually it qualifies or contradicts the first.
- *Too generic.* Describes a pattern that fits many passages; doesn't capture this passage's specific organization.

### Meaning in context

*Right-answer shape.* How a specific word or phrase is being used *in this passage's particular context*. The meaning may differ from the word's general dictionary definition because of how the passage uses it.

*Pre-think.* "What does this word mean *in this sentence and surrounding context*? What synonym would substitute cleanly?"

*Method.* Return to the cited line. Read the sentence before and the sentence after. The word's contextual meaning emerges from the local structure, not from a general definition.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Dictionary definition that doesn't fit context.* The most common type. The general meaning of the word; doesn't capture the passage's specific use.
- *Right meaning, wrong register.* "Stark" can mean "harsh" or "blunt" or "unadorned" depending on context; the answer picks the wrong shade.
- *Adjacent synonym.* A word with overlapping meaning that doesn't quite substitute cleanly.

### Viewpoint attribution

*Right-answer shape.* A claim consistent with the *specific position* the passage attributes to a designated person, group, or school of thought.

*Pre-think.* "What position does the passage attribute to person X? What would person X consequently believe?"

*Method.* Re-read for what the passage attributes to that party. Test each answer against the attributed view, not against the author's view (which may differ).

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Attributes the author's view to the wrong person.* The most common viewpoint-attribution error.
- *Attributes a view that contradicts the cited position.* The answer is the opposite of what the passage attributes.
- *Generalizes the cited position past what's stated.* The passage attributes a narrow claim; the answer extends it.
- *Mixes attributions.* The answer is consistent with party A's view but the question asked about party B.

### Application

*Right-answer shape.* The passage's reasoning extended to a *new scenario or case*. Apply the same logic the author used to a parallel situation.

*Pre-think.* "What logic did the author apply to reach the conclusion? How would that logic apply to the new scenario the question presents?"

*Method.* Identify the author's reasoning structure (causal claim? comparison? generalization?). Apply that same structure to the new case in the question. The right answer is what the author *would* conclude given the same reasoning move applied to the new scenario.

*Trap subtypes.*
- *Contradicts the author's reasoning.* Applies a different (opposite) logic to the new case.
- *Extends the reasoning past where it would apply.* The author's reasoning depends on a condition that doesn't hold in the new scenario.
- *Right scenario, wrong conclusion.* Applies the right reasoning but reaches a conclusion the reasoning wouldn't actually support.
- *Outside-the-passage logic.* Uses reasoning that's plausible but isn't the reasoning the author actually used.

### Quick check

1. *Main idea* answers are tested against what — the whole passage or any single paragraph?
2. *Primary purpose* answers are *verb-led* — give three example purpose verbs.
3. *Detail* questions require what discipline?
4. *Inference* questions hold to what bar?
5. *Author attitude* differs from *tone* in what way?

*Answers.* (1) Tested against the *whole passage*; the right answer covers all paragraphs, not just one. (2) Argue, describe, compare, contrast, evaluate, refute, defend, propose, explain, trace, summarize, criticize, advocate — pick three. (3) Always return to the passage — don't trust memory; the passage often says something subtler than recollection captures. (4) "Must follow" — not "probably," not "plausibly." Over-inference is the most common failure. (5) Attitude targets the author's specific stance toward a *particular object* (a person, theory, viewpoint); tone is the *overall* evaluative stance across the passage.

## Common traps

Six recurring RC-type traps that cut across multiple types.

*Too narrow.* The answer reflects one paragraph or detail but misses the broader scope the question asked about. Common on main idea and primary purpose. Fix: ask "does this cover the whole passage?"

*Too broad.* The answer is generic enough to fit any passage on the topic; doesn't capture *this* passage's specific structure. Fix: ask "is this distinctive to what this passage actually says?"

*Passage language, wrong meaning.* The answer uses words from the passage but in a different sense. Fix: don't reward an answer for vocabulary match; require *meaning match*.

*Over-inference.* On inference questions, picking an answer that *could* follow rather than *must* follow. Fix: rigorously hold to "must."

*Wrong attribution.* Treating a viewpoint presented in the passage as the author's own view, or vice versa. Fix: re-read for attribution markers.

*Outside-passage knowledge.* Picking an answer that's true in the world but not supported by the passage. Fix: only use what the passage provides.

*Answers.* (1) Any three of: too narrow; too broad; passage language with wrong meaning; over-inference; wrong attribution; outside-passage knowledge. (2) Common on main idea and primary purpose questions — too narrow captures one paragraph; too broad fits any passage on the topic. (3) Don't reward an answer for vocabulary match; require *meaning* match — the right answer often paraphrases. (4) Attribution markers are easy to skip — many passages present multiple positions, only one of which the author endorses. (5) Only use what the passage provides; an answer can be true and still wrong because it isn't supported by the text.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating type recognition on a function question.

*Passage excerpt (paraphrased).* "...The traditional theory accounts for ecosystem A's decline well. However, ecosystem B has shown the same pattern of decline, even though ecosystem B was never subjected to the human pressures that the traditional theory cites as the primary driver. *In ecosystem B, decline began roughly a decade after a related species had been removed from the food chain.* This pattern suggests..."

*Question stem.* "The author's mention of the *italicized sentence* primarily serves to..."

*Step 1 — Task.* Function question. Right-answer shape: a description of the *purpose* of the cited sentence in the larger argument.

*Step 2 — Return to the passage and identify role.* The italicized sentence describes a specific pattern in ecosystem B. The previous sentence said the traditional theory cannot explain B's decline. So the italicized sentence is providing *the alternative explanation* — what *did* trigger B's decline (the removal of a related species). It's the author's beginning of an alternative-causation argument.

*Step 3 — Pre-think.* The function is something like "to introduce evidence supporting an alternative explanation" or "to provide a specific example consistent with a non-traditional cause."

*Step 4 — Evaluate sample answers.*

(A) To support the traditional theory by providing additional evidence for it. — OUT, the sentence undermines the traditional theory by proposing an alternative.

(B) To provide an example of an ecosystem similar to ecosystem A. — OUT, the sentence is about a *difference* between A and B, not similarity.

(C) To introduce evidence consistent with an alternative cause for ecosystem decline. — IN, matches pre-think.

(D) To question whether ecosystem B has actually declined. — OUT, doesn't engage with the sentence's content.

(E) To suggest that food chains are unrelated to predator decline. — OUT, contradicts what the sentence is implying.

*Answer.* (C).

*Trap to avoid.* The trap is (A), which sounds plausible to a reader who didn't track the structural role. The previous paragraph established the traditional theory; a reader who isn't watching transitions might assume any subsequent example supports the theory. The discipline of re-reading for *function in the structure* prevents this.

*Time spent.* About 80 seconds.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Main idea: too narrow vs. right scope

*Setup.* A four-paragraph passage describes an economic theory, summarizes critiques of the theory, presents new research challenging the critiques, and ends by suggesting the theory survives the critiques in a modified form. Main-idea question. Choices include: (A) "The economic theory has been challenged by new research," and (B) "Recent research suggests an economic theory survives its critiques in modified form."

*Thinking process.* (A) captures only paragraph 3's content (the new research challenging critiques). (B) captures the *whole arc* — theory, critiques, new research, modified survival. (B) covers all four paragraphs; (A) covers one.

*Solution.* (B). The main idea must reflect the whole passage's structure.

*Common mistake.* Picking (A) because it accurately describes part of the passage. The trap subtype: too narrow — true of one paragraph, not the whole.

*Takeaway.* Test main-idea answers against the *whole passage*, not against any single paragraph. The right answer covers the arc.

### Example 2 — Function: right content, wrong role

*Setup.* The third paragraph quotes a critic. Function question: "The author's mention of [the critic] in the third paragraph functions primarily to..." Choices include: (A) "support the author's main argument by citing an expert," and (B) "introduce a position the author will subsequently rebut."

*Thinking process.* If paragraph 4 (which follows) presents the author's response to the critic, the critic's role is to set up the author's rebuttal. (A) treats the critic as supporting the author's argument; (B) treats them as a position the author opposes. The structural relationship to paragraph 4 determines which is right.

*Solution.* (B), assuming paragraph 4 rebuts. The critic's *content* is accurate in both choices; only their *function* differs.

*Common mistake.* Picking (A) because the answer accurately describes that the critic was quoted. But "was quoted" doesn't mean "supported the author."

*Takeaway.* Function questions ask *why the author included this*, not *what the author said*. Always check the surrounding paragraphs to see how the cited element relates structurally.

### Example 3 — Inference: must vs. could

*Setup.* Passage states: "Studies have found that workers who use the new productivity software complete tasks 15% faster on average than workers who don't." Inference question. Candidate: "All workers in the company should be required to use the new software."

*Thinking process.* The passage establishes a 15% average speedup. The candidate makes a *policy recommendation* that goes well beyond what the passage establishes. The passage doesn't address whether *all* workers would benefit, whether the speedup is worth implementation costs, or whether requiring software is the right policy lever.

*Solution.* OUT. Could be inferred as the author's view (maybe), but isn't a logical necessity from the stated facts.

*Common mistake.* Picking it because the policy seems to follow from the speedup data. Inference is about logical necessity, not policy advocacy.

*Takeaway.* Hold to "must follow." Policy recommendations and applied conclusions are stronger claims than the passage's data alone supports.
