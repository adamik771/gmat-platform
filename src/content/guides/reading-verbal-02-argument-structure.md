# Chapter 2.2 — Argument Structure

## Core idea

Every Critical Reasoning argument has a *skeleton*: a conclusion, the evidence offered for it, and one or more assumptions that bridge the evidence to the conclusion. The skill in this chapter is mapping the skeleton in thirty seconds and identifying *where the argument is vulnerable* — i.e., where an assumption is implicit, where the evidence is weak, where the conclusion overreaches the evidence. Once you can map the skeleton fast, the question type (strengthen, weaken, assumption, etc.) tells you which part of the skeleton the right answer must address.

This chapter is content. The previous chapter set the mindset; this one builds the formal vocabulary. Without this vocabulary, you can't pre-think effectively, and without pre-thinking, the trap answers catch you.

*Answers.* (1) Conclusion, evidence, and one or more assumptions bridging them. (2) About 30 seconds — fast enough to compress to reflex with practice. (3) Which part of the skeleton the right answer must address (the conclusion, the bridge, the implicit assumption, etc.). (4) The vocabulary (conclusion, evidence, assumption, etc.) is content; mastering it is the precondition for the methods that follow. (5) Pre-thinking — without skeleton-mapping, you can't articulate what shape the right answer should take, and the trap answers catch you.

## Why it matters

Three reasons argument structure deserves its own chapter.

*The skeleton is the right-answer guide*. A strengthening answer must support the conclusion. A weakening answer must undercut the bridge from evidence to conclusion. An assumption answer must fill the gap. An inference must be derivable from the evidence. The shape of the right answer is determined by the structure, and structure-mapping is what makes pre-thinking concrete.

*Most CR arguments use a small number of structural patterns*. Causal claims (X caused Y), comparison claims (X is more/less than Y), predictions (X will happen because Y), recommendations (we should do X because Y), and explanations (X happened because Y). Each pattern has a characteristic vulnerability. Memorize the patterns and the vulnerabilities, and the pre-thinking becomes mechanical.

*Reading for skeleton transfers to RC*. Reading Comprehension passages have skeletons too — paragraph roles, viewpoint shifts, author attitude, support relationships. The skill of structural reading shows up in both subtests and benefits from being trained as a single discipline.

## Mental model

Picture every argument as a *bridge*. The evidence is one side. The conclusion is the other side. The assumptions are the *load-bearing supports* between them. A strong argument has many supports and clear evidence on one side and a clean conclusion on the other. A weak argument has missing supports — gaps where the evidence doesn't fully justify the conclusion.

Your job in reading the argument is to *map the bridge* — identify the evidence, the conclusion, and the supports — and then to identify *where the bridge is weakest*. The question stem tells you what to do at the weak point: shore it up (strengthen), pull it down (weaken), name it explicitly (assumption), or extend a beam to a new conclusion (inference).

### Quick check

*Answers.* (1) The conclusion. (2) The load-bearing supports between evidence and conclusion — the implicit claims that make the inference work. (3) Missing supports — gaps where the evidence doesn't fully justify the conclusion. (4) The weakest point — the question targets specifically that gap or vulnerability. (5) Shore it up — add support that closes a gap or rules out a counter-explanation.

## GMAT recognition signals

Five argument-skeleton elements to identify on every CR problem.

*Conclusion.* The author's main claim. Usually one sentence, often signaled by *therefore*, *thus*, *clearly*, *it follows that*, or by being the most general statement in the argument. Sometimes the conclusion is the first sentence (with the rest as evidence) or the last sentence (with the rest as setup). Identify it explicitly.

*Evidence.* Premises, data, observations, or facts the author offers to support the conclusion. Often signaled by *because*, *since*, *given that*, *based on*, or by being the more specific claims in the argument.

*Assumption.* The unstated link between evidence and conclusion. The argument says "the new policy will reduce traffic, *because* it bans cars from downtown." The assumption is *that downtown drivers won't simply route around or use the policy's exceptions to keep driving*. Strong arguments have minimal hidden assumptions; weak arguments hide several.

*Background information.* Setup that contextualizes the argument but isn't itself evidence or conclusion. Often the first sentence or two. Doesn't directly support or undermine the conclusion; just sets the scene.

*Counterpoint.* Some arguments include an opposing view or a concession ("Although critics argue X, the data shows Y"). The counterpoint is what the author is *rejecting* — knowing this clarifies what the author actually believes.

## Method

A four-step skeleton-mapping protocol.

*Step 1. Identify the conclusion first.* Read the argument once, then ask "what is the author trying to convince me of?" Write a one-line summary on scratch. The conclusion is what survives the question "and what's the point of all that?"

*Step 2. Identify the evidence.* What facts, data, or observations does the author offer? List them on scratch.

*Step 3. Identify the assumption.* What must be true, beyond the evidence, for the conclusion to follow? This is the load-bearing support. Often it's an assumption about *causation* (the correlation is causal, not coincidental), about *generalization* (the sample is representative), or about *no alternative* (no other explanation fits).

*Step 4. Identify the vulnerability.* Given the structure, where is the argument weakest? Causal arguments are vulnerable to alternative causes. Comparison arguments are vulnerable to apples-to-oranges. Generalization arguments are vulnerable to non-representative samples. Prediction arguments are vulnerable to changed conditions. Naming the vulnerability is what makes pre-thinking precise.

## Common traps

Six argument-structure traps.

*Misidentifying the conclusion.* The most expensive structural error. If you think Premise A is the conclusion, every answer-choice evaluation is targeting the wrong thing. Fix: re-read the argument and ask explicitly "what is the author trying to convince me of?" Conclusions are usually the most general claim; premises are usually more specific.

*Confusing background with evidence.* Background sets up the context but doesn't support the conclusion. Evidence does. A "weakening" answer that contradicts background is irrelevant; a weakening answer that contradicts evidence is on target.

*Missing the implicit causal claim.* Many GMAT arguments imply causation without stating it. "Cities with bike lanes have lower obesity rates; therefore, building bike lanes reduces obesity." The implicit claim is causal — bike lanes cause lower obesity — and the right weakening answer would offer an alternative cause (e.g., wealth correlates with both bike lanes and lower obesity).

*Treating an inference as evidence.* The author may state an intermediate conclusion that follows from earlier evidence and supports the main conclusion. Mistaking the intermediate for evidence (or for the main conclusion) muddles the structure.

*Ignoring qualifiers.* "Most," "some," "tends to," "in some cases" matter. An argument that concludes "most" is not weakened by an example of "some not" — the argument allowed for that. Read qualifiers carefully.

*Missing the comparison or analogy structure.* Arguments often compare two things ("the program in city A worked, so it will work in city B"). The vulnerability is in the *comparability* — are A and B similar in the relevant ways? An answer that exposes a relevant difference weakens; an answer about an irrelevant difference doesn't.

## Original mini-example

A worked example demonstrating skeleton-mapping on a causal argument.

*Argument.* An analyst at a regional retail chain compared two stores: Store Alpha, which adopted self-checkout kiosks two years ago, and Store Beta, which retained traditional staffed checkouts. Over those two years, average customer wait times at Store Alpha decreased by 30 percent, while average wait times at Store Beta were essentially unchanged. The analyst concluded that installing self-checkout kiosks causes customer wait times to decrease, and recommended that the chain install kiosks in every other store.

*Skeleton-mapping.*

*Conclusion.* Installing self-checkout kiosks causes wait times to decrease. (Causal claim, plus a chain-wide recommendation.)

*Evidence.* One store with kiosks (Alpha) saw wait times drop 30 percent over two years; one store without (Beta) saw no change.

*Assumption.* The differential change reflects the kiosks' causal effect. There's no third variable that distinguishes the two stores and explains the difference. The direction is "kiosks → faster checkout," not the reverse. The result generalizes from one store to chain-wide.

*Vulnerability.* Causal arguments from a *two-case comparison* are vulnerable to several specific weaknesses: *confounding variables that distinguished the two stores from the start*, *changes coincident with kiosk installation that weren't the kiosks themselves*, *unrepresentative samples* (the one store with kiosks may not represent how other stores would respond), and *implementation differences* (Alpha may have also retrained staff or redesigned floor layout).

*Pre-thinking weakeners.* "Store Alpha is in a neighborhood whose population grew younger and more tech-comfortable during the comparison period" (confound). "Store Alpha simultaneously redesigned its store layout to streamline traffic flow" (alternative cause). "Store Beta serves a clientele that has historically resisted self-service technology" (sample mismatch — the chain's other stores may resemble Beta more than Alpha). "The 30-percent reduction was concentrated in the first three months after kiosk installation, before settling at pre-installation levels" (the effect is not durable).

*Pre-thinking strengtheners.* "Five other stores in the chain that subsequently installed kiosks each saw similar reductions in wait times" (replication, addresses sample concerns). "When kiosks at Store Alpha were temporarily removed for maintenance, wait times rose to roughly Beta's level during that period" (intervention evidence). "An independent factor analysis showed that no other variables distinguishing Alpha and Beta correlate with checkout speed" (rules out confounds).

The shape of the skeleton determines what the right answer must do. Once you've mapped it, evaluating answer choices is mechanical. Without the map, every answer choice looks equally plausible.

## More worked examples

### Example 1 — Identifying the conclusion vs. evidence

*Setup.* "Surveys conducted over the past five years show that consumers under 30 prefer streaming services to cable. Therefore, the cable industry will need to fundamentally restructure its business model to remain viable."

*Thinking process.* The word "therefore" is a conclusion-marker. What follows is the conclusion: the cable industry will need to restructure. What precedes is evidence: survey data on consumer preference.

*Solution.* Conclusion: cable industry will need to restructure. Evidence: surveys show consumer preference for streaming.

*Common mistake.* Treating the survey claim as the main conclusion because it's the more specific factual statement. But the *purpose* of the argument is the restructuring claim; the surveys are evidence for it.

*Takeaway.* Conclusion-markers ("therefore," "thus," "it follows that," "clearly") tell you which sentence is the conclusion. The author's purpose is what comes after them, not before.

### Example 2 — Naming the assumption

*Setup.* "Office workers who take a 15-minute walk during their lunch hour report higher afternoon productivity than those who don't. Companies should require all employees to take lunchtime walks."

*Thinking process.* Conclusion: companies should require lunchtime walks. Evidence: walkers report higher afternoon productivity. Assumption: that *requiring* walks would produce the same productivity benefit as the *voluntary* walks the data describes — i.e., the productivity gain isn't unique to people who already chose to walk.

*Solution.* Required assumption: forced walks produce productivity benefits comparable to voluntarily-chosen walks.

*Common mistake.* Identifying the assumption as "walks improve productivity" — that's just the evidence stated more abstractly. The deeper assumption is the *transferability* from voluntary to mandatory.

*Takeaway.* When an argument generalizes from a self-selected group to a mandated population, the load-bearing assumption is that the effect transfers across that selection difference.

### Example 3 — Identifying the structural vulnerability

*Setup.* "The new product launched in March, and quarterly sales rose 12% over the prior quarter. Therefore, the new product is responsible for the sales increase."

*Thinking process.* Causal argument from temporal correlation. Vulnerabilities: (a) other things may have changed simultaneously (seasonal effect, marketing campaign, economic conditions); (b) the comparison quarter may have been an anomalously low base.

*Solution.* The strongest weakener would establish one of those alternatives — e.g., "Quarterly sales typically rise 10-15% in the spring quarter regardless of new-product launches."

*Common mistake.* Assuming the only weakener is "the new product had no effect at all" — too strong, and not what the GMAT's correct answers look like. Correct weakeners usually establish *alternative explanations*, not denials.

*Takeaway.* Causal arguments from temporal correlation are vulnerable to confounding variables, seasonal effects, and base-rate anomalies. The strongest weakener names one of these explicitly.
